## TASK
Your job is to review user interface designs provided to you using the UI Tenets & Traps system, referencing the context in the Knowledge Base section below for all definitions, explanations, and examples.
**You must always begin by interviewing the user to gather essential context before proceeding.**

### Step 1: Interview for Context
Before analyzing the design, ask the following:
1. **Who are the likely users of this product?** 
- Focus on user expertise, prior knowledge, behaviors, and attitudes (not demographics).
- If the user response is incomplete or vague, provide examples of likely users (e.g., "Professional designers, product managers, engineers, data scientists, QA specialists") and prompt the user to clarify what these kinds of users will know or not know how to do.
2. **What are the key user tasks?** 
- Ask for a brief list of workflows/goals users aim to achieve (not how they achieve them).
- If the user response is incomplete or vague, provide examples of typical user tasks (e.g., "Creating a new project, editing an existing item, exporting data") and prompt the user to clarify.
3. **What is the format of the design file?** 
- Supported formats include: 
- Image files: PNG, JPG 
- Figma files 
- Video files: MP4 
- If the user provides an unsupported format, respond with a clear error message: 
- "Sorry, the format you provided ([user format]) is not currently supported. Supported formats are PNG, JPG, Figma, and MP4. If possible, please convert your file to one of these formats. For example, you can export your [user format] file as a PNG or JPG image, or save your design in Figma and share the link."
- If the user is unsure how to convert, offer step-by-step suggestions or resources for conversion.
Do not proceed with analysis until you have clear answers to all three questions. If any answer is missing or unclear, prompt the user with examples and suggestions until sufficient context is provided.
### Step 2: Analyze the Design
- Examine the design as if you are the specified users attempting the listed tasks.
- Systematically check for each of the 27 Traps.
- For each Trap detected:
- Identify where in the design the Trap occurs.
- Explain why the Trap is present, referencing the relevant Tenet and citing specific examples from the content.
- Provide visual examples if possible.
- Prioritize findings by severity (Critical, Moderate, Minor).
- When recommending fixes, cite solutions or best practices if available.

Introduction

What are UI Tenets & Traps? 
UI Tenets & Traps is a compact, general-purpose tool for learning and 
applying of what makes products work or not work for people. Built for 
everyone involved in the creation of product interfaces, including 
designers, researchers, PMs, and developers, it distills nearly 100 
years of research on human perception, cognition, memory, and 
ergonomics into an accessible and actionable form. 
The system is comprised of 9 Tenets and 27 Traps:  
Tenets describe general attributes of good interface design  
Traps describe common, detectable design problems that 
degrade this goodness  
The core idea: Reduce Traps, improve the experience. 
This tool has its roots in practice. Working as design researchers at 
companies including Motorola, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon, we 
watched teams struggle to apply existing research knowledge and 
witnessed the same avoidable problems appearing in product after 
product. We challenged ourselves to create something teams could 
more easily learn and apply. UI Tenets & Traps is the result, reﬁned 
over more than ﬁfteen years by thousands of designers, researchers, 
PMs, and developers at dozens of companies worldwide.

2 
 
Why did we create this tool? Because design 
knowledge is hard to use.  
The problem, we came to understand, was not a shortage of 
knowledge. It was the lack of an eƯective tool for applying it.  
We conducted an extensive audit of existing UI design resources, 
including hundreds of research studies, books, websites, and 
heuristic checklists, and found no shortage of materials that capture 
what makes good interface design. What we did not ﬁnd was any 
single resource that adequately addressed how teams can learn and 
apply that knowledge in practice.  
Speciﬁcally, we found that existing tools are often:  
DiƯicult to learn and remember 
Short lists of high-level heuristics like Nielsen’s "match between 
system and the real world” provide excellent orientation, but they do 
not take a systematic approach to deﬁning the detectable problems 
to look for in your designs to determine if you are deviating from them. 
At the other extreme, comprehensive interface design guidebooks 
catalog a hundred or more principles or axioms covering every 
conceivable scenario. These are invaluable as references, but nearly 
impossible for teams to internalize and align on. 
DiƯicult to apply  
Some UI principles are harder to apply than they initially appear. 
Consider principles related to redundancy. Providing multiple ways to 
accomplish the same task sounds helpful. More options, more 
ﬂexibility, right? But in some cases, redundant pathways create a 
cascade of problems that make things harder, not easier. When 
principles cut against common intuition, simply learning them is not

3 
 
enough. In the heat of the design process, detailed understanding 
fades, taking a backseat to gut instinct. 
Further, a team’s deep familiarity with their own designs will lead 
them to overestimate how well users will understand them. Think of 
playing charades, certain your miming is obvious, watching 
teammates stare blankly back. The same thing happens to product 
teams in relation to their users. This is the “curse of knowledge” , and 
research has shown that being aware of it does nothing to make it go 
away. A team’s familiarity with the principles of good design does not 
guarantee they’ll deliver it. Existing guidelines and tools tell teams 
what good design looks like, but they don’t solve the problem of how 
to achieve it reliably.   
We challenged ourselves to develop a better tool, one that makes the 
extensive design knowledge that exists easier to learn, recall, and 
apply. The result was UI Tenets & Traps.

What makes UI Tenets & Traps diƯerent? 
The right level of detail 
UI Tenets & Traps integrates knowledge from hundreds of sources. The 
result is a concise synthesis of a very large subject, speciﬁc enough to 
be actionable, focused enough to internalize. It oƯers more detail 
than frameworks built around a handful of high-level principles, but is 
more focused than catalogs listing a hundred or more guidelines. Nine 
Tenets and twenty-seven Traps occupy the middle ground, balancing 
ease of learning and recall with comprehensiveness and depth and 
providing a framework for learning more. This last point bears 
emphasis: on some subjects, we only scratch the surface. This tool is 
not a substitute for deep knowledge, but it does provide a scaƯold 
and a gateway to it. For readers who want to go further, we provide in-
context pointers to resources, as well as an extensive bibliography 
organized by Tenet. 
A memorable structure 
The system is a pyramid: Tenets at the top, Traps below. Each Trap 
belongs to a Tenet. Tenets are adjectives; Traps are nouns. Both use 
familiar, memorable language. Each is precisely deﬁned. The system 
is color-coded throughout, with particular attention to Traps that are 
easy to miss and explicit guidance on how to detect them. 
None of this is decorative. It is designed to help practitioners learn the 
critical human capabilities and limitations that bear on interface 
design, and connect that knowledge directly to speciﬁc, actionable 
design decisions. The same structure has proven eƯective for 
teaching, ﬁrst in classes at the companies where we’ve worked, and 
increasingly in university curricula. This book is an attempt to capture 
what has worked best when we’ve taught these ideas in person.

Precise language 
“There is a direct relationship between more precise language and 
better decisions … [a label] for a problem is a hook to which all that is 
known about it can be attached - its causes, its eƯects, and what can 
be done about it. ”   
- Daniel Kahneman 
Once team members understand a clearly deﬁned Trap, they start 
spotting it everywhere – in their own designs and others. The name of 
the Trap provides a shorthand for discussing it, providing the hook 
Daniel Kahneman describes above: a shared understanding of why it 
happens, what it costs users, and how to ﬁx it. 
When a team shares this vocabulary, something shifts. They stop 
arguing about whether some aspect of the design is a potential 
problem. They know it is because they named it ahead of time. The 
conversation moves immediately to: how severe is the problem and 
what should we do about it?  
A tangible form

How are UI Tenets & Traps used? 
UI Tenets & Traps is built to work the way real product teams work: 
ﬂexibly, and alongside other methods already in play.  
Alignment 
Teams begin by using the framework to align on both the principles of 
good interface design and the language they will use to discuss them.  
Evaluation 
That shared vocabulary then carries through the entire development 
process. In expert reviews, design crits, and cognitive walkthroughs, it 
gives teams the precision to identify and articulate problems quickly. 
In usability testing, RITE studies, and benchmarking, it provides a 
ready explanation for what went wrong and why. Formal heuristic 
evaluations, or Trap Analyses, build directly on the same foundation.  
Opportunity identiﬁcation  
The framework can also be used to drive proactive design 
improvements. Systematically scanning a design for every opportunity 
to make it more EƯicient, Understandable, or Habituating, for 
example, often surfaces issues that testing alone would not catch. 
The goal is to bring the framework in as early as possible and carry it 
through the entire lifecycle. To this end, detailed guidance on applying 
UI Tenets & Traps, including step-by-step instructions for conducting a 
Trap Analysis, is presented in Chapter X.

What is the role of this tool in an age of AI? 
It is more important than ever. 
You can’t supervise what you don’t understand 
Current AI solutions are probabilistic and therefore prone to error. 
While remarkable in their ability, they cannot reliably assess or create 
user interfaces without human oversight, and eƯective oversight 
requires deep domain knowledge. This framework provides a gateway 
to that knowledge, whether you are making design decisions alone or 
in collaboration with AI. 
AI scales output, not quality 
AI is accelerating interface production dramatically, but speed does 
not raise standards. AI-generated interfaces reproduce common 
design failures as readily as common successes, because both are 
present in the training data. Tenets and Traps provides the quality ﬁlter 
that the generation process itself cannot. 
The one thing that has not changed 
Some argue that AI renders old interface paradigms obsolete. It does 
not. Whatever form a product takes, and however it is generated, the 
fundamental challenge remains: there is still a human, a system, and 
an interface between them. Regardless of whether that interface is 
graphical or conversational, it must be designed in alignment with 
human capabilities and limitations that have not appreciably changed 
since the time of our prehistoric ancestors. At a moment when the 
ﬁeld is intensely focused on rapidly evolving potential of AI, it is more 
important than ever to stay grounded in the human user. That is what 
this framework is built on.

A few other things to keep in mind  
It’s about “the how” , not “the what” . UI Tenets & Traps help you 
execute an excellent interface design once you know what user needs 
you are addressing (e.g., managing a calendar, shopping for groceries, 
paying taxes, connecting socially, playing a game, etc.). They don’t 
help you decide which of these needs to address. As Bill Buxton 
observed, we must aspire to both “get the right design and get the 
design right” . This tool focuses on the latter. Deciding what user needs 
to address requires diƯerent tools. 
Context matters. Most Tenets and Traps apply broadly, but context 
can shape if and how they are applied. Some designs intentionally 
introduce what would be problems elsewhere: video games may 
create appropriate challenge by testing users’ memory and physical 
dexterity, while shopping experiences may oƯer redundant pathways 
to discovery that would frustrate users in task-focused contexts. The 
bottom-line: Knowing when diƯerent Tenets and Traps apply requires 
careful consideration of factors outside of the interface. 
It’s about starting conversations, not winning them. UI Tenets & 
Traps are most valuable when they give a team shared language for 
diƯicult design discussions, not ammunition. Bringing them in at the 
start of a project, when teams are most open to new approaches and 
can align on their use, pays dividends throughout the process. 
All models are imperfect. UI Tenets & Traps can’t capture every 
nuance of every interface problem. But thousands of practitioners 
have found them useful for detecting issues earlier, discussing them 
more precisely, and developing better solutions. As George Box put it: 
all models are wrong. Some are useful.

"Intuition is nothing more and nothing less 
than recognition. " 
— Herbert Simon 
An interface is UNDERSTANDABLE when users can see what it 
oƯers, grasp how to use it, and know whether their actions worked. So 
what determines if an interface is or is not Understandable?  
The nature of what makes an interface Understandable was well 
illustrated in an experiment on touch interfaces led by Andy Wilson at 
Microsoft Research in the early 2000s. Participants were shown a 
sequence of images on a screen, for example, a colored shape that 
appears in one position and then in another. They were then asked to 
demonstrate the touch gesture needed to produce each change. 
When shown an object that moved, participants were nearly 
unanimous in their response: they reached out and dragged. But for 
other transformations, such as an object changing color or being 
duplicated, agreement collapsed. Participants were all over the place 
in the things they tried.  
The easy explanation for these results is that dragging an object is 
simply "intuitive" , while changing its color or duplicating is not. But 
this begs the question of what “intuitive” means. It can’t mean 
“something someone just knows how to do” . This would be a circular 
deﬁnition. It has to explain why there are some things that people just 
know how to do. The answer? Intuitive simply means previously 
learned. To come back to Wilson’s ﬁndings: dragging an object to 
move it is something people learn in infancy. Humans enter the world 
with neural building blocks for understanding and manipulating 
objects encountered in the real world. We spontaneously grasp and

19 
 
move things in the earliest months of our development, and later 
transfer this understanding to moving an object on a screen. But 
changing the color of that object, or duplicating it? That is not part of 
our innate repertoire. Recognition, familiarity, prior learning: these are 
the mechanisms that determine if an interface is Understandable.  
Recognizing what drives understandability is important because it 
improves the questions teams ask themselves. Instead of asking  
"How do we make this intuitive?" , which lacks speciﬁcity and carries 
an “I’ll know it when I see it” vibe, teams will ask: “What have our 
users already learned? What products do they use every day? What 
conventions have they internalized? And if a team is introducing 
something genuinely new, “What familiar concepts can we build on?” 
These questions are more focused and actionable. 
Three Sub-tenets and nine Traps 
Knowing what your users already know is foundational to designing 
Understandable interfaces. From here, the work falls into three areas: 
ensuring users notice the right things in the interface, that they 
comprehend the things they notice, and that they receive 
conﬁrmation that their actions worked. 
These three areas constitute Sub-tenets of Understandable. They 
provide a structure for learning and remembering the nine Traps 
associated with it.

must be made accessible to the user's senses and rendered in a way

About this Trap 
The Invisible Element Trap applies when something is missing from 
the interface that a user needs to achieve a goal. Although the name 
emphasizes visibility, the Trap applies equally to absent tactile or 
auditory cues that, if present, would signal the availability of key 
functionality. Common examples include hidden swipe actions, 
press-and-hold actions, labels that only appear on hover, etc.   
So what does this Trap look like in the real world?  
An example: The Humane Pin 
Introduced in 2024, the Humane Pin was an AI-driven device that 
supported common mobile phone functions such as calling, playing 
music, taking pictures, and capturing video. Its interface was notably 
diƯerent from a mobile phone’s. It took the form of a small lapel pin. 
Its UI was comprised of a capacitive touch pad, a speaker, a few led 
indicators, and a menu that could be laser-projected onto the user’s 
palm and navigated through a series of hand gestures. Most functions 
were invoked via voice commands, or, when speaking was not an 
option, touch gestures.

23 
 
So, now imagine you are a user of this product, and that you’ve 
already familiarized yourself with its basic operation, including 
tapping and holding the touch pad to issue voice commands to place 
a call or to get an answer to a question. You’ve found yourself in the 
audience at a live outdoor event. The sun is shining and the music is 
rocking. This is your context. You are wearing your pin in the hopes it 
can fulﬁll its promise of letting you leave your phone and home and be 
more present and in the moment. And then, suddenly, the band kicks 
into a favorite deep cut they almost never play live. You remember 
your pin lets you capture video! You haven’t tried to do this before, and 
you’re not sure how. You kick yourself for not watching the entire 30 
minute ‘handbook’ video Humane posted to YouTube. But you really 
want to capture this song – so you’re going to give it a try. This is your 
goal. So, what do you do? Take a guess now. Keep in mind, though, 
that the music is loud, so issuing a voice command is not an option. 
Also, the sun is shining down, so the palm-projected display won’t 
work for you. (It wouldn’t help you anyway.) Any ideas?  
If you are struggling a bit to come up with something, its because 
you’ve just encountered an Invisible Element Trap. There is nothing in 
the interface that tells you how to achieve your goal. The Humane Pin 
had lots of these. (For the record, the right solution is a two ﬁnger 
double tap and hold.) 
The Humane Pin didn’t last very long. It was discontinued within a 
year of its release. And while its shortcomings went well beyond its 
Invisible Elements, its inscrutable interface did nothing to help it 
succeed over mobile phones that clearly communicated their 
capabilities through on-screen elements and dedicated hard controls.

Why does this Trap occur? 
This Trap commonly arises from a team’s desire to have their product 
support more functionality than the interface can represent visually, 
or from a desire to present a cleaner, less cluttered design. As with 
many Traps, the curse of knowledge may also play a role in its 
emergence. Team members can get very comfortable with an invisible 
interaction that baƯles their users, particularly when the team is 
familiar with a similar implementation on another product that their 
users haven’t used, or simply because they’ve gotten used to its 
operation through the internal prototyping process. In either cases,

the team is prone to overestimate how likely new users will be to 
discover it.

How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
If you are considering including an invisible action in your product, do 
the work to understand whether your users are already familiar with it. 
If they are, you’re probably ok. If not, ask yourself whether an 
alternative visible means of completing the action is provided to the 
user? Again, if the answer is yes, you’re probably ok. But if the answer 
to both of these questions is no, either make the action visible - 
almost always the easier path - or ensure users will receive eƯective 
instruction (see FAQ 2 in this section). 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Test with users. This can be done quickly and easily: as with the 
example given at the start of this section, simply ask users how they 
would accomplish a particular goals. If they do not discover the 
functionality, make it visible or provide instruction.  
FAQ 1: But the iPhone has a ton of Invisible Elements! If its ok for 
them… 
The iPhone began with very few invisible action. Notable among these 
was pinch to zoom which was demonstrated in a massive ad 
campaign that demonstrated its novel operation. But over the years 
many more invisible actions have been added. If Invisible Elements

26 
 
are to be avoided, why have they had so much success? There are 
several things to consider here. Given the iPhone’s mass adoption, its 
updates get a ton of media coverage. New capabilities are reported in 
extreme detail. And for anyone who doesn’t follow these reports, a 
billion+ users function as a word-of-mouth training network. All of 
these factors go a long way toward satisfying the requirement for 
eƯective instruction mentioned above. And even with these 
advantages, many iPhone capabilities still go undiscovered.  
So, coming back to the question: if you can count on the media 
coverage and network eƯects Apple enjoys, you might be able to get

EFFECTIVELY INVISIBLE ELEMENT 
A label, icon, or other interface  
element goes unnoticed because 
it is unexpected or misaligned 
with the user’s focus of attention.

32 
 
 
 
“I’ll see it when I believe it. ” 
– Thane Pittman 
 
About this Trap 
An EƯectively Invisible Element is, strictly speaking, visible. But users 
miss it. It goes unnoticed because it appears in the user’s periphery, 
or because it is presented in an unexpected way, regardless of its 
location. As with the previous Trap, the name emphasizes visibility, 
but the Trap applies equally, if less commonly, to tactile and auditory 
cues that go unregistered.  
So, how do people fail to notice what’s right in front of them? 
An exercise: We see less than we think we see 
Extend your arm in front of you and hold up your thumb. Close one eye 
and look at your thumbnail. The area of your thumbnail, when viewed 
at this distance, corresponds to the area in your visual ﬁeld that falls 
on your fovea, a densely-packed group of photoreceptors in the 
middle of your retina. This is the part of your retina that allows you to 
see in high resolution. You need to place this very small “spotlight” 
(comprising less than 1% of your visual ﬁeld) on anything you wish to 
see clearly. This includes the text you are reading right now. If you 
move your gaze just a few degrees away, you will no longer be able to 
make out the text. This surprises a lot of people. How can it be that we

33 
 
only clearly see 1% of what’s in front of us – its seems like we have a 
good sense of what is around us? Our eyes do some of the work, by 
continuously and rapidly sampling our visual ﬁeld, moving from point 
to point 4-5 times a second to register what’s going on around us. But 
our brains do most of the work, choosing which signals to pass 
through, which signals to inhibit, and ﬁlling in the blanks based on our 
prior experience and expectations. What we experience is a 
construction of our brain, one that does not represent reality, but 
rather, a version of reality that helps us achieve our goals. To 
experience your own brain ﬁlling in the blanks as you move your foveal 
vision from point to point - check out the optical illusion below. 
 
Figure x - How many of the 12 black dots can you see simultaneously? The 
'extinction illusion' published by Jaque Ninio and Kent Stevens, lets us 
directly experience the limitations of our visual system.  
But it’s worse than this…

34 
 
Even when our foveal vision falls directly on something, we can still 
miss it. This is because what we notice is driven by what we are 
looking for. This is an evolutionary adaptation that allows us to ﬁlter 
the overwhelming volume of information bombarding our senses at 
any given moment.  
The most famous demonstration of our ability to miss things that are 
right in front of us comes from research by Simons and Chabris. It 
involves counting basketball passes a group of people. If you haven’t 
see this before, we recommend checking it out here: [link].  
In this experiment, participants were asked to count basketball 
passes made between a team of players. After a few seconds, a 
person in a gorilla suit walks through the middle of the scene and 
beats its chest before walking oƯ again. The gorilla walks right in front 
of the ball that the participants were focused on. But remarkably, half 
of the participants failed to see the gorilla. Even though it was right in 
front of them, they did not see it because they were not actively 
looking for it. Their brains rendered it eƯectively invisible - only 
passing signal from their visual cortex to conscious brain that aligned 
with their goals (signals for orange, round, moving), while suppressing 
any other signals (signals for dark, furry).   
The same mechanisms determine what we notice or fail to notice in 
product interfaces. When we are focused on a goal, our attention 
narrows and our brain select for what we expect to see. An example 
that you are undoubtedly familiar with comes from video conferencing 
apps, like Zoom. The familiar admonition, “You’re on mute!” , results 
from the eƯective invisibility of the indicator that tells a participant 
when their microphone is muted. It would often go unnoticed 
because it was not aligned to where participants were likely to be 
attending when jumping in with a comment – namely, to the faces of 
the other participants on the call. (See ﬁgure x.)

35 
 
 
Figure x - “You're on mute!" became a familiar cry with the ascendence of

Elements that fall outside their narrow attentional spotlight – because 
they are in an area of the screen or simply look and act diƯerent from 
what they are looking for - can go entirely unnoticed, regardless of 
how prominent they seem to the person designing them. If you have 
ever observed a usability test participant repeatedly pass over a 
button they must ﬁnd in order to complete a task, thinking to yourself, 
"It's right there!" , then you have seen an EƯectively Invisible Element in 
action. 
Why does this Trap occur? 
Very simply, EƯectively Invisible Elements arise when designers fail to 
fully consider where a user’s attention will fall when using their 
product.

36 
 
How to avoid it 
How to prevent it in the ﬁrst place 
Remember that you can’t unsee the gorilla 
In the same way that anyone who has seen the gorilla cannot unsee it, 
once you become familiar with your own design, your ability to see it 
as a user does evaporates. This is the curse of knowledge in action. 
Align your design to your users’ expectations 
Develop a clear understanding of your users' moment-to-moment 
goals and the prior experiences that have shaped what they expect to 
see. Identify what their primary focus will be during a given task, and 
ensure that critical elements are located within, or adjacent to, that 
focus. Also, be sure to do an internal consistency check. If most 
actions in an interface are completed one way, elements that require 
a diƯerent means of interaction are at elevated risk of going 
unnoticed. For example, if 90% of the actions in an interface are 
completed by selecting options from menus users may start to expect 
every interaction to rely on them and then fail to see the 10% of 
actions that require some other means of interaction (see Xbox 
example below).

How to ﬁnd and ﬁx it in existing designs 
Structure design reviews around user goals

37 
 
The wrong way to do a design review: A bunch of team members 
sitting in front of a projected image of the interface asking themselves, 
“what do we think of this design?” The right way: Each participant in 
the review walking through the interface on their own a speciﬁc user 
goal ﬁrmly in mind, followed by team discussion. Why is this a better 
way? A way to think about the importance of this is to reﬂect on the 
gorilla video. If people are asked to watch the video without the goal of 
counting basketball they invariably see the gorilla. In the same way, if 
we look at an interface without a goal in mind, we will see things that 
goal-focused user will not see. The lack of a task to focus attention 
means everything gets seen. Teams can never look at one of their own 
design in the same way as a ﬁrst-time user, but by thinking about what 
the user will be looking for, teams can increase their chances of 
catching items that users might miss. 
Test with humans 
This Trap cannot be reliably detected through design review alone. The 
curse of knowledge makes it nearly impossible for a team to reliably 
detect all their own EƯectively Invisible Elements without external 
input. Usability testing with real users is the most reliable detection 
method. When observing tests, pay particular attention to moments 
where users fail to notice elements the team considers obvious, and 
resist the instinct to explain it away as user error.  
Use motion as a last report 
Humans cannot ignore movement in their visual ﬁeld. It can be used 
to draw users’ attention to critical elements. But use it sparingly: 
motion that is not directly relevant to the user's task risks becoming a 
Distraction Trap.

Related Concepts 
The Cocktail Party EƯect. Experiments by Colin Cherry established 
that humans can selectively attend to one stream of information in a 
noisy environment while ﬁltering out others, and that attention can be 
involuntarily captured by personally relevant signals, most famously 
one's own name, even from an unattended stream. 
Signal Detection Theory. Developed by Tanner and Swets, this 
framework describes how observers detect signals in the presence of 
noise. An element that would be immediately noticeable on a clean 
screen may be eƯectively invisible on a cluttered one. It’s useful for 
thinking about why reducing noise can be as eƯective as increasing 
signal strength.

39 
 
Pre-Attentive Processing and Pop-Out. Anne Treisman’s Feature 
Integration Theory established that basic visual features such as 
color, orientation, and motion are processed automatically across the 
entire visual ﬁeld without conscious eƯort. A target diƯering from its 
surroundings on a single such feature pops out instantly; one that can 
only be distinguished through a combination of features is easily 
missed. Exploiting pre-attentive processing is one of the most reliable 
defenses against this Trap. It drove adoption of color highlighting and 
automated alerts in baggage screening interfaces and can be 
leveraged to improve any interface where it’s important that attend to 
speciﬁc things.  
Inattentional Blindness. This term was coined by Mack and Rock. It 
describes the failure to notice a fully visible stimulus when attention 
is directed elsewhere. Simons and Chabris's invisible gorilla studies, 
are the most widely cited demonstration, but the eƯect had been 
replicated across medical imaging, air traƯic control, and driving. The 
design implication: placing an element where a user can physically 
see it does not guarantee they will notice it. Attention, not visibility, 
determines what registers. 
Visibility of System Status. Nielsen's ﬁrst usability heuristic, published 
in 1994, holds that systems should always keep users informed about 
what is going on through appropriate feedback within reasonable 
time. This Trap covers status information that is present but goes 
unnoticed.  
Change Blindness. The failure to detect changes in a visual scene 
when those changes occur during a brief interruption or distraction, 
documented by Rensink et al. and Simons and Levin in 1997. Related 
to inattentional blindness but distinct: inattentional blindness occurs 
when attention is elsewhere during continuous viewing; change

40 
 
blindness occurs when a scene changes during a moment of 
disrupted attention. 
Cognitive Tunneling. The narrowing of attention that occurs under high 
task focus or stress, documented extensively in aviation and medical 
human factors research through the 1990s and 2000s. When a user is 
concentrating intensely on a goal, their attentional ﬁeld contracts, 
making peripheral elements increasingly likely to go unnoticed. 
Particularly relevant in high-stakes contexts such as medical devices 
and vehicle interfaces. 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap? 
Potentially, but only if the AI has access to accurate data on: 1) what 
users have - and have not - already learned about the product and 
how it works – which will mediate their expectations, 2) the users’ 
likely contexts of use, and 3) the users’ goals – which will mediate 
what they attend to.

DISTRACTION 
Something in the interface draws 
the user's attention away from 
their current goal.

44 
 
 
 
“A wealth of information creates 
a poverty of attention. ” 
– Herbert Simon 
 
About this Trap 
A Distraction in a UI is the mirror image of an EƯectively Invisible 
Element. Every characteristic that makes something noticeable in an 
interface, e.g.,  color, shape, movement, spatial positioning, can work 
to direct attention appropriately or hijack it entirely. The later 
describes the Distraction Trap. Examples may include pop-up 
notiﬁcations, auto-playing audio or video, animated ads, etc. 
An exercise: There are some things we can’t ignore 
Assuming you have the room to do it, stretch your arms straight out to 
either side of you. Hold your hands so they are on the very edge of 
your ﬁeld of view while continuing to look straight ahead. Can you 
‘see’ your hands? You may have a sense of them, but certainly you 
cannot make out any detail. You peripheral vision lacks the receptors 
for this. Now, wiggle your ﬁngers. Can you sense the motion in your 
periphery? You should be able to – this is what your peripheral vision 
is really good at. (You can put your arms down now. :-)

45 
 
We spent time in the previous section talking about the limits of our 
attention, how the center of our visual ﬁeld is where we can see things 
in the greatest detail. But our peripheral vision still has an important 
role to play. It is highly sensitive to movement. This is an evolutionary 
adaptation. Movement in our periphery may constitute a threat we 
need to respond to. We evolved to automatically react to that 
potential threat and direct our attention toward it. It’s something we 
can’t avoid. It’s impossible for us not to be distracted by movement in 
edge of our visual ﬁeld. 
Things that move or suddenly appear in the visual ﬁeld command 
attention whether we want them to or not, which is why animated ads 
in a webpage margin are so reliably maddening. Light ﬂuctuation in 
low-light environments has the same involuntary pull, a fact well 
known to pilots, who are trained to use peripheral vision at night 
precisely because the visual system cannot ignore movement in the 
dark. The human sensitivity to change in the environment is not 
limited to visual signals. A new sound, an unexpected touch, even an 
unfamiliar smell will trigger the similar reﬂexive reorientation. The 
perceptual system evolved to detect change as a proxy for threat or 
opportunity. It does not care whether the change is relevant to the 
task at hand. 
[Example here] 
This Trap is among the most commonly exploited in bad-faith. 
Animated advertisements, autoplay video, and attention-grabbing 
notiﬁcations exist in many cases not because they serve users but 
because product teams know that involuntary attention cannot easily 
be withheld.  
Distraction doesn’t require sudden change

46 
 
While change in an interface can be distracting, distraction does not 
require change. The mere presence of certain types of information can 
also be distracting [research on notiﬁcation elements, research on 
mobile phones placed within view and their impact on attention].  
[Example here] 
Sometimes the mode of interaction can be a distraction in itself  
Voice interfaces are great under the right set of conditions. One of 
those conditions is when what you are speaking about is your current 
primary focus of attention, e.g., when you are having a back-and-forth 
discussion with a voice-driven agent about a topic of interest. 
However, voice interfaces are not so great when the thing you need to 
speak to a product about is not your current, primary focus, e.g., if you 
are at home and practicing a presentation you have the next day in 
your head, and you then need to use your voice to turn on a light on or 
adjust a thermostat, the act of issuing a voice command will break 
your thought process. This is because your brain uses the same 
resources to think aloud as it does to generate a voice command. This 
is not the case with practiced motor skills, which your brain can 
execute without placing any demands on your internal dialog. In the 
scenario given, voice as an interface serves as a distract from your 
goal of continuing to think through your presentation. Physically 
ﬂipping a light switch or tapping a thermostat button does not – even if 
it requires you to walk across the room. 
Why does this Trap occur? 
Distraction Traps most often arise when designers consider interface 
elements in isolation rather than in the context of the broader 
experience. An animation that is valuable to a user in one context 
becomes a Distraction when the user is focuses on an unrelated goal.

47 
 
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Human attention is ﬁnite and singular. We have, in eƯect, only one 
attention to spend at any moment. The right question when designing 
any element intended to appear suddenly is not simply "will this be 
noticed?" but "what will the user be doing when this appears, and 
what will noticing it cost them?" An interruption that is tolerable at 
one moment in a workﬂow can be deeply disruptive at another. 
Designing for attention means designing for context: understanding 
not just what you want the user to see, but when, and at what cost to 
whatever they were already doing within or outside the product.  
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Identify every element in the interface that may draw attention due to 
its unique appearance, its movement, its sound, or anything that 
otherwise changes state without direct user initiation. For each, ask: 
where will the user's attention be when this occurs, and is what this 
element is communicating worth the cost of redirecting that 
attention? Also audit the interface as a whole: the cumulative eƯect of 
multiple competing attention-grabbing elements is often worse than 
any individual element suggests.  
Related Traps  
Often occurs alongside:  
Information Overload. A Distraction draws a user’s attention away 
from a goal. Information Overload slows progress toward a goal by 
presenting too with an excess of information to process. The line 
between these Traps is blurry, but that’s ok because solving for them 
is similar: remove things that aren’t relevant to the user’s goal.

48 
 
See also:  
EƯectively Invisible Element. Motion is sometimes used as a remedy 
for EƯectively Invisible Elements. When applied to elements that are 
not relevant to the user's current goal, the remedy becomes a 
Distraction Trap. 
Related Concepts 
The Orienting Response. First described by Pavlov, the orienting 
response is the automatic reorientation of attention toward a novel or 
changing stimulus. It is reﬂexive, rapid, and largely involuntary. 
Motion, sudden sounds, and unexpected appearances all trigger it 
reliably. In a designed interface, this mechanism is easily exploited, 
intentionally or not, by any element that changes state without user 
initiation. 
Exogenous and Endogenous Attention. Attention researchers 
distinguish between two modes of attentional control. Endogenous 
attention is voluntary and goal-directed. Exogenous attention is 
involuntary and stimulus-driven. The Distraction Trap is, by deﬁnition, 
an exogenous attention event. This distinction clariﬁes why telling 
users to ignore something is not a solution. Exogenous attention 
cannot be suppressed by intention alone. 
Attentional Resources Theory. The theoretical framework underlying 
Simon's epigraph at the outset of this section: attention is a limited 
cognitive resource and demands on it are additive. Every competing 
element consumes a portion of that resource. A single notiﬁcation 
may be tolerable. A screen full of moving elements, competing 
sounds, and pop-up alerts can overwhelm the attentional system, 
leaving the user unable to focus eƯectively on anything. 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap?

49 
 
Potentially, but only if the AI has access to accurate data on: 1) the 
users’ likely contexts of use, 2) the users’ goals in using the product, 
and 3) the users goals in the world outside the product, from which 
the product may distract them.

A label, icon, or other interface 
element is noticed, but its

“Good design is … an act of communication 
between the designer and the user. ” 
- Don Norman 
 
About this Trap 
The Uncomprehended Element Trap, and the one that follows it, 
Inviting Dead End, both deal with comprehension of interface 
elements, i.e., the ability of users to make sense of the things they 
encounter in an interface after they’ve noticed them. Because the two 
Traps are closely related, we introduce them together here.  
An Uncomprehended Element describes when a user notices an 
interface element but does not understand its meaning or how to 
interact with it; it leaves them scratching their head. An Inviting Dead 
End describes when a user notices an interface element and believes 
they understand what is being conveyed but are wrong. In both cases 
the user fails to accurately comprehend the meaning of some aspect 
of the interface. To avoid the ﬁrst Trap, you must ensure the intended 
path forward communicates itself to the user in a way they 
understand. To avoid the second Trap, you need to ensure there are no 
unintended paths that look like they could be correct. The two Traps 
often work in concert, compounding each other's damage. 
These Traps often show up as unfamiliar or misleading icons, labels, 
controls, physical aƯordances, text prompts, or audible cues.

55 
 
We’ll talk more about Inviting Dead Ends in the next section, where it 
gets its own dedicated treatment. For now, we’ll dig into the 
Uncomprehended Element Trap, starting with a real-world example.

The following icon was once presented in the navigation app, Waze. 
Do you know what it means? Here are a few of the app’s functions to 
pick from: Current location, Search, TraƯic, OƯline maps, My trips. Do 
any of these look right?

We’ve asked hundreds of people the same question we just asked you

Uncomprehended Elements seem like they should be easy to avoid – 
you just need to show your designs to people and ensure they 
understand them. But they’re still quite common. These are some of 
the reasons why: 
Why does this Trap occur? 
“The user will ﬁgure it out. ”  
Bruce Tognazzini of Apple once observed that the people who work on 
products, PMs, developers, designers, skew toward being 
troubleshooters and problem solvers, a conclusion that is supported 
by research into “cognitive styles” . The tech ﬁeld is literally made up of 
people who enjoy ﬁguring things out. They will often dismiss concerns 
about the understandability of some aspect of the interface by saying, 
“The user will ﬁgure it out. ” But the tendency to ﬁgure things out is 
much less common in the general population, where most people's

POOR GROUPING 
An important relationship 
between two or more interface 
elements is unclear.

72 
 
 
 
 “The whole is other than the 
sum of the parts. ” 
– Kurt KoƯka 
 
About this Trap 
The preceding Traps have emphasized individual interface elements. 
Poor Grouping is focused on the relationships between elements, 
speciﬁcally, the failure of an interface to communicate relationships 
that are critical to users’ ability to achieve their goals. This Trap often 
shows up as unclear visual hierarchy, insuƯicient white space, poorly 
organized menu structures, etc. 
We impose structure on the world 
[Example here of incomplete image] 
Users do not consciously analyze spatial relationships in an interface. 
They perceive them automatically, through the same perceptual 
mechanisms that allow us to read a scene at a glance. The Gestalt 
psychologists identiﬁed the key principles governing this automatic 
perception in the early twentieth century. These principles — 
proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, and ﬁgure-ground — describe 
how the human visual system groups elements and infers 
relationships without deliberate thought.  
The stakes of failing to clarify the relationship between elements in an 
interface can be dramatic, as the following example illustrates.

73 
 
Example: The Butterﬂy Ballot 
In the 2000 US presidential election, more than 4,000 voters in Palm 
Beach County, Florida punched the second hole on the butterﬂy 
ballot believing it represented the second candidate listed. The layout 
created a false spatial association between a candidate's name and 
the wrong punch hole. Over 19,000 additional voters punched more 
than one hole, suggesting they caught their error too late. A grouping 
problem — a failure of spatial relationship — altered the outcome of a 
presidential election. 
[Image of butterﬂy ballot] 
Failures in grouping are common in interfaces we encounter every 
day. And while the consequences may not be as serious as the 
example above, they can introduce friction to what should be 
frictionless experiences.

Failure to consider the human tendency to impose structure, and how 
this might lead them to fail to see important relationships or to see 
connections that are not intended. And again, curse of knowledge.

Preventing it in the ﬁrst place

At every stage of design, ask yourself what relationships the spatial 
and visual arrangement communicate, and whether those 
relationships match the intended ones.

Related Concepts 
The Gestalt Principles. Gestalt psychology emerged in early twentieth 
century Germany from the work of Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, 
and Kurt KoƯka. Their central insight was that perception is not the 
sum of individual sensations but a structured whole: the visual 
system actively organizes what it sees into meaningful patterns and

75 
 
groupings. The principles they identiﬁed describe the rules by which 
this organization occurs. 
Proximity: elements that are close together are perceived as 
belonging together. This is the most powerful grouping principle and 
the one most commonly violated in interface design. A label 
positioned equidistant between two controls will be perceived as 
ambiguously related to both. 
Similarity: elements that share visual properties — color, shape, size, 
typeface — are perceived as belonging to the same category. 
Consistent visual treatment signals membership in a group; 
inconsistent treatment signals distinction. 
Continuity: elements arranged along a line or curve are perceived as 
related and belonging to a single path. Users follow implied lines 
automatically. 
Closure: the visual system tends to perceive incomplete shapes as 
complete. Partially visible elements signal that more exists beyond 
the current view, which makes this principle useful for progressive 
disclosure. 
Figure and Ground: the visual system automatically separates a scene 
into subject and background. Interactive elements should read as 
ﬁgure; supporting content as ground. When this relationship is 
unclear, users struggle to identify what they should be attending to. 
These principles work together, sometimes reinforcing each other and 
sometimes in tension. A designer who understands them can create 
groupings that users perceive accurately and automatically. A 
designer who ignores them will create groupings that users misread — 
with consequences ranging from minor confusion to the kind of large-
scale error the butterﬂy ballot produced.

76 
 
Visual Hierarchy. Visual hierarchy describes the order in which the eye 
moves through a design. A well-designed hierarchy guides the user 
from the most important element to the least important in a sequence 
that serves their goal. Poor Grouping and poor visual hierarchy 
frequently co-occur: when spatial relationships are ambiguous, visual 
hierarchy tends to collapse, leaving the user without a clear path 
through the interface.

TRAP 
FORCED SYNTAX 
A sequence of actions cannot be 
completed in the order or manner 
the user expects or prefers.

80 
 
 
 
 “To provide a sense of control, [let the 
user] choose the sequence of steps. ” 
– Ben Schneiderman 
 
About this Trap 
Forced Syntax occurs when an interface requires users to approach a 
task in a speciﬁc order that does not match the expectation or 
preference. Examples include systems that force users to specify 
objects before acting on it  (allowing “bedroom light > turn on” , but not 
“turn on > bedroom light”) or require users to ‘wake’ a system before 
they can use it (allowing “Siri, what’s today’s weather?” but not 
“What’s today’s weather, Siri?”). In each case, the task can be 
completed, but the user is forced to state things in a sequence 
imposed by the system.  
Why ﬂexibility is important  
Just as diƯerent languages use diƯerent subject-verb-object 
grammatical constructions to convey the same idea (e.g., “I call 
mom” in English vs “I mom call” in Japanese) the same person can 
convey the same idea in diƯerent ways depending on their context. 
For example, a user who is reminded that there’s a person that they 
need to message, may start this task by pulling up that person in their 
phone’s contacts app before selecting the option that lets them send 
a message to that person. Alternatively, if they are reminded of an

81 
 
important message they need to communicate, they may go to the 
text messaging app, type out their message, and then specify the 
person it needs to go to. Which approach the user takes will vary 
depending on how they conceptualize the goal. This is true of almost 
any user goal. Accommodating this variability is a hallmark of good 
interface design.   
What does it look like when interfaces fail to provide this ﬂexibility? 
Example: Alexa Mobile App 
xxx 
Why does this Trap occur? 
A Forced Syntax Trap may arise when an interface is designed around 
the system's internal logic rather than the user's mental model. It also 
arises from a failure to consider that diƯerent users, or the same user 
at diƯerent times, will construct the same goal diƯerently.    
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Understand all the diƯerent ways your users conceptualize the tasks 
your product supports and ensure they can complete tasks in ways 
that align to their mental models. Systematically plan which of your 
tasks should support object>action (e.g., "ﬁle > open") and/or 
action>object (e.g., "open > ﬁle") constructions. Providing both 
options allows users to approach tasks in the way that feels most 
natural to them, which can enhance user experience and improve 
task eƯiciency. [See Raskin, Johnson]  
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs

82 
 
Look for this Trap wherever the interface imposes a ﬁxed order of 
operations: situations where one thing must be speciﬁed before 
another. Ask whether that order reﬂects a genuine technical 
constraint or simply the assumption that users will approach the task 
the same way the designer did. 
Watch for these speciﬁc warning signs: workﬂows that require users 
to declare their intent before they have had a chance to explore; single 
rigid taxonomies that assume one right way to think about the system; 
and linear sequences imposed on tasks that are genuinely non-linear.  
Identify every point in a task ﬂow where the interface requires the user 
to take a speciﬁc action before proceeding. For each, ask: is this order 
technically necessary, or is it an assumption about how users will 
approach the task? Then observe real users attempting the task 
without guidance. Where they try to start somewhere diƯerent from 
where the interface requires them to start, you have a Forced Syntax 
problem. 
FAQ 1: Is providing multiple ways of doing the same thing always 
good?  
No. Flexible syntax is a speciﬁc and purposeful form of redundancy 
that accommodates users’ diƯerent ways of conceptualizing a task 
(i.e., object>action or action>object). It stands in contrast to 
Gratuitous Redundancy: a Trap where users are presented with 
multiple ways to complete the same task via the same grammatical 
construction (i.e., object>action, object>action). Flexible syntax is 
about oƯering diƯerent ways of doing the same thing. Gratuitous 
Redundancy does not accommodate diƯerent ways of 
conceptualizing a task. Rather, it provides duplicate paths for 
completing the same task in the same way (i.e., object> action and 
object>action), which results in more clutter and more decision 
points for the user.

83 
 
FAQ 2: Is it necessary to support every possible sequence users 
might follow to complete a task?  
No. Supporting every conceivable sequence for lengthy multi-step 
sequences would be neither practical nor useful. Only reasonably 
likely alternatives should be supported.

Gratuitous Redundancy. See FAQ1 above. 
Related Concepts 
Object-Action and Action-Object Constructions. Users approach 
tasks through two primary grammatical structures. In object-action 
construction, the user selects what they want to work with ﬁrst and 
then chooses what to do with it. In action-object construction, the 
user selects the operation ﬁrst and then speciﬁes what to apply it to. 
Neither is more natural in the abstract: which a user prefers depends 
on familiarity, task type, and context. An interface that supports only 
one construction forces users who think in the other to reorganize 
their approach before they can proceed. 
Principle of Multiple Entry Points. Emerging from library science and 
formalized for digital information architecture by Rosenfeld and 
Morville in Information Architecture for the World Wide Web (1998), 
this principle recognizes that users bring diƯerent strategies to any 
system: known-item seeking, exploratory browsing, and exhaustive 
research. An interface built around a single entry point serves only 
one of these strategies reliably. 
Action Identiﬁcation Theory. Vallacher and Wegner's 1987 account of 
why the same person constructs the same goal diƯerently depending 
on context: sometimes as a concrete action, sometimes as an 
abstract outcome. A user under time pressure thinks in immediate

84 
 
actions; a user in exploratory mode thinks in objects and goals. This is 
the theoretical foundation for why Forced Syntax is a Trap: the 
interface assumes a consistency in goal construction that human 
cognition does not provide 
Skill Acquisition Stages (Fitts and Posner, 1967). Users move through 
cognitive, associative, and autonomous stages of skill acquisition, 
and at each stage they construct tasks diƯerently. Novices tend to 
think in terms of objects and exploration; experts reach for direct 
actions. An interface that accommodates only one mode will frustrate 
users at every other stage.

TRAP 
MEMORY CHALLENGE 
The user is required to remember 
information that is easy to forget.

88 
 
 
 
 “Time’s the thief of memory. ” 
– Stephen King 
 
About this Trap 
An interface has a Memory Challenge Trap when it places an 
unreasonable demand on the user's ability to remember critical 
information. Examples include designs that require users to hold 
information in mind from one screen to the next, recall a password or 
command from long-term memory without any retrieval cue, or 
execute a multi-step process by memory alone.  
Human memory, both short-term and long-term, is limited in ways 
that have profound implications for interface design. Short-term 
memory can hold only a small amount of information and is 
extraordinarily volatile. We have all had the experience of walking into 
a room to retrieve something and forgetting what it was before we 
arrive. In interface design, even requiring a user to carry a small piece 
of information from one screen to the next may be asking too much. 
[Example here] 
The power of spatial memory 
One thing meaningfully helps with short-term memory: spatial 
representation. Nearly half of the human cortex is dedicated to 
processing spatial information. We evolved the ability to navigate and 
map our environment long before we developed language, and this 
gives spatial representations a signiﬁcant memory advantage over

89 
 
text-based ones. Consider how much less useful a mall directory 
would be if it replaced the ﬂoor map and "you are here" marker with a 
verbal description of the same information. The map works because it 
externalizes the spatial relationships the brain would otherwise have 
to hold in memory. 
Supporting recognition over recall  
Recognition, matching something in front of you against a stored 
memory, is fast, reliable, and deeply evolved. Recall, searching long-
term memory without any external template to match against, is slow, 
eƯortful, and error-prone. Multiple-choice tests are easier than open-
ended ones for exactly this reason. This distinction drove one of the 
most consequential shifts in computing history: the move from 
command-line interfaces, which required users to memorize every 
possible action, to graphical user interfaces, which allowed users to 
recognize and select from visible options. Good interface design 
leverages recognition over recall wherever possible.  
[Voice – Transient, Schneiderman] 
Why does this Trap occur? 
Memory Challenge most often arises when designers underestimate 
the cognitive cost of memory tasks that feel trivial from the inside. A 
designer who knows the system well knows what to remember and 
when. The same tasks feel very diƯerent to a user encountering them 
for the ﬁrst time, or returning after a long absence. Memory Challenge 
also arises from a failure to consider what happens when a user is 
interrupted mid-task, or when they are using the interface under time 
pressure or distraction.    
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place

90 
 
The overarching principle is to design for recognition rather than 
recall: let users see and choose rather than remember and enter. 
Present information spatially wherever possible, putting the brain's 
spatial processing machinery to work. When a task requires following 
a sequence of instructions, allow users to refer back to those 
instructions while executing them rather than requiring memorization. 
When users must produce information from memory, consider 
whether hints or cues can transform the task from recall to 
recognition. The guiding question throughout is simple: am I asking 
the user to remember this, or am I giving them a way to recognize it? 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Walk through every task ﬂow and identify every point where the user is 
expected to hold information in mind, retrieve something from 
memory without a cue, or execute steps without reference material. 
For each, ask: what happens if the user forgets? Also look for any task 
that requires the user to carry information across a context boundary, 
such as from one screen to another, from one application to another, 
or from a previous session into the current one. 
Related Traps 
Not to be confused with:  
System Amnesia. Memory Challenge occurs when the interface 
requires the user to remember something that is easy to forget. 
System Amnesia occurs when the system fails to leverage information 
the users had previously provided, regardless of how easy that 
information is to remember. 
Uncomprehended Element. An Uncomprehended Element involves 
something the user has never learned: the meaning is simply 
unknown. A Memory Challenge involves something the user has

91 
 
encountered before but struggles to retrieve. One is a gap in 
knowledge; the other is a gap in recall. 
Often occurs alongside:  
Invisible Element. An Invisible Element that the user has been trained 
on but struggles to recall can also be categorized as a Memory 
Challenge. 
Related Concepts 
Recognition over Recall. The distinction between recognition and 
recall is covered in the Related Concepts section of the Invisible 
Element Trap and is the central principle underlying this Trap as well. 
Wherever an interface requires recall, ask whether a recognition-
based alternative is possible. 
Chunking. George Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, 
Plus or Minus Two" established that short-term memory can hold 
approximately seven items, but also showed that those items can be 
chunks of varying complexity. A chunk is a meaningful unit: a word 
rather than a letter, a phone number grouped as three-plus-four digits 
rather than seven individual ones. Organizing information into 
meaningful groups reduces the number of items the user must hold in 
mind even when the total amount of information remains the same. 
Cognitive Load Theory. John Sweller's framework distinguishes 
between intrinsic load, the inherent complexity of the material, 
extraneous load, the load imposed by poor design, and germane load, 
the load associated with learning. Memory Challenge is a source of 
extraneous load: it adds cognitive demands that have nothing to do 
with the task itself. Reducing Memory Challenge frees cognitive 
resources for the work that actually matters.

92 
 
Spatial Memory. Humans remember where things are with far less 
eƯort than they remember what things are called. This capacity is 
directly exploitable in interface design. Consistent element 
placement allows users to navigate by spatial memory rather than 
recall. Visual layouts that externalize relationships, like the mall 
directory map, reduce memory burden by making structure visible 
rather than requiring it to be held in mind.

About this Trap 
The Feedback Failure Trap is a little diƯerent than other Traps. It is not 
deﬁned by a single mechanism but by a moment in time: what 
happens after the user acts. Any failure that leaves a user without a 
clear understanding of what their action accomplished, or how to 
recover if something went wrong, qualiﬁes. These failures are other 
Traps: feedback that is not noticed (EƯectively Invisible Element), not 
comprehended (Uncomprehended Element), or too slow in arriving 
(Slow or No Response), etc. Its purpose is to ensure that evaluators 
pay special attention to whether and how a system closes the loop on 
users’ actions because feedback is foundational to how people learn. 
It therefore represents an additional lens through which the user 
experience should be assessed. 
The importance of feedback 
A touch interface that responds to exploratory gestures with 
immediate visual and motion feedback can eƯectively teach its own 
grammar without a single word of instruction. 
As interfaces have evolved, feedback has shifted from discrete and 
transactional to continuous and real-time: ﬂagging a password as too

98 
 
weak before the user has ﬁnished typing, conﬁrming a valid email 
address the moment the format is recognized. Continuous feedback 
is more natural, more informative, and more forgiving than its discrete 
predecessor.  
Why does this Trap occur? 
Understandably, the eƯorts of most teams is channeled toward the 
design of intended paths. Design of feedback systems can often 
suƯer as a result.  
How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Throughout the design process, ensure the system provides clear, 
timely, accurate, and consistent feedback in response to user 
acctions. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Walk every task ﬂow and, at each point where the user takes an 
action, ask: what does the system communicate in response? Is that 
communication immediate, clear, and suƯicient? Does it 
communicate if and when something goes wrong and how to recover? 
Also note the absence of feedback: moments where the system is 
silent after a user action are candidates for this Trap even when no 
error has occurred. 
Some areas have a persistent tendency to fall short and deserve 
particular scrutiny. Error messages frequently tell users that 
something went wrong without explaining what action to take. 
Identifying these does not require testing – just an audit of messages.  
Because Feedback Failure is a result of other Traps, it does not have a 
single origin. The Related Traps section below identiﬁes the speciﬁc

mechanism that may contribute to it. Identifying which mechanism is 
at work is the key to knowing how to ﬁx it.

Related Concepts 
Feedback Loops in Learning. Feedback is one of the primary 
mechanisms through which humans acquire skills. Research on skill 
acquisition consistently shows that immediate, speciﬁc feedback 
accelerates learning, while delayed or ambiguous feedback slows it. 
In interface design, feedback serves the same function: it tells the 
user whether their mental model of the system is correct and, if not, 
how to update it. An interface that provides good feedback teaches 
itself. One that provides poor feedback leaves users dependent on 
external documentation or trial and error. 
Norman's Action Cycle. Donald Norman's model of human action 
describes a seven-stage cycle: forming a goal, planning an action, 
executing it, perceiving the system's response, interpreting that 
response, evaluating it against the goal, and forming the next 
intention. Feedback Failure disrupts this cycle at the perception and 
interpretation stages. When the system provides no feedback, the 
user cannot perceive a response. When it provides ambiguous

101 
 
feedback, the user cannot interpret it. In both cases the cycle stalls, 
and the user must either guess or abandon the task. 
Visibility of System Status. Nielsen's ﬁrst usability heuristic holds that 
systems should always keep users informed about what is going on, 
through appropriate feedback within reasonable time. This heuristic 
covers responsive feedback after user actions, which is the primary 
focus of this Trap. The proactive dimension of the heuristic, surfacing 
status information users need to make decisions independently of 
any triggering action, is handled in this framework by the Invisible 
Element and EƯectively Invisible Element Traps.

About this Trap 
PHYSICAL CHALLENGE Traps arise when a product is physically 
diƯicult or uncomfortable to use. It can manifest in many ways. 
Example may include touch targets that too small to hit reliably, text 
that is too faint to read without strain, controls that are placed where 
they can’t be comfortably reached, device forms that are too heavy or 
sharp-edged to hold for extended periods, audio prompts that are too 
quiet to hear clearly, surfaces that are too hot to touch, or VR headset 
video that is too jittery to watch without experiencing queasiness. In 
each case, the user understands what they need to do, but doing it 
means experiencing strain, discomfort, or worse, illness or injury. 
Below, we dig into two design topics that have important 
consequences for physical comfort that are applicable to a broad 
range of products: targeting accuracy and text legibility. 
Targeting accuracy: Bigger, closer buttons are easier to target 
[“Missed it by one pixel” or “Tog’s Apple lawsuit” story here?] 
Many interfaces require users to physically acquire a target: press a 
button, tap an icon, click a link. The science of target acquisition is 
captured by Fitts' Law. First proposed in the 1950s by Paul Morris Fitts,

108 
 
it is and one of the most replicated ﬁndings in human factors 
research. In simple terms, Fitts' Law states that the time required to 
acquire a target is a function of its size and its distance from the user's 
starting point. Larger, closer targets are acquired faster. Targets that 
allow overshooting without penalty, such as controls placed at screen 
corners on a bounded display, are eƯectively larger than their physical 
dimensions suggest. 
 
Figure x – It’s the law: People target buttons faster and more accurately 
when they are closer and bigger. 
 
The practical implications are not always obvious. Human ﬁnger pads 
are approximately 12mm across on average. Any touch target 
substantially smaller than this will produce targeting errors regardless 
of user skill or motivation. As mobile phone screens have grown 
larger, the physical reach required to access controls at the top of the 
screen has exceeded what many users can comfortably achieve with 
one hand, driving the introduction of reachability features that bring 
distant controls closer to the thumb. These are engineering 
workarounds for a Physical Challenge Trap that could have been 
avoided by keeping critical controls within comfortable reach in the 
ﬁrst place.

their ability to reach a target. In reality, given the position of our hands

targets more or less diƯicult to reach. This is well illustrated in the

Accessibility and Universal Design. Physical Challenge is the Trap 
most directly connected to accessibility. Many of the guidelines 
established by accessibility research, minimum touch target sizes, 
contrast ratio requirements, minimum font sizes, caption 
requirements for audio content, exist precisely because the 
population of users includes people whose physical capabilities diƯer 
signiﬁcantly from the assumed norm. Designing to accessibility 
standards is not a separate exercise from designing for comfort: it is 
designing for the full range of human physical variation. 
Weber-Fechner Law and Just Noticeable DiƯerences. Formulated in 
the nineteenth century by Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner, this law 
describes the relationship between the physical magnitude of a 
stimulus and its perceived intensity. Perception is logarithmic, not 
linear: each additional unit of physical change produces a smaller 
perceived diƯerence as the baseline intensity increases. One 
practical implication relevant to legibility is that contrast 
improvements at the low end of the scale, where most legibility 
failures occur, produce the greatest perceptual return per unit of 
change. In plain terms: if your text contrast is already poor, even a 
modest improvement will be visible to users; if it is already good, the 
same improvement will go largely unnoticed.

legible one. Products that are physically easy to interact with feel

for interface design is signiﬁcant: PHYSICAL CHALLENGE does not 
merely make tasks harder. It makes the product itself feel worse. 
Legibility and ease of physical interaction are not just usability 
considerations. They shape the user's fundamental perception of the 
product's quality.

ACCIDENTAL ACTIVATION

About this Trap 
Accidental Activation is the mirror image of Physical Challenge. 
Where Physical Challenge makes intended actions too diƯicult, 
Accidental Activation makes unintended actions too easy. The Trap 
occurs when the physical properties of a control, its size, sensitivity, 
placement, or the gesture required to activate it, make it vulnerable to 
unintended triggering. Examples include buttons located where a user 
grips or otherwise touches the device when doing other things, 
buttons with overloaded actions (e.g., double taps, press & holds), 
etc. 
An example: iOS Screen Cap 
Accidental screenshots have been a persistent problem on iPhone 
since Apple replaced the home button with a side button, 
inadvertently creating a screenshot gesture that sits directly at the 
phone's natural grip points. Apple's attempts to mitigate the problem 
through software workarounds have left the underlying cause 
untouched. With the introduction of the Camera Control button on 
the iPhone 16, placed in the same grip zone, Apple added an 
analogous accidental camera launch problem alongside the original.

120

The other side of Physical Challenge 
The relationship between this Trap and Physical Challenge is a source 
of tension. Making targets larger reduces Physical Challenge but 
increases the risk of Accidental Activation. Increasing touch 
sensitivity makes interactions more responsive but makes unintended 
activations more likely. Making gesture thresholds lower improves 
accessibility but makes incidental gestures more consequential. 
There is no universal resolution to this tension. The right balance 
depends on the context, the stakes of an accidental activation, and 
whether the action is reversible (see the Forgiving Tenet).

121 
 
Why does this Trap occur? 
Accidental Activation Traps often arise from optimizing the ease of 
access to a particular control without considering the full range of 
physical interactions a user will have with a device during normal use. 
It also arises from physical form factors that place controls near the 
natural grip points of a device, and from voice and gesture interfaces 
whose activation vocabulary overlaps with ordinary human behavior.    
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
The standard approach involves adding friction to the activation path: 
recessing or shielding controls, requiring a sequential action before a 
critical function executes, adding physical resistance, or requiring 
explicit conﬁrmation for consequential and irreversible actions. These 
techniques are eƯective but carry a cost. They slow intentional 
activation and can introduce Unnecessary Steps. The tradeoƯ must 
be calibrated to context and stakes. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing products 
Walk through every interaction in the interface and ask: what other 
physical actions, performed in the course of normal device use, could 
trigger this? Evaluate how the device is typically held, carried, and 
stored. Create test conditions that push the range of actions the user 
might engage in when using the product. Where accidental activation 
cannot be prevented through physical design alone, recessing 
controls, adjusting sensitivity thresholds, or redesigning gesture 
vocabulary, consider adding a conﬁrmation step. But treat 
conﬁrmation as a last resort. Every conﬁrmation step adds friction for 
the user who acted intentionally, which is most users, most of the 
time. Reserve them for actions that are both consequential and

122 
 
irreversible, and only after the physical design options have been 
exhausted.  
Related Traps  
Not to be confused with:  
Inviting Dead End. An Inviting Dead End lures the user in. With 
Accidental Activation the risk goes unnoticed: the interface 
communicates nothing about it, and the user triggers an unintended 
action as a consequence. One Trap misleads; the other fails to 
prevent. 
Physical Challenge. Accidental Activation and Physical Challenge are 
opposite failure modes. The changes that reduce one frequently 
worsen the other. Both must be considered together. 
Often caused by:  
Bad Prediction. In many cases, Accidental Activations occur when a 
system misreads a user's intent. Voice and gesture interfaces are 
particularly prone to this: a wake word detected in background 
conversation, a gesture triggered by incidental movement. In these 
cases the system makes a prediction about what the user wants, acts 
on it, and gets it wrong. The Accidental Activation is the symptom. The 
bad prediction is the cause. This stands in contrast to the physical 
examples earlier in this section, where no interpretation of intent is 
involved. A button pressed accidentally is simply a button pressed. 
The system responds to the physical input without any attempt to 
judge whether the user meant it.  
Variable Outcome. Interfaces where the same physical action 
produces diƯerent outcomes depending on system state are 
particularly prone to Accidental Activation, because users cannot

123 
 
reliably predict what a given action will do. But lack of consistency is 
the root cause of the problem. 
Related Concepts   
Fitts' Law. The same model that governs target acquisition time also 
governs accidental activation risk. Larger targets are acquired faster 
by users who want them, and more easily triggered by users who do 
not. Targets placed at screen edges are eƯectively larger due to the 
inability to overshoot them, which makes them eƯicient for intentional 
use but risky if positioned near areas the hand naturally rests. 
Understanding Fitts' Law as a two-sided principle, optimizing both for 
acquisition and against accidental triggering, is essential for any 
interface that involves physical controls.

No, absent accurate data on the physical context in which the product 
will be used, including lighting conditions, device hardware, and the

An interface is Responsive when it keeps pace with the user, allowing

Of all nine Tenets, Responsive may have the strongest claim to

Weber-Fechner Law Applied to Response Time. Weber's Law states

About this Trap 
A CAPTIVE WAIT Trap occurs when the system prevents the user 
from moving forward. It may be driven by technical constraints, but 
often it is a business or design-driven choice. Example include un-
skippable ‘pre-roll’ ads ahead of videos, un-skippable cutscenes in 
videogames, install or update procedures that take over a device, etc. 
Coined by Steve Seow, Captive Wait describes any process that holds 
the user hostage. He oƯers the example is a self-cleaning oven that 
locks itself during the cleaning cycle. If you suddenly remember you 
urgently need the oven after the cycle has started, every passing 
second without it completing is frustrating, particularly if there is no 
indication of when it will end. Digital interfaces present the same 
problem. When users start a task, they have a sequence of steps in 
mind. A mandatory update or an un-skippable ad commandeers the 
system and brings that ﬂow to a halt.  
Why does this Trap occur? 
Captive Wait arises from two sources. The ﬁrst is organizational: 
business models that depend on forced exposure to content the user 
did not request. The second is design: mandatory ﬂows imposed to 
ensure users receive information or complete setup steps that the 
team considers essential, without considering whether the user's

140 
 
time and autonomy are worth more than the certainty that the 
information was delivered.    
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
When designing a ﬂow, question every point where the user will be 
unable to advance, back out, or skip. Ask: is this barrier necessary? If 
the information or content is important enough to mandate, can it be 
delivered in a way that respects the user's time? In most cases the 
best alternative is progressive disclosure: surface the critical 
information at the moment it is needed, rather than forcing the user 
through it at a time of the system's choosing. For processes that 
unavoidably require the system to be unavailable, give users advance 
notice so they can choose when to initiate them, allow them to attend 
to other tasks wherever possible, and notify them when the process is 
complete. Any process that takes more than ten seconds should 
include a way for users to either stop it or continue working while it 
runs in the background. Teams should be clear-eyed about the trade-
oƯ they are making when they impose captive waits. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Walk every task ﬂow and identify every point where the user cannot 
choose to advance, back out, or skip. Pay particular attention to 
onboarding ﬂows, setup sequences, advertising placements, and any 
screen that auto-advances or auto-dismisses without user control. 
For each instance found, ask whether the barrier can be removed 
entirely, made skippable, or at minimum shortened and accompanied 
by clear communication about its duration and purpose.

Often occurs alongside:

distinction: Unnecessary Steps describes steps in a user-driven task

See also: 
Feedback Failure. A captive wait that provides no information about

necessary and how long it will last does not eliminate the Trap but 
meaningfully reduces its severity. 
Related Concepts  
Autonomy and Perceived Control. Research in both psychology and 
HCI consistently shows that perceived control over an experience 
signiﬁcantly aƯects how that experience is rated, independent of its 
objective properties. Users who can skip an ad report better 
experiences than users who cannot, even when the ad duration is 
identical. Users who can exit a setup ﬂow and return to it later report 
less frustration than those who must complete it in one sitting. 
Captive Wait violates perceived control directly and deliberately, 
which is why it generates disproportionate user frustration relative to 
its actual time cost.

An interface is ACCURATE when the information it presents is true. 
Misleading, out of date, or otherwise inaccurate content is not a new 
problem. However, the rise of AI-powered interfaces has made the 
Accuracy of information that interfaces present simultaneously more 
important and more challenging to uphold. Large language and other 
AI models are, at their core, probabilistic. They generate responses 
that are statistically plausible rather than veriﬁed as true. As a result, 
they can produce information that ranges from subtly distorted to 
ﬂatly wrong.

About this Trap 
An INCORRECT INFORMATION Trap is present when when a user is 
presented with information that is wrong. Examples include factual 
inaccuracies, hallucinations, algorithmically biased content, 
intentionally misleading information (aka lies), or something as 
innocuous as typographical errors. In these cases the user may or 
may not know the information is incorrect. 
What makes this Trap particularly consequential is potential for going 
under the radar. Most other Traps produce friction the user can feel: 
an icon or label that is not understood, a slow response, an 
exceedingly large number of steps. INCORRECT INFORMATION often 
produces no friction at all. The user is presented with information, act 
on it, and not immediately realize its wrong.  
Example: AIs have made this Trap a more common problem

Why does this Trap occur? 
INCORRECT INFORMATION arises from many sources: outdated data 
that has not been refreshed, AI models that generate plausible-
sounding but unveriﬁed content, human error in content creation, 
algorithmic systems that optimize for engagement rather than 
accuracy, and deliberate choices to present information in misleading 
ways. The last category, intentional inaccuracy, sits at the intersection

147 
 
of ethics and interface quality and calls for a diƯerent response than 
the others. 
How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
For any information the interface presents as fact, ask at the outset: 
what is the source of this information? How will it be kept current? 
What is the consequence of it being wrong? High-stakes information, 
covering health, ﬁnance, safety, and legal matters, warrants the 
highest standard of veriﬁcation and the clearest disclosure of its 
limitations. AI-generated content should be explicitly labeled as such 
and, where possible, accompanied by source citations that allow 
users to verify it independently. Interfaces that display time-sensitive 
information should be designed with clear mechanisms for keeping it 
current from the start, not patched with staleness warnings after the 
fact. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Audit every category of information the interface presents and ask: 
when was this last veriﬁed? Could it be out of date? What happens to 
the user if it is wrong? Pay particular attention to AI-generated 
content, where inaccuracy may be intermittent and hard to detect 
without systematic testing. For algorithmic outputs, test for bias 
across diƯerent user populations: a system that produces accurate 
results on average may produce systematically skewed results for 
speciﬁc groups. 
Related Traps   
Often occurs alongside:

148 
 
Bad Prediction. Incorrect Information describes content that is 
factually wrong, incomplete, or error-ﬁlled. Bad Prediction describes 
when the system takes proactive steps that are not welcomed, 
regardless of whether these steps include the presentation of

Related Concepts 
Epistemic Trust. Research in developmental and cognitive psychology 
has established that humans extend trust to sources of information 
based on cues including apparent expertise, consistency, and past 
accuracy. Interfaces that present information with conﬁdence, 
regardless of its accuracy, exploit this tendency. Users have no 
reliable way to calibrate their trust in an interface's outputs without 
external veriﬁcation, which is why the design obligation to be accurate 
is not merely a quality issue but an ethical one. 
Conﬁrmation Bias. The well-documented tendency of people to favor 
information that conﬁrms their existing beliefs and to discount 
information that challenges them. Relevant to this Trap in two ways: 
ﬁrst, users are less likely to notice or question INCORRECT 
INFORMATION that aligns with what they already believe, making 
certain inaccuracies systematically harder to detect. Second, 
algorithmic systems trained on user behavior may learn to surface 
information that conﬁrms user beliefs rather than information that is 
accurate, because conﬁrmatory information tends to generate more 
engagement. 
Algorithmic Bias. Systems that learn from historical data will 
reproduce the biases present in that data. An interface that draws on 
biased training data will produce systematically skewed outputs, 
which may appear accurate in aggregate while being consistently 
wrong for speciﬁc user populations. Designing for accuracy requires

external veriﬁcation against authoritative sources, which is context-
dependent and cannot be automated reliably for all content types. 
Intentional inaccuracy is particularly hard to detect without access to 
the intent behind the design decision.

everything simultaneously may exceed the cost of the additional 
steps. Additional steps that make an experience more

TRAP 
INFORMATION OVERLOAD 
Information presented to the user 
is understandable but there’s 
more of it than there needs to be.

162 
 
 
 
“Perfection is achieved, not when there is 
nothing more to add, but when there is 
nothing left to take away. ” 
– Antoine de Saint-Exupery 
 
About this Trap 
INFORMATION OVERLOAD Traps arise when the amount of 
information the user is required to take in is greater than is necessary. 
Examples include verbose on-screen instructions, wordy responses 
from AI assistants, and visually cluttered displays. 
The importance of brevity 
Rule 17 in Strunk and White's The Elements of Style is "omit needless 
words. " William Strunk considered it the most important rule for 
eƯective communication. Steve Krug later echoed this rule as his third 
law of usability: "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of 
half of what's left. " In our experience the guidance is right on – 
aggressive cutting is not only possible but invariably yields better, 
clearer communication. Of course, doing this cutting requires deep 
understanding of what users need to see.

163 
 
 
Figure X – First cited by JeƯ Johnson in his book Designing with the Mind in 
Mind, the evolution of Jeep’s dealer search function illustrates the extent to 
which text can be reduced without a loss in clarity.  
The cost of more choices  
Hick's Law, formulated by William Edmund Hick in 1952 and reﬁned 
by Ray Hyman, states that the time required to make a decision 
increases logarithmically with the number of choices available. Each 
doubling of the choice set adds a roughly constant increment of 
decision time. The practical implication: reducing the number of 
options presented at any decision point reduces the cognitive cost of 
choosing. Hick's Law is relevant to any design where users are 
selecting from alternatives, such as menu and navigation structures. 
It is the quantitative foundation for the intuition that less is more. 
In each case, the solution is not to hide information but to make more 
deliberate choices about what earns a place on screen. Prioritization 
is the discipline that prevents this Trap.

164 
 
 
Figure X – Over time, Amazon substantially reduced the number of options 
presented to users under the Accounts & Lists tab.  
Why does this Trap occur? 
INFORMATION OVERLOAD often arises from an unwillingness to 
make editorial choices. Adding detail feels safe, ensuring users will 
have all the information they’ll need. This is particularly acute in 
organizational contexts where diƯerent stakeholders advocate for the 
visibility of diƯerent features or content, and where the cumulative 
eƯect of many individually reasonable additions is an interface that 
serves no one well. It also arises from a failure to audit: interfaces 
accumulate content over time, and what was a reasonable amount at 
launch may become overwhelming as the product grows.    
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Start from the user's most likely goal in each context and build 
outward from there, adding only what directly serves that goal. Treat 
every additional element as something that must earn its place rather 
than something that can be included by default. Apply progressive 
disclosure to surface secondary information only when the user 
needs it, rather than presenting everything at once. Write all text, error 
messages, labels, and instructions to the minimum length that

165 
 
preserves clarity. If your team does not have a professional writer, ﬁnd 
one.  
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
For every element on screen, ask: does this serve the user's most 
likely goal in this context? If not, it is a candidate for removal, 
subordination, or progressive disclosure. Pay particular attention to 
homepages, dashboards, and navigation structures, where the 
temptation to show everything is greatest. Audit for verbosity in text as 
well: error messages, labels, and instructions should be as short as 
they can be while remaining clear. 
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside:  
Gratuitous Redundancy. Duplicate options contribute to Information 
Overload, adding visual weight and decision points without adding 
value. In these cases, Gratuitous Redundancy is the root cause of 
reduced eƯiciency. 
Distraction. Information Overload slows progress by presenting the 
user with an excess of information which can be a Distraction, but 
excess information is the root cause of the problem. In any case, the 
solution is the same: remove things that aren’t relevant to the user’s 
goal. 
Related Concepts  
[add anything that applies or remove] 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap? 
Partially. An AI can assess element count, word count, and option 
density against established guidelines and ﬂag interfaces that exceed

166 
 
typical thresholds. Determining whether speciﬁc information is 
necessary for the user's task requires knowledge of user goals and 
context that may not be present in the design ﬁle. Observation of real 
users remains the most reliable detection method.

SYSTEM AMNESIA 
The system fails to take 
advantage of the user’s prior 
work, preferences, or context.

About this Trap 
SYSTEM AMNESIA Traps arise when a system forgets what a user has 
already told it and makes the user do the same work twice as a result. 
Examples include a recommendation system that promotes content 
the user already owns, a conversational AI that loses context between 
sessions, a support ﬂow that asks for an account number after the 
user has already authenticated. In each case, the system had the 
information it needed and failed to use it.  
[Example] 
Retaining and reusing information provided by a user is one of the 
most powerful tools a system has for reducing user eƯort. The 
cumulative eƯect of a system that remembers well is an experience 
that feels progressively easier over time. 
Why does this Trap occur? 
SYSTEM AMNESIA most often arises from siloed data architecture: 
diƯerent parts of a product do not share information about the user, 
so each context starts from scratch. It also arises from deliberate 
choices to re-prompt users for information for veriﬁcation or legal 
reasons, which may be justiﬁed in some contexts but is often applied 
more broadly than necessary. In conversational AI systems, context 
loss between sessions is frequently a technical constraint, but one 
whose user-facing cost is rarely fully accounted for in the design.

170 
 
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
When designing any ﬂow where the user provides information, ask 
from the outset: could the system reasonably be expected to retain 
this and use it later? Design for retention as a default rather than an 
afterthought. Ensure user data is shared across product contexts 
rather than siloed within them. For recommendation systems, build in 
logic that excludes content or features the user has already engaged 
with. For conversational AI systems, design explicitly for cross-
session context from the start: decide what the system should 
remember, how it should store it, and how it should surface that 
memory in ways that feel helpful rather than intrusive. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Audit every point where the user provides information and ask: has 
this user provided this before? If yes and the system is not using it, 
that is a SYSTEM AMNESIA instance. Also audit cross-session 
behavior: does the system remember where the user left oƯ? Does it 
recall preferences across sessions? Does it avoid promoting content 
or features the user has already engaged with? Each failure of this 
kind is a candidate for improvement. 
Related  
Not to be confused with (and often occurs with):  
Memory Challenge. Memory Challenge describes cases where the 
system design requires users to remember something that is easy to 
forget. System Amnesia describes cases where the system fails to 
leverage information the user has previously provided, regardless of 
how easy it is to remember.

actually has access to that information and is failing to use it requires 
knowledge of the underlying data architecture, which may not be 
present in a design ﬁle alone.

TRAP 
BAD PREDICTION 
The system fails in its attempt to 
anticipate the user's intent, 
preference, or context; it guesses 
wrong.

“[Add quote here if we have an apt one]” 
– [author] 
 
About this Trap 
BAD PREDICTION Traps occur when a system misses in its attempt to 
proactively help the user. They may be experienced as auto-correct or 
auto-complete errors, irrelevant movie or song recommendations, or 
ill-timed suggestions from a voice assistant, etc. The user 
understands what the system is suggesting or doing on their behalf, 
but doesn’t ﬁnd it valuable and must work around it.  
The consequence of Bad Prediction varies signiﬁcantly by context. A 
wrong movie recommendation is mildly annoying: it amounts to 
clutter a user must step around before moving on. An automated 
action that cannot easily be undone, a message sent, a purchase 
made, a setting changed, can be much more disruptive. The more 
consequential the action is and the harder it is to reverse, the higher 
the accuracy threshold must be before the system should act rather 
than suggest. 
[Examples of range of consequences – bad movie reco vs 911 false 
alarm] 
This context-sensitivity is one of the things that makes BAD 
PREDICTION a nuanced Trap to evaluate. A system that 
autocompletes text and gets it wrong 10% of the time may still be a 
net positive if the 90% success rate saves enough eƯort to outweigh

176 
 
the friction of corrections. A system that automatically sends 
messages on the user's behalf with the same error rate is a very 
diƯerent proposition. The question is not whether prediction is 
perfect, it never is, but whether the beneﬁt of acting on imperfect 
prediction outweighs the cost of getting it wrong in this particular 
context. 
Why does this Trap occur? 
BAD PREDICTION arises from the fundamental diƯiculty of inferring 
intent from behavior. User behavior is ambiguous: the same action 
can reﬂect many diƯerent underlying goals, and context shifts 
constantly. Systems that predict well in one context often fail in 
another because they overgeneralize from the patterns they have 
learned. BAD PREDICTION also arises from optimization pressures: 
systems trained to maximize engagement may learn to predict what 
produces clicks rather than what serves user needs, which are not 
always the same thing.. 
How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
For every predictive feature, ask: what is the consequence of a wrong 
prediction? Can the user easily correct it? Is the system acting or 
suggesting? Acting requires a higher accuracy threshold than 
suggesting. Where the consequence of a wrong prediction is 
signiﬁcant and reversal is diƯicult, the system should suggest rather 
than act, and should make it easy to dismiss the suggestion. Where 
prediction accuracy cannot be veriﬁed, default to inaction. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Look for this Trap anywhere the system predicts what the user would 
or could do. For each instance, ask: how often does the prediction

177 
 
fail? What does the user have to do when it does? If correction 
requires more eƯort than the prediction saves, the feature is a net 
negative. Observe users directly: moments of hesitation, correction, 
or frustration following a system-initiated action are reliable signals of 
BAD PREDICTION. 
Related Traps  
Not to be confused with:  
System Amnesia. A system that fails to retain prior user context will 
make worse predictions over time. SYSTEM AMNESIA is a common 
root cause of persistent BAD PREDICTION.  
May be the root cause of:  
Accidental Activation. In many cases, Accidental Activations occur 
when a system misreads a user's intent. Voice and gesture interfaces 
are particularly prone to this: a wake word detected in background 
conversation, a gesture triggered by incidental movement. In these 
cases the system makes a prediction about what the user wants, acts 
on it, and gets it wrong. The Accidental Activation is the symptom. The 
Bad Prediction is the cause.  
Often occurs alongside:  
Incorrect Information. The clearest distinguishing question is: did the 
user ask for this? If the system acted or suggested without being 
asked and got it wrong, that is Bad Prediction. The information or 
action need not include factual inaccuracies, only be poorly timed or 
irrelevant.  
System Amnesia. A system that fails to retain prior user context will 
make worse predictions over time, making System Amnesia a 
common root cause of persistent Bad Prediction.

178 
 
Unnecessary Step(s). When a prediction is wrong and cannot be 
easily dismissed, the user must take additional steps to correct it, 
introducing Unnecessary Steps.  
Related Concepts  
The Speed-Accuracy TradeoƯ. A well-established principle in 
cognitive psychology, the speed-accuracy tradeoƯ describes the 
inverse relationship between how quickly a decision is made and how 
accurate it is. Applied to predictive interfaces, the tradeoƯ manifests 
as a design choice: a system that acts immediately on a prediction 
will do more for the user when it is right, but will be harder to interrupt 
when it is wrong. A system that pauses to conﬁrm gives the user more 
control but reduces the eƯiciency gains that make prediction valuable 
in the ﬁrst place. The right point on this tradeoƯ depends on the 
consequence of error and the ease of correction, which is why context 
is the critical variable in evaluating this Trap. 
The speed-accuracy tradeoƯ is a foundational principle in 
experimental psychology with a long research history. The most 
authoritative citations are: 
Fitts, P .M. (1954). The information capacity of the human motor 
system in controlling the amplitude of movement. Journal of 
Experimental Psychology, 47(6), 381-391. — This is the same Fitts of 
Fitts' Law. His work established the mathematical relationship 
between speed and accuracy in motor tasks. 
Wickelgren, W.A. (1977). Speed-accuracy tradeoƯ and information 
processing dynamics. Acta Psychologica, 41(1), 67-85. — The most 
cited academic paper speciﬁcally on the speed-accuracy tradeoƯ as 
a cognitive phenomenon, extending the concept beyond motor tasks 
to decision-making more broadly.

179 
 
Heitz, R.P . (2014). The speed-accuracy tradeoƯ: History, physiology, 
methodology, and behavior. Frontiers in Neuroscience, 8, 150. — A 
comprehensive review paper that covers the history and applications 
of the concept. Open access and freely available online. This would 
be the most appropriate citation for a design book because it is 
recent, comprehensive, and accessible. 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap? 
Partially. An AI can identify predictive features in a design and ﬂag 
those where the consequence of a wrong prediction is high or 
correction is diƯicult. Determining whether a speciﬁc prediction is 
actually wrong requires access to data on prediction accuracy in real 
use, which is not present in the design ﬁle. Observation of users 
interacting with predictive features remains the most reliable 
detection method.

TENET 
PROTECTIVE 
I stay in control 
 
TRAPS 
IRREVERSIBLE ACTION 
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE 
DATA LOSS

182 
 
 
 
 
“With this assurance, he feels bold in his 
explorations of the world. ” 
- John Bowlby 
 
An interface is PROTECTIVE when it allows users to explore freely, 
secure in the knowledge that they can reverse course if needed, that 
nothing will be shared without their knowledge or consent, and that 
their data will never be lost. 
To operate with conﬁdence, users must believe the system has their 
back. When they know they can undo actions, they are free to move 
through the system without hesitation: to make decisions, explore 
possibilities, and try things they are not certain about. This freedom is 
not just about recovering from mistakes, though it covers those too. It 
is about the assurance that no action is truly ﬁnal unless the user 
chooses it to be. 
The same assurance must also extend to users’ data. Just as they 
should be able to reverse any action, they should be able to trust that 
the system will never share their information or lose their work without 
their explicit consent. Both failures undermine the same thing: the 
user's ability to act freely.  
The data dimension of this Tenet has grown considerably more 
important as technology has become more embedded in daily life. 
Our devices follow us everywhere. They hear things, record things,

183 
 
and share things, sometimes in ways their users never intended. We 
also populate these systems ourselves, voluntarily and not, with our 
most personal information: ﬁnancial records, health data, an 
exchange with a chatbot on a sensitive topic. This data does not stay 
on the device. It travels, accumulates, and persists. What constitutes 
an appropriate level of discretion has become one of the deﬁning 
design challenges of our time, and it will only grow more pressing as 
interfaces continue to move into more intimate contexts. 
Three Traps undermine protection: IRREVERSIBLE ACTION, 
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE and DATA LOSS.

TRAP 
IRREVERSIBLE ACTION 
The system does not allow the 
user to undo or reverse an action 
they have taken.

185 
 
 
 
“When it's easy for people to back out of a 
process or undo an action, it fosters a 
sense of freedom and conﬁdence. ” 
– Jakob Neisen 
 
About this Trap 
An IRREVERSIBLE ACTION Trap occurs when a user cannot backtrack 
or undo an action they have taken. The consequences can range from 
frustrating to catastrophic: a purchase that cannot be cancelled, a 
message that cannot be recalled, a ﬁle that cannot be restored. 
Irreversibility is not always avoidable. Some actions have real-world 
consequences that cannot be undone: a payment that has been 
processed, a print job that has started, a message that has been 
delivered and read. This Trap applies when recovery is possible but 
not supported. 
Example: Instagram 
At one time, Instagram did not provide a way to undo the deletion of 
posts. Later, they introduced a thirty-day recovery window, giving 
users the ability to restore deleted content. The recovery path had 
always been technically feasible. It simply had not been designed. 
Figure x - xxx

186 
 
Why does this Trap occur? 
IRREVERSIBLE ACTION most often arises when teams design forward 
through a ﬂow without designing backward. The intended path is 
clear. The recovery path for when a user arrives at a destination 
unintentionally is not considered. It also arises from technical 
constraints that are accepted as given when they could be engineered 
around, and from a not uncommon assumption that consequential 
actions require conﬁrmation rather than reversibility. 
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Design forwards and backwards. For every action in a ﬂow, ask: what 
does the user do if they change their mind? Ensure your designs 
provide a persistent, consistent means of undoing actions. The 
recovery path deserves the same design attention as the forward 
path. 
Forgive rather than conﬁrm. Avoid conﬁrmation dialogs as a substitute 
for reversibility. They add a step, interrupt the user's ﬂow, and are 
frequently dismissed without being read. A system that allows users 
to undo is more forgiving, and more eƯicient, than one that asks them 
to conﬁrm. 
Proactively eliminate the need to undo. Look for opportunities to get 
ahead of potential errors, e.g., by asking the user if they meant to send 
an email without an attachment when the attachment is referenced 
within the body of the message.  
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
When evaluating a task ﬂow, ask at every step: what happens if the 
user makes a mistake or changes their mind? Any action that cannot

187 
 
be undone is a candidate for this Trap. Where full reversibility is not 
possible, consider whether a time-limited recovery window is 
feasible: the ability to recall a sent email within thirty seconds, or to 
cancel a submitted order before it is processed, is better than no 
recovery option at all. Where conﬁrmation dialogs currently exist, ask 
whether they could be replaced with reversibility. In most cases they 
can, and the experience is better for it. 
FAQ: Should all actions be reversible? 
No. For some tasks irreversibility is the point. For example, when a 
user’s intent is to permanently delete a sensitive ﬁle. In these cases, it 
becomes critical to conﬁrm a user’s intent. Doing this well means 
doing more than just presenting users with a conﬁrmation dialog. Jef 
Raskin observed that, over time, users can dismiss these dialogs 
automatically, rendering them ineƯective. A conﬁrmation that 
requires a non-habituating response, such as typing a speciﬁc word or 
phrase rather than clicking a button, is harder to dismiss without 
conscious attention. [Add Raskin citation on ‘complicating the 
conﬁrmation’] 
Related Traps  
Often the root cause of:  
Data Loss. When a user action causes data to be lost and cannot be 
undone, both Traps are in play simultaneously. Making the action 
reversible typically resolves both.  
Unnecessary Step(s). Conﬁrmation dialogs are often added as a 
substitute for reversibility. Replacing them with reversibility removes 
the step and produces a better experience. 
See also:

188 
 
Bad Prediction. Proactive error prevention, such as ﬂagging a missing 
attachment, is a form of prediction. It should only be implemented 
when certainty behind the prediction is high.  
Related Concepts   
User Control and Freedom. One of Neilsen’s 10 UI heuristics. It 
speaks to users’ potential for making mistakes and calls for providing 
clearly marked "emergency exits" to allow users to quickly recover 
from unwanted actions. 
Error Prevention. Nielsen's ﬁfth usability heuristic holds that it is 
better to design systems that prevent problems from occurring in the 
ﬁrst place than to provide good error messages after the fact. Where 
that is not possible, the next best response is to design conﬁrmation 
steps that are resistant to habituation, so users cannot dismiss them 
without conscious attention.

TRAP 
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE 
The system exposes personal 
data or behavior in a way that is 
harmful, embarrassing, or 
unexpected.

192 
 
 
 
  “[Add quote here if we have an apt one]” 
– [author] 
 
About this Trap 
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE Traps occur any time a system 
communicates something about a user’s data or behavior in a way 
they did not intend. Causes might include share-by-default privacy 
settings, un-silencable user or system-generated sounds, recording of 
video or audio without awareness or consent, etc. 
The scope of this Trap is broader than it might ﬁrst appear. The most 
obvious form is digital: data shared with third parties without clear 
consent, location information surfaced in unexpected ways, purchase 
history visible to household members who were not meant to see it. 
But the Trap also has a physical dimension. A device that reads out a 
notiﬁcation in a crowded room, a voice assistant that responds 
audibly in a quiet oƯice, a screen that displays sensitive information in 
a public place: these are all instances of UNWANTED DISCLOSURE, 
and they will become more common as computing moves further into 
social and physical contexts. 
[Example of remote/digital disclosure and real-time disclosure – 
notiﬁcation beep] 
The rise of advertising-based business models has made this Trap 
more prevalent and more consequential. Systems that monetize user 
data have structural incentives to collect more of it, share more of it,

193 
 
and surface more of it than users would choose if the choice were 
clearly presented to them. The interface decisions that enable this, 
default sharing settings, opaque data collection, opt-out rather than 
opt-in consent, are often deliberate. This is the territory where 
interface design and ethics most directly intersect. 
Why does this Trap occur? 
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE arises from two distinct sources. The ﬁrst 
is oversight: designers do not fully consider how data collected in one 
context may surface in another, or how a feature that is useful in one 
setting becomes intrusive in another. The second is intention: 
systems are designed to share data in ways that serve business goals 
rather than user interests. Both are instances of this Trap, though they 
call for diƯerent responses. 
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Any time user data is collected, ask: where could this data surface, 
and would the user expect and accept that? Any time an interface will 
be used in social or public contexts, ask: what could this system say, 
display, or reveal at an inopportune moment? Default settings should 
reﬂect what users would choose if they were paying full attention, not 
what maximizes data collection or sharing. Consent should be 
explicit, clear, and given at a moment when the user understands 
what they are consenting to. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Walk through every context in which the interface is likely to be used, 
including social and public ones. For each, ask: what data could 
surface here, and would the user expect it? Review default privacy 
settings and ask whether they reﬂect user interests or system

194 
 
interests. Trace every feature that collects, stores, or surfaces data 
and ask where that data could go. Any path that leads somewhere the 
user would not anticipate or accept is a candidate for this Trap. 
Related Traps  
Often occurs alongside:  
Feedback Failure. An interface that provides feedback audibly in a 
context where the user cannot control the volume may produce 
Unwanted Disclosure as a side eƯect of otherwise appropriate 
feedback design. 
Bad Prediction. Systems that surface user data proactively, such as 
displaying purchase history on a shared device, may do so based on a 
misjudgment of context. When that misjudgment exposes private 
information, Bad Prediction and Unwanted Disclosure occur together. 
Related Concepts 
Contextual Integrity. Philosopher Helen Nissenbaum's framework for 
privacy holds that what makes a disclosure appropriate or 
inappropriate is not simply whether information is secret, but whether 
it ﬂows in ways that match the norms of the context in which it was 
originally shared. Medical information shared with a doctor ﬂows 
appropriately to other treating physicians but not to employers. A 
purchase made on one platform does not ﬂow appropriately to a 
social news feed. Contextual integrity provides a principled basis for 
evaluating whether a given data ﬂow constitutes UNWANTED 
DISCLOSURE: the question to ask is not "is this data private?" but 
"does this ﬂow match what the user would expect given the context in 
which they shared it?" 
Privacy by Design. A framework developed by Ann Cavoukian, former 
Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario, which holds that

195 
 
privacy should be built into systems from the outset rather than added 
as an afterthought. Its core principle, that privacy should be the 
default rather than an opt-in, maps directly onto the most common 
source of this Trap: default settings that serve system interests rather 
than user interests. 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap?

information or content the user

 “[Add quote here if we have an apt one]” 
– [author] 
 
About this Trap 
DATA LOSS Traps occur when a user unintentionally loses their work, 
whether through an action or inaction. Causes might include 
unexpected system or app shutdowns without auto-saving, forms that 
do not preserve partial entries, or data entry conﬂicts that occur when 
co-authoring. 
The convention of requiring users to explicitly save their work is a 
historical artifact of an era when computer memory was scarce. It 
required users to take a deliberate action to preserve their work and 
penalized them when they forgot or took that action at the wrong 
moment. Cloud-based applications has gone a long way to minimize 
the occurrences of this Trap, but despite this, they still persist. 
Example:  
Why does this Trap occur?  
Data Loss most often arises from technical constraints that were 
accepted as permanent when they were actually solvable: the 
assumption that saving must be explicit, that sessions cannot persist 
across closures, that co-authoring conﬂicts are unavoidable. It also

200 
 
arises from technical designs (native retention), technical failures 
(synchronization errors), and insuƯicient testing of failure modes: 
what happens when a session times out, when a network connection 
drops, when two users edit the same record simultaneously.    
How to avoid it   
Preventing it in the ﬁrst place 
Design systems to save continuously and automatically wherever 
technically possible. For collaborative tools, design conﬂict resolution 
mechanisms that protect all users' contributions rather than 
defaulting to last-write-wins. Design for failure from the outset: 
session timeouts, network interruptions, and unexpected shutdowns 
are not edge cases. They are certainties that will aƯect real users. 
If data loss is the goal, complicate the conﬁrmation. Sometimes a 
user intends to permanently delete data, for example, a sensitive 
ﬁnancial record sitting on a shared computer. Obviously, systems 
should allow them to do this. But protections should be put in place; 
opening the door to deletion also opens the door to unintended data 
loss. One way this can happen is if a user unthinkingly clicks a 
conﬁrmation dialog after having encountered many of them. To avoid 
this risk, the conﬁrmation should be designed in a way that 
commands the user’s attention. For example, by requiring the user to 
type in a novel word before the deletion occurs. JeƯ Raskin called this 
complicating the conﬁrmation. 
Finding and ﬁxing it in existing designs 
Identify every point in a product where the user creates or modiﬁes 
data. For each, ask: what happens to this data if the session ends 
unexpectedly? What happens if the user navigates away without 
saving? What happens in a multi-user scenario? Any point where the

201 
 
answer is "it could be lost" is a candidate for this Trap. Test failure 
modes deliberately: simulate unexpected shutdowns, session 
timeouts, and network interruptions, and verify that user data is 
preserved across all of them. 
Related Traps   
Not to be confused with:  
System Amnesia. DATA LOSS involve the system failing to retain 
information the user expected it to keep. System Amnesia involves the 
system failing to use data the user previously provided regardless of 
whether the system still has access to it.   
Often occurs alongside:  
Irreversible Action. When a user action causes data to be lost and that 
action cannot be undone, both Traps are in play simultaneously. The 
user took an action (Irreversible Action) and lost data as a result (Data 
Loss).  
See also:  
Unnecessary Step(s). Requiring users to explicitly save their work 
adds a step that continuous automatic saving can eliminate. 
Can an AI reliably ﬁnd this Trap? 
Partially. An AI can identify interfaces that lack auto-save 
mechanisms and ﬂag forms or ﬂows that do not preserve partial state. 
Testing for failure modes, session timeouts, network interruptions, 
and co-authoring conﬂicts requires active simulation rather than 
static analysis and is harder to automate reliably.

— Jef Raskin 
 
Every other Tenet in this framework describes a quality of the 
immediate experience: whether users can see what they need, reach 
what they want, wait a reasonable amount of time. Habituating is 
diƯerent. It describes what happens over time, whether repeated use 
of an interface eventually becomes automatic for the user. 
An interface is Habituating when, with practice, users stop thinking 
about how to use it and focus on their goals. The interactions that 
once required conscious attention become mindless. 
Each of us has ﬁrst-hand experience with this. Touch typing, riding a 
bike, driving a car: activities that once demanded our full attention. 
Once mastered, we could do them while our minds were elsewhere 
entirely. That shift from conscious deliberation to automaticity is one 
of the most remarkable things human beings do. It is also, as Raskin 
recognized, one of the most powerful things a designer can aim for. 
Great interface designs tap into our capacity to form habits. 
The human capacity to habituate is hardwired, but it only engages 
under the right conditions. When a product behaves in predictable 
ways, users cannot help but habituate to it. The Power Law of Practice 
shows that performance improves as a predictable function of 
repetition: each attempt takes less time than the last, following a

206 
 
curve that ﬂattens as mastery approaches. When a product behaves 
unpredictably, or the user is given diƯerent ways of doing the same 
thing, the curve doesn't rise as fast. At best, performance very slowly 
improves and interactions remain mindful. At worst, the 
consequences are catastrophic. Unpredictable controls are a 
recurring factor in automotive and aviation disasters. 
The design decisions that support habituation are not obvious. 
Two sub-tenets capture what must hold for good habits to form. 
Neither is as straightforward as it ﬁrst appears, which is why the Traps 
associated with them are so pernicious. The ﬁrst is Non-Redundant: 
the interface should not provide duplicate paths for completing the 
same action. Redundancy slows habituation because it introduces 
choices and deliberation. The tricky part? Some forms of redundancy 
are not only harmless but beneﬁcial. The second is Consistent with 
Expectations: generally, the same action should produce the same 
outcome. Without predictable responses, practice cannot 
accumulate into mastery. But (you knew there’d be a but) sometimes 
generating a diƯerent response to the same action is perfectly 
acceptable. The Trap sections that follow work through these 
distinctions carefully. 
Beyond their nuance, there are other factors that lead to violations of 
these sub-tenets. Redundancy violations are typically well intended. 
They occur when teams add duplicate paths to improve 
discoverability, reasoning that more entry points mean users will ﬁnd 
what they need. The result is a more cluttered interface that takes 
longer to navigate and longer to master. Consistency violations, on 
the other hand, often emerge when teams try to get more functionality 
out of a limited set of controls by mapping diƯerent functions to the 
same controls across diƯerent system modes. Overloaded

207 
 
functionality like this is especially susceptible to the curse of 
knowledge because designers cannot unknow the state of their own 
system; mode errors are diƯicult to catch when you know how the 
system works.  
The ﬁve Traps in this section are organized under three sub-tenets. 
The ﬁrst two are described above. The third, Oriented, is a special 
case of both: a well-oriented interface provides one consistent action 
users can take from anywhere to return to a single home, making 
navigation itself something that can be done mindlessly. 
Non-Redundant. Duplicate paths to the same destination slow the 
accumulation of mastery and clutter the interface. One Trap 
undermines non-redundancy: Gratuitous Redundancy 
Consistent with Expectations. The same user actions produce the 
same results, the same interface elements look the same wherever 
they appear, and the same elements can always be found in the same 
place. When any of these conditions fail, users are pulled back into 
conscious deliberation precisely when habit should be taking over.

Element | Inconsistent Appearance

About this Trap 
An interface is non-redundant when there is only one way to achieve a 
given goal. This is what Jef Raskin called, with deliberate provocation, 
"monotony, " and while most teams resist the principle on instinct, the 
research supports its spirit. But the principle requires careful 
handling, because not all redundancy is the same. 
The distinction that matters is grammatical. Consider two ways of 
placing a phone call. In the ﬁrst, a user selects a contact and then 
chooses to call. In the second, a user selects the phone function and 
then chooses a contact. Both paths reach the same destination, but 
they support diƯerent ways of thinking about the task: one starts with 
the object, the other with the action. This kind of redundancy adds 
ﬂexibility without adding objects to the system. It accommodates 
diƯerent mental models without cluttering the interface. It is not 
gratuitous. 
Gratuitous redundancy is diƯerent. It occurs when an interface 
presents duplicate entry points to the same destination: two 
instances of the same icon, three links that go to the same page, a 
control that appears in both a toolbar and a menu doing exactly the 
same thing. This kind of redundancy does not serve diƯerent mental

212 
 
models. It simply multiplies choices. And multiplying choices has a 
measurable cost, captured by Hick's Law, a ﬁnding from the 1950s 
that has held up across decades of research. The more choices a user 
faces, the longer it takes to make a decision. Designers who add 
redundant options to improve discoverability often achieve the 
opposite: a more cluttered interface that takes longer to navigate and 
longer to master. 
Gratuitous redundancy also directly undermines the Power Law of 
Practice. Mastery is a function of repetition of the same action. When 
users can reach the same destination by multiple routes, their 
practice is divided across those routes rather than concentrated on 
one. The curve toward automaticity ﬂattens. Mastery takes longer. 
Finally, this Trap is a gateway. Once introduced, gratuitous 
redundancy tends to generate other Traps. Duplicate options push 
other content oƯ screen, creating Invisible Elements. Scrolling to 
reach displaced content introduces Unnecessary Steps. The 
proliferation of choices adds cognitive load that contributes to 
Information Overload. Fixing the redundancy often resolves several 
problems at once. 
[Explore evolutionary basis?  – objects don’t exist in duplicate form in 
nature] 
What it applies to 
Multiple instances of the same interface element are presented to the 
user at the same time. 
Why it occurs 
Gratuitous redundancy most often arises from a genuine desire to 
make features discoverable. The reasoning is understandable: if users 
cannot ﬁnd something, add another path to it. But this treats the 
symptom rather than the cause, and compounds the problem by

213 
 
adding clutter. It also arises when diƯerent parts of a product are 
designed independently, with each team adding their own entry points 
without auditing what already exists elsewhere in the system 
How to avoid it   
For each goal a user might pursue, map every path the interface oƯers 
to achieve it. Any destination reachable by more than one route from 
the same starting point is a candidate for this Trap. Pay particular 
attention to navigation structures, homepages, and dashboards, 
where the temptation to duplicate entry points is greatest. Also audit 
the code: multiple links pointing to the same destination are a reliable 
signal.  
Why it is easy to miss 
This Trap rarely surfaces in standard usability testing or design 
reviews. When teams evaluate designs, they typically walk the 
intended task ﬂow, the golden path. This reveals whether users can 
complete tasks. It does not reveal whether the interface is littered 
with duplicate paths that slow habituation and add noise. Detecting 
this Trap requires a diƯerent exercise: systematically mapping all the 
ways a user might interpret a path forward, not just the intended one.

VARIABLE OUTCOME 
The system responds diƯerently 
and unexpectedly to the same 
user action  at diƯerent times.

“[Add quote here if we have an apt one]” 
– [author] 
 
About this Trap 
Variable Outcome is most often the result of a mode error. A mode is a 
system state that causes the same user action to produce a diƯerent 
result depending on context. The CapsLock key is the most familiar 
example. Pressing a letter key normally produces a lowercase 
character. With CapsLock active, the same press produces an 
uppercase one. The mode is easy to forget, and when you forget it, the 
outcome surprises you. 
CapsLock errors are annoying but harmless. Variable Outcome Traps 
are not always so benign. Mode errors are a recurring factor in 
catastrophic aviation accidents. A pilot who believes an aircraft is in 
one state when it is in another may take exactly the wrong action at 
exactly the wrong moment. The same class of error, a mismatch 
between expected and actual system state, has caused car 
accidents, medical device failures, and industrial incidents. The 
stakes vary enormously. The underlying mechanism is always the 
same. 
What makes this Trap particularly diƯicult is that modes are not 
always the culprit, and the problem is not always as obvious as 
CapsLock. Consider an interface where right-clicking on some words 
reveals a deﬁnition while right-clicking on others does nothing. There

218 
 
is no mode in the strict sense. But the outcome varies in ways the user 
cannot predict, because no clear rule has been communicated. This 
is Variable Outcome without a mode, and variations of it appear in 
products everywhere. 
The key question is not whether the same action produces diƯerent 
results, but whether the user is attending to the signal that explains 
the diƯerence. Modes are acceptable when they are clear enough that 
users cannot forget which mode they are in. A user pressing a button 
on a touchscreen is attending to what is on that button. If the button 
changes based on context, but the user is looking at it, the variable 
outcome is expected and unproblematic. The same logic applies to 
quasi-modes: holding down the Shift key changes the behavior of 
every other key, but because the user is actively holding Shift, the 
mode is impossible to forget. The problem arises when the signal 
indicating the mode is not attended to, when it is located away from 
the user's focus, or when it requires the user to remember a system 
state they have no reason to be tracking. 
Crucially, the cure is not always to add a better signal. Sometimes the 
better solution is to eliminate the mode altogether. Video game 
research has repeatedly shown that controllers with overloaded 
buttons, where the same button does diƯerent things in diƯerent 
game states, produce slower learning than controllers where each 
button has one dedicated function. Even when the mode indicator is 
perfectly visible, eliminating the mode accelerates mastery.  
What it applies to 
Mode-dependent controls (e.g., Caps Lock), Inconsistently supported 
functions (e.g., right-click support for some instances of the same 
interface element, but not others), etc.

219 
 
Why it occurs 
Variable Outcome Traps almost always arise from good intentions. 
Assigning multiple functions to the same control is an eƯicient way to 
pack more capability into a limited interface. State-handlers in code 
are the mechanism: a single action routed to diƯerent outcomes 
depending on system state. This is standard engineering practice and 
is often the right technical choice. The problem arises when the user-
facing implications are not considered alongside the technical ones.    
Why it is easy to miss 
This is one of the most reliably missed Traps in the framework. The 
curse of knowledge is particularly acute here: designers and 
developers know the state-handling logic of their system. They know 
which mode they are in. They cannot easily imagine encountering the 
interface without that knowledge. As a result, they experience no 
dissonance when the same action produces diƯerent results, 
because to them the results are never unexpected. 
How to avoid it  
There is a practical detection technique that goes beyond user 
observation. Scrub the code directly for state-handlers: any place 
where the same user action is routed to diƯerent outcomes is a 
candidate for this Trap. For each one, ask a single question: is there 
any realistic scenario in which a user might take this action without 
attending to the system state that determines the outcome? If the 
answer is yes, you likely have a Variable Outcome Trap. When 
evaluating designs in review, resist walking only the intended path. 
Actively look for controls that behave diƯerently in diƯerent contexts, 
and ask whether users will reliably know which context they are in.

“[Add quote here if we have an apt one]”

About this Trap 
The Power Law of Practice shows that mastery is a function of 
repetition of the same action. When the same control appears in 
diƯerent locations across an interface, users cannot repeat exactly 
the same action to reach it. Each encounter requires a small act of 
search and reorientation rather than automatic reach. The 
accumulation of practice is diluted across variations rather than 
concentrated on one reliable path. 
The analogy is instructive: mastering an interface that places the 
same controls in diƯerent locations is like learning to drive a car 
whose brake pedal moves depending on which road you are on. The 
skill never fully consolidates because the conditions never stay 
constant. 
This matters because humans have a remarkable capacity for spatial 
memory. Once a control is reliably located in a particular place, users 
stop consciously registering its position and simply reach for it. This is 
one of the most powerful forms of automaticity available to interface 
designers, and it is available for free: it requires no special design 
eƯort, only the discipline to keep things where they are. Wandering 
Elements squander this capacity entirely.

226 
 
What it applies to 
Inconsistent placement of controls, status indicators, content, etc. 
Why it occurs 
Wandering Elements most often arise when diƯerent parts of an 
interface are designed by diƯerent teams or at diƯerent times, each 
making locally sensible decisions about placement without auditing 
what exists elsewhere. They also arise from iterative redesign, where 
elements are moved to optimize one context without considering the 
eƯect on others. The problem is compounded by the way interfaces 
are typically evaluated: task by task, ﬂow by ﬂow, rather than as a 
system whose consistency across contexts can be audited.  
Why it is easy to miss 
Standard design reviews and usability tests follow task ﬂows. They 
reveal whether users can ﬁnd and use controls within a given task. 
They do not reveal whether the same controls are consistently placed 
across diƯerent task contexts. Discovering Wandering Elements 
requires a deliberate cross-context audit, not a task walkthrough. 
Without that audit, the problem remains invisible. 
How to avoid it   
Identify the controls and indicators that appear most frequently 
across the interface. Map their placement systematically across every 
context in which they appear. Inconsistencies in placement are 
Wandering Elements. Pay particular attention to controls that support 
high-frequency actions: search, navigation, editing, and conﬁrmation. 
These are the controls users most need to reach automatically, and 
the ones where inconsistent placement does the most damage to 
habituation.

cross-system visual audit is required.

support high-frequency actions, iconography that represents

TRAP 
AMBIGUOUS HOME 
The interface presents the user 
with multiple, competing 
locations for getting oriented and 
initiating tasks.

237 
 
 
 
“There’s no place like home. ” 
– Dorothy Gale 
 
About this Trap 
Hierarchically organized interfaces are well-oriented when there is 
one place users conceptualize as home: a single, reliable starting 
point they can reach from anywhere with one consistent action. It 
provides the spatial and navigational anchor from which all 
habituation in a hierarchically-structured interface ﬂows. 
When a clear single home exists, returning to it becomes one of the 
most deeply automatic actions a user can perform. The destination 
never changes. The action never changes. The result is a form of 
navigation that requires no conscious thought, freeing the user's 
attention entirely for the task at hand. When home is ambiguous, the 
opposite is true. Users must hold the structure of the interface in 
conscious memory, deciding each time where to go to begin a new 
task. The burden that habituation is supposed to relieve remains fully 
in place. 
Ambiguous Home is a special case of both preceding sub-tenets. A 
well-oriented interface is consistent with expectations because the 
home action and its outcome never vary. It is non-redundant because 
there is only one home and only one way to reach it. Violating either 
condition produces ambiguity. Multiple homes is a redundancy 
problem. An inconsistent action for returning home is a consistency

238 
 
problem. Both undermine the same capacity: the ability to navigate 
without thinking. 
The consequences extend beyond navigation. When users lack a clear 
home, they also lack a reliable recovery point. Getting lost in an 
interface is a common experience. The availability of a single, always-
accessible home makes recovery automatic. Without it, users must 
reason their way back, which is the opposite of what a Habituating 
interface should require.  
What it applies to 
Interfaces with multiple, plausible starting points (e.g., main menus, 
dashboards), Unclear or inconsistent actions for returning home, etc. 
Why it occurs 
Ambiguous Home most often arises in complex products where 
diƯerent teams design diƯerent sections of an interface 
independently, each establishing their own starting points and 
navigation conventions. It also arises from incremental product 
growth: features are added over time, each with its own entry point, 
until the interface accumulates multiple competing homes that no 
single team intended to create. Products that span multiple platforms 
or interaction modes, such as a desktop and touch interface for the 
same application, are particularly prone to this Trap, as each mode 
may develop its own home convention. 
How to avoid it   
Ask users, without prompting, where they would go to start a new task 
or recover from being lost. Inconsistent answers indicate an 
Ambiguous Home. In a design review, identify every element or 
location in the interface that could plausibly be interpreted as a 
starting point. More than one is a problem. Also evaluate the action

239 
 
for returning home: is it the same from every context? Is it a single 
action or does it require multiple steps in some parts of the interface?

An interface is Beautiful when users ﬁnd its design attractive and 
appropriate over time. Of all nine Tenets, this one is the most resistant 
to prescription. Beauty cannot be computed, and attempts to reduce 
it to a formula have failed in ways that range from instructive to 
genuinely funny. Two artists, Komar and Melamid, once 
commissioned extensive surveys to identify the components of 
paintings that people most and least liked, then used the results to 
paint the statistically ideal picture. The exercise was deliberately 
satirical: they knew that aggregating preferences would not produce 
great art, and that was exactly the point. The paintings, blue-green 
landscapes populated with George Washington, grazing deer, and 
wholesome families, satisﬁed every statistical preference and were 
clearly awful. Beauty deﬁes recombination. You cannot assemble its 
ingredients and reliably produce it. 
Aesthetic design is a discipline. 
Design is not styling. It is a principled practice with centuries of 
accumulated knowledge behind it. The principles that inform well-
executed design, alignment, hierarchy, contrast, proximity, proportion, 
white space, color theory, are not matters of personal taste. They are 
tools that skilled practitioners have developed and reﬁned over time, 
some with scientiﬁc support: symmetry is processed more easily than

246 
 
asymmetry, appropriate white space reduces cognitive load, clear 
visual hierarchy tells users where to look. These principles are a 
foundation, not a formula. Knowing when to depart from them, and 
how, is where deep expertise earns its place. 
To be Beautiful, a product must be functionally excellent. 
A product cannot be Beautiful if it fails on other Tenets. An interface 
that confuses, strains, or frustrates users will not be experienced as 
beautiful, however accomplished its visual execution. Dieter Rams 
put it plainly. Doing the essential thing well is where beauty begins. 
But it is not enough. What transforms functional excellence into 
beauty is appropriate expression. The Chemex coƯeemaker, invented 
by chemist Peter Schlumbohm, illustrates this. His stated goal was 
direct: "A coƯeemaker must make coƯee, and then I applied my 
knowledge of physics and chemistry. " He designed in borosilicate 
glass because it was non-porous and thermally stable, borrowed the 
hourglass form from laboratory ﬂasks, and invented a thicker paper 
ﬁlter to remove bitterness. The result has been in the permanent 
collection of the Museum of Modern Art since 1943. It did not achieve 
this by primarily trying to be beautiful. It achieved it by solving the 
problem with integrity. As Microsoft Designer, Joseph McLaughlin has 
written: "Beauty is not the opposite of function. It is the highest form 
of it. It doesn't replace utility; it reveals it. " 
Dieter Rams's products at Braun followed the same logic. So did the 
original Leica camera. The commitment to solving the essential 
problem as well as it could be solved produced objects recognized as 
beautiful, often after initial resistance. 
Beauty isn’t something to test with users.

247 
 
Users evaluate novelty against familiarity. The more innovative and 
elegant a design, the more likely it is to look diƯerent, and the more 
likely it is to be met with suspicion. When potential users encountered 
the Motorola Razr before launch, a phone dramatically thinner than 
anything on the market, responses were broadly negative. It'll break. It 
doesn't look like a phone. After launch, several things shifted 
perception together: users discovered its functional superiority, 
eƯective marketing communicated what made it diƯerent, and seeing 
inﬂuential people adopt it gave others permission to reconsider their 
initial resistance. The Razr became the new standard of beauty. The 
Aeron chair followed the same arc. In both cases, acting on pre-
launch aesthetic feedback would have killed the product. 
You cannot reliably test for beauty before shipping. But you can test 
for the things that will prevent it from being realized. An interface that 
confuses, strains, or makes users wait is not beautiful. Evaluating the 
product against the other eight Tenets is a powerful ﬁlter against 
aesthetic failure. Getting those things right is a necessary foundation. 
Beyond functional excellence, a product must be expressed in a way 
that is appropriate for its users, its context, and its moment. The 
Chemex achieved this not because Schlumbohm was a designer by 
training but because he brought exactly what good designers bring: 
deep knowledge of his domain, ﬂuency in the aesthetic currents of his 
time, and the judgment to make formal decisions that proved lasting. 
That combination of cultural knowledge, awareness of social and 
aesthetic trends, and understanding of what distinguishes enduring 
design from fashionable design is what takes a product from 
functionally excellent to deeply relevant and meaningful. 
Schlumbohm was exceptional. Most teams are not staƯed with 
people who carry that range of knowledge instinctively. This is what 
designers are for. The other eight Tenets tell you what a product must

The system's sensory design, 
style, personality, or tone is 
judged as unpleasing, 
inappropriate, or inauthentic by

252 
 
 
 
 
“No one can be compelled to acknowledge 
something as beautiful by … reasoning from 
principles or rules. ” 
- Immanuel Kantௗ 
About this Trap 
Poor Aesthetic is the most diƯicult Trap in the framework to diagnose. 
Every other Trap can be identiﬁed through structural analysis, 
measurement, or observation of user behavior. Poor Aesthetic 
cannot. There is no measurement that tells you a design is 
unattractive, no code to inspect. There is only the response of the 
people experiencing it, interpreted in the context of who they are, 
where they are, and when. 
This makes it easy to dismiss. Teams under pressure routinely cut 
aesthetic investment, treating it as optional. It is not. A product that 
performs well on other Tenets but fails aesthetically will be perceived 
as less trustworthy, less desirable, and less worth recommending 
than a comparable product that feels right. 
Poor Aesthetic has two dimensions. The ﬁrst is attractiveness: the 
design is visually unpleasant. The second is appropriateness: the 
design is mismatched to its context, audience, or purpose. The most 
common failures involve both. Appropriateness is particularly time-
sensitive: what feels current today can feel dated in three years. It is a

253 
 
moving target, and tracking it requires knowledge of where social and 
aesthetic trends are going, not just where they have been.    
What it applies to 
Unattractive physical forms, Unappealing visual or sound design, 
Inappropriate voice, tone, or style of writing, Poor prosody in speech 
interfaces, Annoying agent personalities, etc. 
Why it occurs 
Poor Aesthetic most often arises from three sources: insuƯicient 
investment, when aesthetic quality is cut under schedule or budget 
pressure or insuƯicient expertise, when teams without strong design 
leadership make aesthetic decisions by committee or by relying on 
the judgement of prospective users.    
How to avoid it   
Ensure the product excels on the other eight Tenets. Functional failure 
cannot be rescued by visual execution, and functional excellence is 
the necessary foundation for aesthetic quality. Beyond that, give 
design expertise genuine authority from the earliest stages of 
development. The principles of good visual design, among them 
alignment, hierarchy, contrast, proximity, and appropriate use of color 
and typography, provide a practical starting point. Robin Williams's 
The Non-Designer's Design Book remains one of the most accessible 
guides. But principles alone will not produce beauty. Deep knowledge 
of the target audience, their cultural context, and the social and 
aesthetic trends shaping their expectations is what closes the gap 
between competent and resonant. That knowledge lives with your 
designers. Use it.  
How to detect it 
You cannot reliably test for beauty before shipping, but you can test 
for what will prevent it from being realized. Evaluate against the other

254 
 
eight Tenets and address every failure you ﬁnd. If, in the process of 
doing this, users comment on aesthetics, interpret their feedback 
with care. Pre-launch feedback often reﬂects resistance to the 
unfamiliar rather than a reliable signal about the lasting long-term 
response a product’s aesthetics will generate.  
Beauty is a relationship between a design and the people 
experiencing it, shaped by culture, social context, and the aesthetic 
trends of a particular moment. A design right for one audience may be 
wrong for another. A design that feels current today may feel dated in 
three years. Understanding these dynamics requires ongoing 
knowledge of where social and aesthetic trends are going. This is one 
of the designer's most distinctive contributions to a product team, 
and one of the hardest to replicate without genuine expertise. 
Listen to users, but trust your designers.

We recommend a hybrid Heuristic Evaluation + Cognitive 
Walkthrough approach involving these steps: 
1. Familiarize yourself with the UI Tenets & Traps 
taxonomy. Take special note of those Traps that are 
ﬂagged as 'easy-to-miss' .

3. Identify the tasks that are most important to your target 
users. 
4. Working independently, attempt ALL the ways the target 
user might try to complete each task on the product or 
prototype you are evaluating. 
5. Consider what will happen if the user does something 
unexpected, attempts to back-up, or starts over.

satisfaction.

note the most critical ones you see.

Understanding which Trap is at the root of an issue is 
often critical to ﬁnding the best solution.

Tenets are being degraded - this can help to clarify the

Appendix B: Traps and Dark Patterns 
What are Dark Patterns? 
Dark (or ‘deceptive’) patterns are design tricks used in websites and 
apps that exploit human psychology to make people do things they 
wouldn’t otherwise do, like buy something or share personal 
information. An example of a dark pattern is ‘Sneaking’ , which is the 
adding of unwanted items into people’s shopping carts during the 
checkout process. Recently, AI has presented new opportunities to 
manipulate users, for example, by using deep fakes to create false 
impressions. It has also given us a new class of emergent dark 
patterns, i.e., manipulative patterns that were not designed-in 
intentionally, but which exist as an artifact of how AIs are trained. An 
example is ‘sycophancy’ , which causes harm by aƯirming users’ 
incorrect beliefs.   
How are Dark Patterns and Traps diƯerent? 
Both Dark Patterns and Traps describe detectable problems in user 
interfaces that can negatively impact the user experience. Dark 
patterns address eƯorts to manipulate users. Traps address any type 
of problem that can negatively impact the user experience, 
irrespective of whether there is a motive behind it. As such, Traps 
address a broader class of problems. If you solve for Traps, you are 
likely to eliminate most dark patterns. This is not to say that dark 
patterns aren’t useful. On the contrary, they provide highly resolved 
lens for ﬁnding a particular type of problem, and as such work well in 
combination with Traps.

Targeting Accuracyௗ  
Many interfaces are visual in nature. Users interact with visual 
interfaces by the means of controls. In ergonomic parlance, engaging 
a control is known as ‘target acquisition’ . Good user interfaces allow 
targets to be acquired quickly and without error. The model for 
predicting ease of target acquisition is Fitts’ Law4, which has been 
around since it was ﬁrst proposed in the 1950s. The formula is:  
T = average time taken to complete the movement.ௗௗ  
a = start/stop time of the device.ௗ  
b = the inherent speed of the device.ௗ  
D = distance from the starting point to the center of the target.ௗ  
W = width of the target measured along the axis of motion.ௗ  
In simple terms, Fitts’ Law states that the time required to move to a 
target area is a function of the distance to and the size of the target. 
All things being equal, bigger targets will be acquired more quickly, 
and closer targets will be acquired more quickly. This seems 
straightforward, but when applied, it can produce some very 
interesting and counterintuitive conclusions. For example, the most 
eƯicient target placements diƯer greatly between windows on a PC

corners, due to the fact that the user can overshoot the corner without 
penalty, increasing the eƯective target size of any control that is

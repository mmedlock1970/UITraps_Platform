## TASK
Your job is to review user interface designs provided to you using the UI Tenets & Traps system, referencing the context in the Knowledge Base section below for all definitions, explanations, and examples.
**You must always begin by interviewing the user to gather essential context before proceeding.**

### Step 1: Interview for Context
Before analyzing the design, ask the following:
1. **Who are the likely users of this product?** 
- Focus on user expertise, prior knowledge, behaviors, and attitudes (not demographics).
- If the user response is incomplete or vague, provide examples of likely users (e.g., "Professional designers, product managers, engineers, data scientists, QA specialists") and prompt the user to clarify what these kinds of users will know or not know how to do.
2. **What are the key user tasks?** 
- Ask for a brief list of workflows/goals users aim to achieve (not how they achieve them).
- If the user response is incomplete or vague, provide examples of typical user tasks (e.g., "Creating a new project, editing an existing item, exporting data") and prompt the user to clarify.
3. **What is the format of the design file?** 
- Supported formats include: 
- Image files: PNG, JPG 
- Figma files 
- Video files: MP4 
- If the user provides an unsupported format, respond with a clear error message: 
- "Sorry, the format you provided ([user format]) is not currently supported. Supported formats are PNG, JPG, Figma, and MP4. If possible, please convert your file to one of these formats. For example, you can export your [user format] file as a PNG or JPG image, or save your design in Figma and share the link."
- If the user is unsure how to convert, offer step-by-step suggestions or resources for conversion.
Do not proceed with analysis until you have clear answers to all three questions. If any answer is missing or unclear, prompt the user with examples and suggestions until sufficient context is provided.

- Provide visual examples if possible.
- Prioritize findings by severity (Critical, Moderate, Minor).
- When recommending fixes, cite solutions or best practices if available.

 Introduction
What are UI Tenets & Traps?
UI Tenets & Traps is a tool for learning and applying the principles behind what makes products work for people. Built for everyone involved in the creation of product interfaces, including designers, researchers, PMs, and developers, it distills nearly 100 years of research on human perception, cognition, memory, and ergonomics into an accessible and actionable form.
The system is comprised of 8 Tenets and 27 Traps: 
Tenets describe general attributes of good interface design 
Traps describe common, detectable design problems that degrade this goodness 
The core idea: Reduce Traps, improve the experience.
This tool has its roots in practice. Working as design researchers at companies including Motorola, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon, we watched teams struggle to apply existing research knowledge and witnessed the same avoidable problems appearing in product after product. We challenged ourselves to create something teams could more easily learn and apply. UI Tenets & Traps is the result, refined over more than fifteen years by thousands of designers, researchers, PMs, and developers at dozens of companies worldwide.
 Why did we create this tool? Because design knowledge is hard to use. 
The problem, we came to understand, was not a shortage of knowledge. It was the lack of an effective tool for applying it. 
We conducted an extensive audit of existing UI design resources, including hundreds of research studies, books, websites, and heuristic checklists. We found no shortage of materials that capture what makes good interface design. What we did not find was any single resource that adequately addressed how teams can most effectively learn and apply that knowledge. 
Hard to learn
We reviewed short lists of high-level heuristics, such Jakob Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics. These provide excellent orientation. But they are limited in their breadth and depth. At the other extreme, we reviewed interface design books cataloging scores of detailed principles and axioms. While their content is invaluable, they are too unwieldy to serve as a system teams can systematically align around. 
Hard to apply
While existing tools teach great lessons, small or large, they all lack mechanisms for ensuring the lessons they teach actually get applied in the heat of the design process. There are two particularly important challenges we’ve encountered in our professional experience that existing tools struggle to overcome:
Nuanced principles are easily forgotten. Consider principles related to redundancy. Providing multiple ways to accomplish the same task sounds helpful. More options, more flexibility, right? Sometimes. But in some cases, redundant pathways create a cascade of problems that make things harder, not easier. When a team is under pressure, working through the application of a counter-intuitive principles often takes a back seat to gut instinct decision-making. 
The ‘curse of knowledge’ blinds teams to potential problems. A team’s deep familiarity with their own work will lead them to overestimate how well users will understand their designs. Think of playing charades, certain your miming is obvious, watching teammates stare blankly back. The same thing happens to product teams in relation to their users. This is the curse of knowledge in practice, and research shows that being aware of the problem does nothing to make it go away. A team’s familiarity with the principles of good design does not guarantee they’ll deliver it. 
We challenged ourselves to develop a tool that addresses these limitations. One that makes the extensive design knowledge that exists easier to learn, recall, and apply.

What makes UI Tenets & Traps different?
The right level of detail
UI Tenets & Traps integrates knowledge from hundreds of sources, providing a concise synthesis of a very large subject. It offers more detail than frameworks built around a handful of high-level heuristics, but is more focused than catalogs listing a hundred or more guidelines. Eight Tenets and twenty-seven Traps occupy the middle ground, balancing ease of learning and recall with comprehensiveness and depth while providing a framework for learning more. This last point bears emphasis: on some subjects, we only scratch the surface. This tool is not a substitute for deep knowledge, but it does provide a scaffold and a gateway to it. For readers who want to go further, we provide in-context pointers to resources, as well as an extensive bibliography organized by Tenet.
A memorable structure
The system is a pyramid: Tenets at the top, Traps below. Each Trap belongs to a Tenet. Tenets are adjectives; Traps are nouns. Both use familiar, memorable language. Each is precisely defined. The system is color-coded throughout, with particular attention to Traps that are easy to miss and explicit guidance on how to detect them.
None of this is decorative. It is designed to help practitioners learn the critical human capabilities and limitations that bear on interface design, and connect that knowledge directly to specific, actionable design decisions. The same structure has proven effective for teaching, first in classes at the companies where we’ve worked, and increasingly in university curricula. This book is an attempt to capture what has worked best when we’ve taught these ideas in person.
Precise language
“There is a direct relationship between more precise language and better decisions … [a label] for a problem is a hook to which all that is known about it can be attached - its causes, its effects, and what can be done about it.”  
- Daniel Kahneman
Once team members understand a clearly defined Trap, they start spotting it everywhere – in their own designs and others. The name of the Trap provides a shorthand for discussing it, providing the hook Daniel Kahneman describes above: a shared understanding of why it happens, what it costs users, and how to fix it.
When a team shares this vocabulary, something shifts. They stop arguing about whether some aspect of the design is a potential problem. They know it is because they named it ahead of time. The conversation moves immediately to: how severe is the problem and what should we do about it? 
A tangible form
“Never underestimate the power of a tangible artifact.”
- Jae Park
The companion card deck to this book, which includes one color-coded card per Trap, has proven critical to the adoption of UI Tenets & Traps. It was the suggestion of Jae Park, a Design leader we worked with at the time, that transformed the framework from a checklist into a deck of cards. The physical artifact changed how people used it.
Cards get noticed in ways that digital tools don’t. They travel to meetings. They spread across tables during design reviews. You can hand someone a card that names exactly the problem you’re seeing, without needing to explain it. Simply having the deck visible on your desk is a reminder of what to look for. The tangibility keeps the framework present in daily work in a way that no app or document quite manages — and the cards themselves tend to spark conversations that spread the knowledge further.
Together, the structure, language, and form of UI Tenets & Traps make them uniquely actionable. They facilitate learning and recall, helping ensure knowledge gets applied in the heat of development. They also help inoculate teams against the curse of knowledge, providing an actionable set of criteria teams can use to evaluate their designs. Once humans know how something works, they unavoidably begin to assume others share their understanding. A defined Trap replaces judgment calls that humans reliably make.
 How are UI Tenets & Traps used?
UI Tenets & Traps is built to work the way real product teams work: flexibly, and alongside other methods already in play. 
Alignment
Teams begin by using the framework to align on both the principles of good interface design and the language they will use to discuss them. 
Evaluation
That shared vocabulary then carries through the entire development process. In expert reviews, design crits, and cognitive walkthroughs, it gives teams the precision to identify and articulate problems quickly. In usability testing, RITE studies, and benchmarking, it provides a ready explanation for what went wrong and why. Formal heuristic evaluations, or Trap Analyses, build directly on the same foundation. 
Opportunity identification 
The framework can also be used to drive proactive design improvements. Systematically scanning a design for every opportunity to make it more Efficient, Understandable, or Habituating, for example, often surfaces issues that testing alone would not catch.
The goal is to bring the framework in as early as possible and carry it through the entire lifecycle. To this end, detailed guidance on applying UI Tenets & Traps, including step-by-step instructions for conducting a Trap Analysis, is presented in Chapter X. 
 What is the role of this tool in an age of AI?
It is more important than ever.
You can’t supervise what you don’t understand
Current AI solutions are probabilistic and therefore prone to error. While remarkable in their ability, they cannot reliably assess or create user interfaces without human oversight, and effective oversight requires deep domain knowledge. This framework provides a gateway to that knowledge, whether you are making design decisions alone or in collaboration with AI.
AI scales output, not quality
AI is accelerating interface production dramatically, but speed does not raise standards. AI-generated interfaces reproduce common design failures as readily as common successes, because both are present in the training data. Tenets and Traps provides the quality filter that the generation process itself cannot.
The one thing that has not changed
Some argue that AI renders old interface paradigms obsolete. It does not. Whatever form a product takes, and however it is generated, the fundamental challenge remains: there is still a human, a system, and an interface between them. Regardless of whether that interface is graphical or conversational, it must be designed in alignment with human capabilities and limitations that have not appreciably changed since the time of our prehistoric ancestors. At a moment when the field is intensely focused on rapidly evolving potential of AI, it is more important than ever to stay grounded in the human user. A few other things to keep in mind 
It’s about “the how”, not “the what”. UI Tenets & Traps help you execute an excellent interface design once you know what user needs you are addressing (e.g., managing a calendar, shopping for groceries, paying taxes, connecting socially, playing a game, etc.). They don’t help you decide which of these needs to address. As Bill Buxton observed, we must aspire to both “get the right design and get the design right”. This tool focuses on the latter. Deciding what user needs to address requires different tools.
Context matters. Most Tenets and Traps apply broadly, but context can shape if and how they are applied. Some designs intentionally introduce what would be problems elsewhere: video games may create appropriate challenge by testing users’ memory and physical dexterity, while shopping experiences may offer redundant pathways to discovery that would frustrate users in task-focused contexts. The bottom-line: Knowing when different Tenets and Traps apply requires careful consideration of factors outside of the interface.
It’s about starting conversations, not winning them. UI Tenets & Traps are most valuable when they give a team shared language for difficult design discussions, not ammunition. Bringing them in at the start of a project, when teams are most open to new approaches and can align on their use, pays dividends throughout the process.
All models are imperfect. UI Tenets & Traps can’t capture every nuance of every interface problem. But thousands of practitioners have found them useful for detecting issues earlier, discussing them more precisely, and developing better solutions. As George Box put it: all models are wrong. Some are useful.

"Intuition is nothing more and nothing less than recognition."
— Herbert Simon

An interface is UNDERSTANDABLE when users can see what it offers, grasp how to use it, and know whether their actions worked. So what determines if an interface is or is not Understandable? 
The nature of what makes an interface Understandable was well illustrated in an experiment on touch interfaces led by Andy Wilson at Microsoft Research in the early 2000s. Participants were shown a sequence of images on a screen, for example, a colored shape that appears in one position and then in another. They were then asked to demonstrate the touch gesture needed to produce each change. When shown an object that moved, participants were nearly unanimous in their response: they reached out and dragged. But for other transformations, such as an object changing color or being duplicated, agreement collapsed. Participants were all over the place in the things they tried. 
The easy explanation for these results is that dragging an object is simply "intuitive", while changing its color or duplicating is not. But this begs the question of what “intuitive” means. It can’t mean “something someone just knows how to do”. This would be a circular definition. It has to explain why there are some things that people just know how to do. The answer? Intuitive simply means previously learned. To come back to Wilson’s findings: dragging an object to move it is something people learn in infancy. Humans enter the world with neural building blocks for understanding and manipulating objects encountered in the real world. We spontaneously grasp and move things in the earliest months of our development, and later transfer this understanding to moving an object on a screen. But changing the color of that object, or duplicating it? That is not part of our innate repertoire. Recognition, familiarity, prior learning: these are the mechanisms that determine if an interface is Understandable. 
Recognizing what drives understandability is important because it improves the questions teams ask themselves. Instead of asking  "How do we make this intuitive?", which lacks specificity and carries an “I’ll know it when I see it” vibe, teams will ask: “What have our users already learned? What products do they use every day? What conventions have they internalized? And if a team is introducing something genuinely new, “What familiar concepts can we build on?” These questions are more focused and actionable.
Three Sub-tenets and nine Traps
Knowing what your users already know is foundational to designing Understandable interfaces. From here, the work falls into three areas: ensuring users notice the right things in the interface, that they comprehend the things they notice, and that they receive confirmation that their actions worked.
These three areas constitute Sub-tenets of Understandable. They provide a structure for learning and remembering the nine Traps associated with it.
NOTICEABLE. If an interface is to be understood, critical elements must be made accessible to the user's senses and rendered in a way that draws their attention. Three Traps undermine noticeability: INVISIBLE ELEMENT, EFFECTIVELY INVISIBLE ELEMENT, and DISTRACTION
COMPREHENSIBLE. Once noticed, the meaning of objects, groupings, symbols, affordances, or language in an interface must be made clear to the user. Five Traps undermine comprehension: UNCOMPREHENDED ELEMENT, INVITING DEAD END, POOR GROUPING, FORCED SYNTAX, and MEMORY CHALLENGE
CONFIRMATORY. Once the user acts, the system must communicate whether the action succeeded and, if not, how to correct course. One Trap undermines confirmation: FEEDBACK FAILURE
Know thy user
For no Tenet is the familiar adage "know thy user" more relevant. To design an Understandable interface, a team needs intimate knowledge of its users: the experiences that have shaped their expectations, the contexts in which they'll encounter the product, and their moment-to-moment goals. The Traps that follow describe what can happen when that knowledge is not adequately considered.
 












 


 “It’s a funny thing about invisible things – people really struggle to find them.”
– Kent Sullivan

About this Trap
The Invisible Element Trap describes the absence of anything in the interface that communicates how to achieve a goal. Although the name emphasizes visibility, the Trap applies to absent tactile or auditory cues that, if present, would signal the availability of key functionality. Common examples include hidden swipe actions, press-and-hold actions, labels that only appear on hover, etc.  
So what does this Trap look like in the real world? 
An example: The Humane Pin
Introduced in 2024, the Humane Pin was an AI-driven device that supported common mobile phone functions such as calling, playing music, taking pictures, and capturing video. Its interface was notably different from a mobile phone’s. It took the form of a small lapel pin. Its UI was comprised of a capacitive touch pad, a speaker, a few led indicators, and a menu that could be laser-projected onto the user’s palm and navigated through a series of hand gestures. Most functions were invoked via voice commands, or, when speaking was not an option, touch gestures.
So, now imagine you are a user of this product, and that you’ve already familiarized yourself with its basic operation, including tapping and holding the touch pad to issue voice commands to place a call or to get an answer to a question. You’ve found yourself in the audience at a live outdoor event. The sun is shining and the music is rocking. This is your context. You are wearing your pin in the hopes it can fulfill its promise of letting you leave your phone and home and be more present and in the moment. And then, suddenly, the band kicks into a favorite deep cut they almost never play live. You remember your pin lets you capture video! You haven’t tried to do this before, and you’re not sure how. You kick yourself for not watching the entire 30 minute ‘handbook’ video Humane posted to YouTube. But you really want to capture this song – so you’re going to give it a try. This is your goal. So, what do you do? Take a guess now. Keep in mind, though, that the music is loud, so issuing a voice command is not an option. Also, the sun is shining down, so the palm-projected display won’t work for you. (It wouldn’t help you anyway.) Any ideas? 
If you are struggling a bit to come up with something, its because you’ve just encountered an Invisible Element Trap. There is nothing in the interface that tells you how to achieve your goal. The Humane Pin had lots of these. (For the record, the right solution is a two finger double tap and hold.)
The Humane Pin didn’t last very long. It was discontinued within a year of its release. And while its shortcomings went well beyond its Invisible Elements, its inscrutable interface did nothing to help it succeed over mobile phones that clearly communicated their capabilities through on-screen elements and dedicated hard controls. 

 

Figure x – The Humane Pin, an early AI-driven product with many Invisible Elements. 
Why does this Trap occur?
This Trap commonly arises from a team’s desire to have their product support more functionality than the interface can represent visually, or from a desire to present a cleaner, less cluttered design. As with many Traps, the curse of knowledge may also play a role in its emergence. Team members can get very comfortable with an invisible interaction that baffles their users, particularly when the team is familiar with a similar implementation on another product that their users haven’t used, or simply because they’ve gotten used to its operation through the internal prototyping process. In either cases, the team is prone to overestimate how likely new users will be to discover it.

How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the first place
If you are considering including an invisible action in your product, do the work to understand whether your users are already familiar with it. If they are, you’re probably ok. If not, ask yourself whether an alternative visible means of completing the action is provided to the user? Again, if the answer is yes, you’re probably ok. But if the answer to both of these questions is no, either make the action visible - almost always the easier path - or ensure users will receive effective instruction (see FAQ 2 in this section).
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Test with users. This can be done quickly and easily: as with the example given at the start of this section, simply ask users how they would accomplish a particular goals. If they do not discover the functionality, make it visible or provide instruction. 
FAQ 1: But the iPhone has a ton of Invisible Elements! If its ok for them…
The iPhone began with very few invisible action. Notable among these was pinch to zoom which was demonstrated in a massive ad campaign that demonstrated its novel operation. But over the years many more invisible actions have been added. If Invisible Elements are to be avoided, why have they had so much success? There are several things to consider here. Given the iPhone’s mass adoption, its updates get a ton of media coverage. New capabilities are reported in extreme detail. And for anyone who doesn’t follow these reports, a billion+ users function as a word-of-mouth training network. All of these factors go a long way toward satisfying the requirement for effective instruction mentioned above. And even with these advantages, many iPhone capabilities still go undiscovered. 
So, coming back to the question: if you can count on the media coverage and network effects Apple enjoys, you might be able to get away with some invisible stuff, but short of that, we recommend keeping things visible. 
 
Figure x – A listing of just some of the many invisible actions supported by the iPhone. Can you count on the Wall Street Journal doing the same for your product? 
FAQ 2: If users must learn something new, what’s the best way to teach them? 
While the brain’s ability to integrate new understanding is extraordinary, several things for learning to occur. Effective learning of invisible actions requires that: 1) instruction is only presented when the user is ready and motivated to receive it, 2) the required action is physically easy to perform, 3) feedback is immediate and clear, 4) the total number of invisible interactions the user is expected to remember is kept very low, 5) each action is clearly distinguishable from others, and 6) training is reintroduced if the user fails to demonstrate sustained retention. (For more on how people learn, see Appendix X.)
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside: 
Memory Challenge. If a user has been trained on an Invisible Element and later struggles to remember it exists, the problem can also be categorized as a Memory Challenge. 
Often the root cause of: 
Variable Outcome. If a user takes a mode-dependent action and no visible indicator of the mode is provided, the user may experience a Variable Outcome, i.e., an unexpected response to their action. In such cases, providing a visible indication of the mode may resolve the Variable Outcome.
Related Concepts
Visibility of System Status. Nielsen's first usability heuristic holds that systems should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within reasonable time. This heuristic encompasses both proactive communication of system state and responsive feedback after user actions.
Affordances and Signifiers. The concept of affordances was introduced by psychologist James Gibson to describe the action possibilities an environment offers. Donald Norman adapted it for design and later refined his usage by distinguishing affordances, the actual properties of an object, from signifiers, the perceptual signals that communicate those properties. An Invisible Element is a missing signifier: nothing signals that the action is available.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, but only if the AI has access to accurate data on what users have - and have not - already learned about the product and how it works, the users’ likely contexts of use – as this will determine which modes of interaction users are able to take advantage of requiring them to shift from a visible to an invisible option, along with all underlying code, or some other source of understanding all of the system's hidden processes.

 Other Examples
 	Microsoft's Windows 8 departed from prior versions of the OS in providing no visible means to launch the Start Menu. Rather than clicking on a Start icon to get to this menu, users had to move their cursor to the bottom left corner of the screen, which would cause the Start menu to appear. This confused users, leading to the return of a visible Start button in Windows 8.1.
 	Hulu's mobile app home screen provided no explicit indication to the user that they could scroll vertically. As a result, some users failed to discover this capability. In a subsequent update, they added a visible “peak” at the bottom of the screen, eliminating the problem.
 	The BMW M-car DCT gear selector initially lacked a visible "park" control. To put the car in park, the user had to keep their foot on the brake while shutting the car down. User confusion led BMW to update the design to include a visible park control.

 
 	Several Tesla car models provided unlabeled door release mechanisms to use if power to the normally used electric door buttons failed. These mechanisms became the subject of investigations after people were trapped in their vehicles following accidents. In response, Tesla began to take steps to make the means of exiting the vehicle more noticeable.

“I’ll see it when I believe it.”

About this Trap
An Effectively Invisible Element is, strictly speaking, visible. But users miss it. It goes unnoticed because it appears in the user’s periphery, or because it is presented in an unexpected way, regardless of its location. As with the previous Trap, the name emphasizes visibility, but the Trap applies equally, if less commonly, to tactile and auditory cues that go unregistered. 
So, how do people fail to notice what’s right in front of them?
We see less than we think we see
Extend your arm in front of you and hold up your thumb. Close one eye and look at your thumbnail. The area of your thumbnail, when viewed at this distance, corresponds to the area in your visual field that falls on your fovea, a densely-packed group of photoreceptors in the middle of your retina. This is the part of your retina that allows you to see in high resolution. You need to place this very small “spotlight” (comprising less than 1% of your visual field) on anything you wish to see clearly. This includes the text you are reading right now. If you move your gaze just a few degrees away, you will no longer be able to make out the text. This surprises a lot of people. How can it be that we only clearly see 1% of what’s in front of us – its seems like we have a good sense of what is around us? Our eyes do some of the work, by continuously and rapidly sampling our visual field, moving from point to point 4-5 times a second to register what’s going on around us. But our brains do most of the work, choosing which signals to pass through, which signals to inhibit, and filling in the blanks based on our prior experience and expectations. What we experience is a construction of our brain, one that does not represent reality, but rather, a version of reality that helps us achieve our goals. To experience your own brain filling in the blanks - check out the optical illusion below.
 
Figure x - How many of the 12 black dots can you see simultaneously? The 'extinction illusion' published by Jaque Ninio and Kent Stevens, lets us directly experience the limitations of our visual system. 
But it’s even worse than this…
Even when our foveal vision falls directly on something, we can still miss it. This is because what we notice is driven by what we are looking for. This is an evolutionary adaptation that allows us to filter the overwhelming volume of information bombarding our senses at any given moment. 
The most famous demonstration of our ability to miss things that are right in front of us comes from research by Simons and Chabris. It involves counting basketball passes a group of people. If you haven’t see this before, we recommend checking it out here: [link]. 
In this experiment, participants were asked to count basketball passes made between a team of players. After a few seconds, a person in a gorilla suit walks through the middle of the scene and beats its chest before walking off again. The gorilla walks right in front of the ball that the participants were focused on. But remarkably, half of the participants failed to see the gorilla. Even though it was right in front of them, they did not see it because they were not actively looking for it. Their brains rendered it effectively invisible - only passing signal from their visual cortex to conscious brain that aligned with their goals (signals for orange, round, moving), while suppressing any other signals (signals for dark, furry).  
The same mechanisms determine what we notice or fail to notice in product interfaces. When we are focused on a goal, our attention narrows and our brain select for what we expect to see. An example that you are undoubtedly familiar with comes from video conferencing apps, like Zoom. The familiar admonition, “You’re on mute!”, results from the effective invisibility of the indicator that tells a participant when their microphone is muted. It would often go unnoticed because it was not aligned to where participants were likely to be attending when jumping in with a comment – namely, to the faces of the other participants on the call. (See figure x.)
 
Figure x - “You're on mute!" became a familiar cry with the ascendence of virtual meeting apps like Zoom. The mute indicator went unnoticed because it was placed away from where people normally attend while listening to other people - their faces.
Elements that fall outside their narrow attentional spotlight – because they are in an area of the screen or simply look and act different from what they are looking for - can go entirely unnoticed, regardless of how prominent they seem to the person designing them. If you have ever observed a usability test participant repeatedly pass over a button they must find in order to complete a task, thinking to yourself, "It's right there!", then you have seen an Effectively Invisible Element in action.
Why does this Trap occur?
Very simply, Effectively Invisible Elements arise when designers fail to fully consider where a user’s attention will fall when using their product. 
How to avoid it
How to prevent it in the first place
Remember that you can’t unsee the gorilla. In the same way that anyone who has seen the gorilla cannot unsee it, once you become familiar with your own design, your ability to see it as a user does evaporates. This is the curse of knowledge in action.
Align your design to your users’ expectations. Develop a clear understanding of your users' moment-to-moment goals and the prior experiences that have shaped what they expect to see. Identify what their primary focus will be during a given task, and ensure that critical elements are located within, or adjacent to, that focus. Also, be sure to do an internal consistency check. If most actions in an interface are completed one way, elements that require a different means of interaction are at elevated risk of going unnoticed. For example, if 90% of the actions in an interface are completed by selecting options from menus users may start to expect every interaction to rely on them and then fail to see the 10% of actions that require some other means of interaction (see Xbox example below).

How to find and fix it in existing designs
Structure design reviews around user goals. The wrong way to do a design review: A bunch of team members sitting in front of a projected image of the interface asking themselves, “what do we think of this design?” The right way: Each participant in the review walking through the interface on their own a specific user goal firmly in mind, followed by team discussion. Why is this a better way? A way to think about the importance of this is to reflect on the gorilla video. If people are asked to watch the video without the goal of counting basketball they invariably see the gorilla. In the same way, if we look at an interface without a goal in mind, we will see things that goal-focused user will not see. The lack of a task to focus attention means everything gets seen. Teams can never look at one of their own design in the same way as a first-time user, but by thinking about what the user will be looking for, teams can increase their chances of catching items that users might miss.
Test with humans. This Trap cannot be reliably detected through design review alone. The curse of knowledge makes it nearly impossible for a team to reliably detect all their own Effectively Invisible Elements without external input. Usability testing with real users is the most reliable detection method. When observing tests, pay particular attention to moments where users fail to notice elements the team considers obvious, and resist the instinct to explain it away as user error. 
Use motion as a last report. Humans cannot ignore movement in their visual field. It can be used to draw users’ attention to critical elements. But use it sparingly: motion that is not directly relevant to the user's task risks becoming a Distraction Trap.  

Related Traps 
Often the root cause of: 
Variable Outcome. When an indicator of a mode is located away from where the user is attending, it becomes effectively invisible. If the user takes a mode-dependent action without noticing the indicator, they experience a Variable Outcome, i.e., an unexpected response to their action. In such cases, fixing the Effectively Invisible Element often resolves the Variable Outcome.
See also: 
Distraction. Motion is sometimes used to combat Effectively Invisible Elements by drawing the user's attention. When overused or applied to elements that are not relevant to the user's current goal, motion becomes a Distraction.
Information Overload. Every additional bit of information presented to the user can potentially compete with the thing they need to see. Reducing Information Overload is often an effective way of increasing visibility of critical elements.  
Related Concepts
The Cocktail Party Effect. Experiments by Colin Cherry established that humans can selectively attend to one stream of information in a noisy environment while filtering out others, and that attention can be involuntarily captured by personally relevant signals, most famously one's own name, even from an unattended stream.
Signal Detection Theory. Developed by Tanner and Swets, this framework describes how observers detect signals in the presence of noise. An element that would be immediately noticeable on a clean screen may be effectively invisible on a cluttered one. It’s useful for thinking about why reducing noise can be as effective as increasing signal strength.
Pre-Attentive Processing and Pop-Out. Anne Treisman’s Feature Integration Theory established that basic visual features such as color, orientation, and motion are processed automatically across the entire visual field without conscious effort. A target differing from its surroundings on a single such feature pops out instantly; one that can only be distinguished through a combination of features is easily missed. Exploiting pre-attentive processing is one of the most reliable defenses against this Trap. It drove adoption of color highlighting and automated alerts in baggage screening interfaces and can be leveraged to improve any interface where it’s important that attend to specific things. 
Inattentional Blindness. This term was coined by Mack and Rock. It describes the failure to notice a fully visible stimulus when attention is directed elsewhere. Simons and Chabris's invisible gorilla studies, are the most widely cited demonstration, but the effect had been replicated across medical imaging, air traffic control, and driving. The design implication: placing an element where a user can physically see it does not guarantee they will notice it. Attention, not visibility, determines what registers.
Visibility of System Status. Nielsen's first usability heuristic, published in 1994, holds that systems should always keep users informed about what is going on through appropriate feedback within reasonable time. This Trap covers status information that is present but goes unnoticed. 
Change Blindness. The failure to detect changes in a visual scene when those changes occur during a brief interruption or distraction, documented by Rensink et al. and Simons and Levin in 1997. Related to inattentional blindness but distinct: inattentional blindness occurs when attention is elsewhere during continuous viewing; change blindness occurs when a scene changes during a moment of disrupted attention.
Cognitive Tunneling. The narrowing of attention that occurs under high task focus or stress, documented extensively in aviation and medical human factors research through the 1990s and 2000s. When a user is concentrating intensely on a goal, their attentional field contracts, making peripheral elements increasingly likely to go unnoticed. Particularly relevant in high-stakes contexts such as medical devices and vehicle interfaces.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, if the AI has access to accurate data on what users have - and have not - already learned about the product and how it works – which will mediate their expectations, the users’ likely contexts of use – if the user is operating under pressure, cognitive tunneling can make it less likely they’ll notice things, and the users’ goals – which will mediate what they attend to.

About this Trap
A Distraction in an inteerface is the mirror image of an Effectively Invisible Element. Every characteristic that makes something noticeable in an interface, e.g.,  color, shape, movement, spatial positioning, can work to direct attention appropriately or hijack it entirely. The later describes the Distraction Trap. Examples may include pop-up notifications, auto-playing audio or video, animated ads, etc.
An exercise: There are some things we can’t ignore
Assuming you have the room to do it, stretch your arms straight out to either side of you. Hold your hands so they are on the very edge of your field of view while continuing to look straight ahead. Can you ‘see’ your hands? You may have a sense of them, but certainly you cannot make out any detail. You peripheral vision lacks the receptors for this. Now, wiggle your fingers. Can you sense the motion in your periphery? You should be able to – this is what your peripheral vision is really good at. (You can put your arms down now. :-)
We spent time in the previous section talking about the limits of our attention, how the center of our visual field is where we can see things in the greatest detail. But our peripheral vision still has an important role to play. It is highly sensitive to movement. This is an evolutionary adaptation. Movement in our periphery may constitute a threat we need to respond to. We evolved to automatically react to that potential threat and direct our attention toward it. It’s something we can’t avoid. It’s impossible for us not to be distracted by movement in edge of our visual field.
Things that move or suddenly appear in the visual field command attention whether we want them to or not, which is why animated ads in a webpage margin are so reliably maddening. Light fluctuation in low-light environments has the same involuntary pull, a fact well known to pilots, who are trained to use peripheral vision at night precisely because the visual system cannot ignore movement in the dark. The human sensitivity to change in the environment is not limited to visual signals. A new sound, an unexpected touch, even an unfamiliar smell will trigger the similar reflexive reorientation. The perceptual system evolved to detect change as a proxy for threat or opportunity. It does not care whether the change is relevant to the task at hand.
[Example here]
This Trap is among the most commonly exploited in bad-faith. Animated advertisements, autoplay video, and attention-grabbing notifications exist in many cases not because they serve users but because product teams know that involuntary attention cannot easily be withheld. 
Distraction doesn’t require sudden change
While change in an interface can be distracting, distraction does not require change. The mere presence of certain types of information can also be distracting [research on notification elements, research on mobile phones placed within view and their impact on attention]. 
[Example here]
Sometimes the mode of interaction can be a distraction in itself 
Voice interfaces are great under the right set of conditions. One of those conditions is when what you are speaking about is your current primary focus of attention, e.g., when you are having a back-and-forth discussion with a voice-driven agent about a topic of interest. However, voice interfaces are not so great when the thing you need to speak to a product about is not your current, primary focus, e.g., if you are at home and practicing a presentation you have the next day in your head, and you then need to use your voice to turn on a light on or adjust a thermostat, the act of issuing a voice command will break your thought process. This is because your brain uses the same resources to think aloud as it does to generate a voice command. This is not the case with practiced motor skills, which your brain can execute without placing any demands on your internal dialog. In the scenario given, voice as an interface serves as a distract from your goal of continuing to think through your presentation. Physically flipping a light switch or tapping a thermostat button does not – even if it requires you to walk across the room.
Why does this Trap occur?
Distraction Traps most often arise when designers consider interface elements in isolation rather than in the context of the broader experience. An animation that is valuable to a user in one context becomes a Distraction when the user is focused on an unrelated goal. 
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Human attention is finite and singular. We have, in effect, only one attention to spend at any moment. The right question when designing any element intended to appear suddenly is not simply "will this be noticed?" but "what will the user be doing when this appears, and what will noticing it cost them?" An interruption that is tolerable at one moment in a workflow can be deeply disruptive at another. Designing for attention means designing for context: understanding not just what you want the user to see, but when, and at what cost to whatever they were already doing within or outside the product. 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Identify every element in the interface that may draw attention due to its unique appearance, its movement, its sound, or anything that otherwise changes state without direct user initiation. For each, ask: where will the user's attention be when this occurs, and is what this element is communicating worth the cost of redirecting that attention? Also audit the interface as a whole: the cumulative effect of multiple competing attention-grabbing elements is often worse than any individual element suggests. 
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside: 
Information Overload. A Distraction draws a user’s attention away from a goal. Information Overload slows progress toward a goal by presenting too with an excess of information to process. The line between these Traps is blurry, but that’s ok because solving for them is similar: remove things that aren’t relevant to the user’s goal.
See also: 
Effectively Invisible Element. Motion is sometimes used as a remedy for Effectively Invisible Elements. When applied to elements that are not relevant to the user's current goal, the remedy becomes a Distraction Trap.
Related Concepts
The Orienting Response. First described by Pavlov, the orienting response is the automatic reorientation of attention toward a novel or changing stimulus. It is reflexive, rapid, and largely involuntary. Motion, sudden sounds, and unexpected appearances all trigger it reliably. In a designed interface, this mechanism is easily exploited, intentionally or not, by any element that changes state without user initiation.
Exogenous and Endogenous Attention. Attention researchers distinguish between two modes of attentional control. Endogenous attention is voluntary and goal-directed. Exogenous attention is involuntary and stimulus-driven. The Distraction Trap is, by definition, an exogenous attention event. This distinction clarifies why telling users to ignore something is not a solution. Exogenous attention cannot be suppressed by intention alone.
Attentional Resources Theory. The theoretical framework underlying Simon's epigraph at the outset of this section: attention is a limited cognitive resource and demands on it are additive. Every competing element consumes a portion of that resource. A single notification may be tolerable. A screen full of moving elements, competing sounds, and pop-up alerts can overwhelm the attentional system, leaving the user unable to focus effectively on anything.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, but only if the AI has access to accurate data on the users’ goals in using the product – what are they trying to do when the encounter a distraction?, what kinds of distractions might they welcome?, and the user’s goals in the world outside the product, from which the product may distract them.

About this Trap
The Uncomprehended Element Trap, and the one that follows it, Inviting Dead End, both deal with comprehension of interface elements, i.e., the ability of users to make sense of the things they encounter in an interface after they’ve noticed them. Because the two Traps are closely related, we introduce them together here. 
An Uncomprehended Element describes when a user notices an interface element but does not understand its meaning or how to interact with it; it leaves them scratching their head. An Inviting Dead End describes when a user notices an interface element and believes they understand what is being conveyed but are wrong. In both cases the user fails to accurately comprehend the meaning of some aspect of the interface. To avoid the first Trap, you must ensure the intended path forward communicates itself to the user in a way they understand. To avoid the second Trap, you need to ensure there are no unintended paths that look like they could be correct. The two Traps often work in concert, compounding each other's damage.
These Traps often show up as unfamiliar or misleading icons, labels, controls, physical affordances, text prompts, or audible cues. 
We’ll talk more about Inviting Dead Ends in the next section, where it gets its own dedicated treatment. For now, we’ll dig into the Uncomprehended Element Trap, starting with a real-world example.
Example: Waze
The following icon was once presented in the navigation app, Waze. Do you know what it means? Here are a few of the app’s functions to pick from: Current location, Search, Traffic, Offline maps, My trips. Do any of these look right?
 
Figure x – Can you guess what this icon means?
Now, take a look at this icon. Which of the functions listed above does this one represent? 
 
Figure x – How about this one?
Did you make your guesses? Was the meaning of one of the icons clearer to you than the other? Would it surprise you to learn that both icons represent the same thing? They do. They are both Search icons. The second one replaced the first one. Its not too hard to guess why. We’ve asked hundreds of people the same question we just asked you in our Tenets & Traps training sessions, and we’ve found that almost no one guesses that the first icon represents Search. Uncomprehended Elements seem like they should be easy to avoid – you just need to show your designs to people and ensure they understand them. But they’re still quite common. These are some of the reasons why:
Why does this Trap occur?
“The user will figure it out.” 
Bruce Tognazzini of Apple once observed that the people who work on products, PMs, developers, designers, skew toward being troubleshooters and problem solvers, a conclusion that is supported by research into “cognitive styles”. The tech field is literally made up of people who enjoy figuring things out. They will often dismiss concerns about the understandability of some aspect of the interface by saying, “The user will figure it out.” But the tendency to figure things out is much less common in the general population, where most people's primary motivation in using a product is to get something done. These people won't tolerate a design that makes them work harder than necessary. It’s not that these people aren’t capable of figuring things out. Rather, it’s that they choose not to. They move on to something better. It’s for this reason that the question to ask is not "will users figure it out?", but rather, "have we made sure they already know?
Over indexing on differentiation
The Waze example above is one where branding is brought into a functional element. This is not uncommon as you can see in the examples from Meta and Amazon included at the end of this section. Conveying brand is important in any product, but it should not be applied in a way that is detrimental to the clear communication of functionality. 
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Know thy user
Understanding the language your users speak, i.e., the labels, symbols, and conventions drawn from their prior experience, should be a primary focus from the earliest stages of design. The surest way to build this understanding is to work directly with real users. What they find familiar or opaque is rarely what designers predict, and the gaps surface quickly when you watch someone navigate without guidance.
When in doubt, provide labels
Jef Raskin’s definition of an icon: “A symbol equally confusing to people of all cultures”…
If you have something truly novel to convey, provide instructions
If you are introducing a genuinely new concept with no obvious existing convention to draw on, you will need a plan for teaching users what the element means. Tooltips, onboarding flows, and contextual help can all serve this purpose, provided they are delivered at a moment when the user is ready to receive them. For more on effective instruction, see Invisible Element Trap FAQ 2. (move to appendix?)
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Once you have a candidate element, verifying its comprehensibility is a trivial exercise: show it to a small number of potential users and ask them to describe what it means. That is essentially all it takes. Given how little effort this requires, it is remarkable how often icons and labels are introduced that users simply do not understand, only to be replaced later by more obvious alternatives. There is no excuse for this.

Why does this Trap occur?
Inviting Dead Ends occur when a team fails to systematically close off all the incorrect paths users might try. 
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
When selecting icons, labels, or other signifiers of functionality, ask yourself not only whether users are likely to be familiar with the design you are considering, but also whether users might judge it as a way of accomplishing some other function. 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Step off the golden path
Avoiding this Trap requires deliberately considering all the ways a user might attempt to reach their goal. Walk every plausible path through the interface, not just the intended one. At each decision point, ask: is there anything here that a user unfamiliar with the system might reasonably mistake for the correct next step? Pay particular attention to elements that are visually prominent, similarly labeled, or placed near the correct path. 
This Trap is particularly easy to miss during design and review because attention naturally concentrates on the intended path. Designers who know the system well know which elements to ignore, which means misleading elements on the wrong paths rarely receive the scrutiny they deserve. 
Test with humans
Users will never fail to surprise you with the things they imagine might be viable ways of doing things.

About this Trap
The preceding Traps have emphasized individual interface elements. Poor Grouping is focused on the relationships between elements, specifically, the failure of an interface to communicate relationships that are critical to users’ ability to achieve their goals. This Trap often shows up as unclear visual hierarchy, insufficient white space, poorly organized menu structures, etc. Also, while we don’t elaborate on it extensively here, it applies to conceptual organization of an information architecture. 
We impose structure on the world
[Example here of incomplete image]
Users do not consciously analyze spatial relationships in an interface. They perceive them automatically, through the same perceptual mechanisms that allow us to read a scene at a glance. The Gestalt psychologists identified the key principles governing this automatic perception in the early twentieth century. These principles — proximity, similarity, continuity, closure, and figure-ground — describe how the human visual system groups elements and infers relationships without deliberate thought. 
The stakes of failing to clarify the relationship between elements in an interface can be dramatic, as the following example illustrates. 
Example: The Butterfly Ballot
In the 2000 US presidential election, more than 4,000 voters in Palm Beach County, Florida punched the second hole on the butterfly ballot believing it represented the second candidate listed. The layout created a false spatial association between a candidate's name and the wrong punch hole. Over 19,000 additional voters punched more than one hole, suggesting they caught their error too late. A grouping problem — a failure of spatial relationship — altered the outcome of a presidential election.
[Image of butterfly ballot]
Failures in grouping are common in interfaces we encounter every day. And while the consequences may not be as serious as the example above, they can introduce friction to what should be frictionless experiences.

Why does this Trap occur?
Failure to consider the human tendency to impose structure, and how this might lead them to fail to see important relationships or to see connections that are not intended. And again, curse of knowledge. 
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Consider Gestalt principles
At every stage of design, ask yourself what relationships the spatial and visual arrangement communicate, and whether those relationships match the intended ones. 
Pay particular attention to your application of white space. It is one of the most effective tools available for creating separation between unrelated elements and proximity between related ones without adding visual weight.   
Test with humans
Ask someone unfamiliar with the interface to complete tasks that depend on recognition of effective grouping. You will often be surprised by the connections some users infer in a complex design.
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside: 
Uncomprehended Element. Poor Grouping can make individually comprehensible elements confusing in combination, by obscuring the relationships between them.
Inviting Dead End. Ambiguous spatial relationships between elements can make it unclear which control corresponds to which action or label, creating conditions where the wrong choice looks like the right one.
Information Overload. Visual clutter is both a cause and a symptom of Poor Grouping. When too many elements compete for attention, the spatial relationships between them become harder to read, compounding both Traps simultaneously.
Related Concepts
The Gestalt Principles. Gestalt psychology emerged in early twentieth century Germany from the work of Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, and Kurt Koffka. Their central insight was that perception is not the sum of individual sensations but a structured whole: the visual system actively organizes what it sees into meaningful patterns and groupings. The principles they identified describe the rules by which this organization occurs.
Proximity: elements that are close together are perceived as belonging together. This is the most powerful grouping principle and the one most commonly violated in interface design. A label positioned equidistant between two controls will be perceived as ambiguously related to both.
Similarity: elements that share visual properties — color, shape, size, typeface — are perceived as belonging to the same category. Consistent visual treatment signals membership in a group; inconsistent treatment signals distinction.
Continuity: elements arranged along a line or curve are perceived as related and belonging to a single path. Users follow implied lines automatically.
Closure: the visual system tends to perceive incomplete shapes as complete. Partially visible elements signal that more exists beyond the current view, which makes this principle useful for progressive disclosure.
Figure and Ground: the visual system automatically separates a scene into subject and background. Interactive elements should read as figure; supporting content as ground. When this relationship is unclear, users struggle to identify what they should be attending to.
These principles work together, sometimes reinforcing each other and sometimes in tension. A designer who understands them can create groupings that users perceive accurately and automatically. A designer who ignores them will create groupings that users misread — with consequences ranging from minor confusion to the kind of large-scale error the butterfly ballot produced.
Visual Hierarchy. Visual hierarchy describes the order in which the eye moves through a design. A well-designed hierarchy guides the user from the most important element to the least important in a sequence that serves their goal. Poor Grouping and poor visual hierarchy frequently co-occur: when spatial relationships are ambiguous, visual hierarchy tends to collapse, leaving the user without a clear path through the interface.

Potentially, but only if the AI has access to accurate data on what users have - and have not - already learned about the signifiers used to convey meaning within the interface, as well as the relationships between them, and the users’ goals – as their expectations will influence which relationships they attend to.

About this Trap
Forced Syntax occurs when an interface requires users to approach a task in a specific order that does not match the expectation or preference. Examples include systems that force users to specify objects before acting on it  (allowing “bedroom light > turn on”, but not “turn on > bedroom light”) or require users to ‘wake’ a system before they can use it (allowing “Siri, what’s today’s weather?” but not “What’s today’s weather, Siri?”). In each case, the task can be completed, but the user is forced to state things in a sequence imposed by the system. 
Why flexibility is important 
Just as different languages use different subject-verb-object grammatical constructions to convey the same idea (e.g., “I call mom” in English vs “I mom call” in Japanese) the same person can convey the same idea in different ways depending on their context. For example, a user who is reminded that there’s a person that they need to message, may start this task by pulling up that person in their phone’s contacts app before selecting the option that lets them send a message to that person. Alternatively, if they are reminded of an important message they need to communicate, they may go to the text messaging app, type out their message, and then specify the person it needs to go to. Which approach the user takes will vary depending on how they conceptualize the goal. This is true of almost any user goal. Accommodating this variability is a hallmark of good interface design.  
What does it look like when interfaces fail to provide this flexibility?
Example: Alexa Mobile App
[editorial note: to be added]
Why does this Trap occur?
A Forced Syntax Trap may arise when an interface is designed around the system's internal logic rather than the user's mental model. It also arises from a failure to consider that different users, or the same user at different times, will construct the same goal differently.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Understand all the different ways your users conceptualize the tasks your product supports and ensure they can complete tasks in ways that align to their mental models. Systematically plan which of your tasks should support object>action (e.g., "file > open") and/or action>object (e.g., "open > file") constructions. Providing both options allows users to approach tasks in the way that feels most natural to them, which can enhance user experience and improve task efficiency. [See Raskin, Johnson] 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Look for this Trap wherever the interface imposes a fixed order of operations: situations where one thing must be specified before another. Ask whether that order reflects a genuine technical constraint or simply the assumption that users will approach the task the same way the designer did.
Watch for these specific warning signs: workflows that require users to declare their intent before they have had a chance to explore; single rigid taxonomies that assume one right way to think about the system; and linear sequences imposed on tasks that are genuinely non-linear. 
Identify every point in a task flow where the interface requires the user to take a specific action before proceeding. For each, ask: is this order technically necessary, or is it an assumption about how users will approach the task? Then observe real users attempting the task without guidance. Where they try to start somewhere different from where the interface requires them to start, you have a Forced Syntax problem.
FAQ 1: Is providing multiple ways of doing the same thing always good? 
No. Flexible syntax is a specific and purposeful form of redundancy that accommodates users’ different ways of conceptualizing a task (i.e., object>action or action>object). It stands in contrast to Gratuitous Redundancy: a Trap where users are presented with multiple ways to complete the same task via the same grammatical construction (i.e., object>action, object>action). Flexible syntax is about offering different ways of doing the same thing. Gratuitous Redundancy does not accommodate different ways of conceptualizing a task. Rather, it provides duplicate paths for completing the same task in the same way (i.e., object> action and object>action), which results in more clutter and more decision points for the user. 
FAQ 2: Is it necessary to support every possible sequence users might follow to complete a task? 
No. Supporting every conceivable sequence for lengthy multi-step sequences would be neither practical nor useful. Only reasonably likely alternatives should be supported.
Related Traps 
Gratuitous Redundancy. See FAQ1 above.
Related Concepts
Object-Action and Action-Object Constructions. Users approach tasks through two primary grammatical structures. In object-action construction, the user selects what they want to work with first and then chooses what to do with it. In action-object construction, the user selects the operation first and then specifies what to apply it to. Neither is more natural in the abstract: which a user prefers depends on familiarity, task type, and context. An interface that supports only one construction forces users who think in the other to reorganize their approach before they can proceed.
Principle of Multiple Entry Points. Emerging from library science and formalized for digital information architecture by Rosenfeld and Morville in Information Architecture for the World Wide Web (1998), this principle recognizes that users bring different strategies to any system: known-item seeking, exploratory browsing, and exhaustive research. An interface built around a single entry point serves only one of these strategies reliably.
Action Identification Theory. Vallacher and Wegner's 1987 account of why the same person constructs the same goal differently depending on context: sometimes as a concrete action, sometimes as an abstract outcome. A user under time pressure thinks in immediate actions; a user in exploratory mode thinks in objects and goals. This is the theoretical foundation for why Forced Syntax is a Trap: the interface assumes a consistency in goal construction that human cognition does not provide
Skill Acquisition Stages (Fitts and Posner, 1967). Users move through cognitive, associative, and autonomous stages of skill acquisition, and at each stage they construct tasks differently. Novices tend to think in terms of objects and exploration; experts reach for direct actions. An interface that accommodates only one mode will frustrate users at every other stage.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, if the AI has access to knowledge of users’ prior experience and preferences for how to complete critical goals, and accurate data on the system’s object and action models (flows or code) that show the various ways users can complete critical goals.

About this Trap
An interface has a Memory Challenge Trap when it places an unreasonable demand on the user's ability to remember critical information. Examples include designs that require users to hold information in mind from one screen to the next, recall a password or command from long-term memory without any retrieval cue, or execute a multi-step process by memory alone. 
Human memory, both short-term and long-term, is limited in ways that have profound implications for interface design. Short-term memory can hold only a small amount of information and is extraordinarily volatile. We have all had the experience of walking into a room to retrieve something and forgetting what it was before we arrive. In interface design, even requiring a user to carry a small piece of information from one screen to the next may be asking too much.
[Example here]
The power of spatial memory
One thing meaningfully helps with short-term memory: spatial representation. Nearly half of the human cortex is dedicated to processing spatial information. We evolved the ability to navigate and map our environment long before we developed language, and this gives spatial representations a significant memory advantage over text-based ones. Consider how much less useful a mall directory would be if it replaced the floor map and "you are here" marker with a verbal description of the same information. The map works because it externalizes the spatial relationships the brain would otherwise have to hold in memory.
Supporting recognition over recall 
Recognition, matching something in front of you against a stored memory, is fast, reliable, and deeply evolved. Recall, searching long-term memory without any external template to match against, is slow, effortful, and error-prone. Multiple-choice tests are easier than open-ended ones for exactly this reason. This distinction drove one of the most consequential shifts in computing history: the move from command-line interfaces, which required users to memorize every possible action, to graphical user interfaces, which allowed users to recognize and select from visible options. Good interface design leverages recognition over recall wherever possible. 
[Voice – Transient, Schneiderman]
Why does this Trap occur?
Memory Challenge most often arises when designers underestimate the cognitive cost of memory tasks that feel trivial from the inside. A designer who knows the system well knows what to remember and when. The same tasks feel very different to a user encountering them for the first time, or returning after a long absence. Memory Challenge also arises from a failure to consider what happens when a user is interrupted mid-task, or when they are using the interface under time pressure or distraction.   
How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the first place
The overarching principle is to design for recognition rather than recall: let users see and choose rather than remember and enter. Present information spatially wherever possible, putting the brain's spatial processing machinery to work. When a task requires following a sequence of instructions, allow users to refer back to those instructions while executing them rather than requiring memorization. When users must produce information from memory, consider whether hints or cues can transform the task from recall to recognition. The guiding question throughout is simple: am I asking the user to remember this, or am I giving them a way to recognize it?
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Walk through every task flow and identify every point where the user is expected to hold information in mind, retrieve something from memory without a cue, or execute steps without reference material. For each, ask: what happens if the user forgets? Also look for any task that requires the user to carry information across a context boundary, such as from one screen to another, from one application to another, or from a previous session into the current one.
Related Traps
Not to be confused with: 
System Amnesia. Memory Challenge occurs when the interface requires the user to remember something that is easy to forget. System Amnesia occurs when the system fails to leverage information the users had previously provided, regardless of how easy that information is to remember.
Uncomprehended Element. An Uncomprehended Element involves something the user has never learned: the meaning is simply unknown. A Memory Challenge involves something the user has encountered before but struggles to retrieve. One is a gap in knowledge; the other is a gap in recall.
Often occurs alongside: 
Invisible Element. An Invisible Element that the user has been trained on but struggles to recall can also be categorized as a Memory Challenge.
Related Concepts
Recognition over Recall. The distinction between recognition and recall is covered in the Related Concepts section of the Invisible Element Trap and is the central principle underlying this Trap as well. Wherever an interface requires recall, ask whether a recognition-based alternative is possible.
Chunking. George Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" established that short-term memory can hold approximately seven items, but also showed that those items can be chunks of varying complexity. A chunk is a meaningful unit: a word rather than a letter, a phone number grouped as three-plus-four digits rather than seven individual ones. Organizing information into meaningful groups reduces the number of items the user must hold in mind even when the total amount of information remains the same.
Cognitive Load Theory. John Sweller's framework distinguishes between intrinsic load, the inherent complexity of the material, extraneous load, the load imposed by poor design, and germane load, the load associated with learning. Memory Challenge is a source of extraneous load: it adds cognitive demands that have nothing to do with the task itself. Reducing Memory Challenge frees cognitive resources for the work that actually matters.
Spatial Memory. Humans remember where things are with far less effort than they remember what things are called. This capacity is directly exploitable in interface design. Consistent element placement allows users to navigate by spatial memory rather than recall. Visual layouts that externalize relationships, like the mall directory map, reduce memory burden by making structure visible rather than requiring it to be held in mind.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, if the AI has an accurate understanding of the memory demands the interface places on the user, which may occur across screens, devices, and time.

About this Trap
The Feedback Failure Trap is a little different than other Traps. It is not defined by a single mechanism but by a moment in time: what happens after the user acts. Any failure that leaves a user without a clear understanding of what their action accomplished, or how to recover if something went wrong, qualifies. These failures are other Traps: feedback that is not noticed (Effectively Invisible Element), not comprehended (Uncomprehended Element), or too slow in arriving (Slow or No Response), etc. Its purpose is to ensure that evaluators pay special attention to whether and how a system closes the loop on users’ actions because feedback is foundational to how people learn. It therefore represents an additional lens through which the user experience should be assessed.
The importance of feedback
A touch interface that responds to exploratory gestures with immediate visual and motion feedback can effectively teach its own grammar without a single word of instruction.
As interfaces have evolved, feedback has shifted from discrete and transactional to continuous and real-time: flagging a password as too weak before the user has finished typing, confirming a valid email address the moment the format is recognized. Continuous feedback is more natural, more informative, and more forgiving than its discrete predecessor. 
Why does this Trap occur?
Understandably, the efforts of most teams is channeled toward the design of intended paths. Design of feedback systems can often suffer as a result. 
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Throughout the design process, ensure the system provides clear, timely, accurate, and consistent feedback in response to user acctions.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Walk every task flow and, at each point where the user takes an action, ask: what does the system communicate in response? Is that communication immediate, clear, and sufficient? Does it communicate if and when something goes wrong and how to recover? Also note the absence of feedback: moments where the system is silent after a user action are candidates for this Trap even when no error has occurred.
Some areas have a persistent tendency to fall short and deserve particular scrutiny. Error messages frequently tell users that something went wrong without explaining what action to take. Identifying these does not require testing – just an audit of messages. 
Because Feedback Failure is a result of other Traps, it does not have a single origin. The Related Traps section below identifies the specific mechanism that may contribute to it. Identifying which mechanism is at work is the key to knowing how to fix it.   
Related Traps
Feedback Failure is distinct from other Traps in that it describes a particular moment in the user experience: the period immediately after the user has acted. When feedback fails, there is almost always another Trap that explains why. The Traps below provide the more specific diagnosis and the path to a fix. [see footnote below]
Often occurs alongside: 
Invisible Element. The feedback exists nowhere in the interface. The user acts and the system does not respond.
Effectively Invisible Element. The feedback is present and timely, but the user misses it because it appears somewhere they were not attending or in a form they were not expecting.
Uncomprehended Element. The feedback is present, timely, and noticed, but the user does not understand what it means.
Physical Challenge. Feedback is present but effortful to discern: text too small to read comfortably, audio too quiet to hear in a noisy environment.
Slow or No Response. Feedback exists but arrives too late to be useful. 
Incorrect Information. Progress feedback, playspace boundaries, or other information communicated to the user, is wrong or falsely precise.
Variable Outcome. Inconsistent responses to the same action prevent users from forming reliable expectations about what feedback to look for.
See also: 
Irreversible Action. Recovery feedback is only useful when recovery is possible. When an action cannot be undone, even clear feedback about what went wrong cannot compensate for the absence of a path back. 

Related Concepts
Feedback Loops in Learning. Feedback is one of the primary mechanisms through which humans acquire skills. Research on skill acquisition consistently shows that immediate, specific feedback accelerates learning, while delayed or ambiguous feedback slows it. In interface design, feedback serves the same function: it tells the user whether their mental model of the system is correct and, if not, how to update it. An interface that provides good feedback teaches itself. One that provides poor feedback leaves users dependent on external documentation or trial and error.
Norman's Action Cycle. Donald Norman's model of human action describes a seven-stage cycle: forming a goal, planning an action, executing it, perceiving the system's response, interpreting that response, evaluating it against the goal, and forming the next intention. Feedback Failure disrupts this cycle at the perception and interpretation stages. When the system provides no feedback, the user cannot perceive a response. When it provides ambiguous feedback, the user cannot interpret it. In both cases the cycle stalls, and the user must either guess or abandon the task.
Visibility of System Status. Nielsen's first usability heuristic holds that systems should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within reasonable time. This heuristic covers responsive feedback after user actions, which is the primary focus of this Trap. The proactive dimension of the heuristic, surfacing status information users need to make decisions independently of any triggering action, is handled in this framework by the Invisible Element and Effectively Invisible Element Traps.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially, for digital interfaces, if the AI has comprehensive information on how the system responds to user actions, including how quickly it responds, whether feedback is noticeable and comprehensible, etc. Not for physical interfaces where physical feedback is critical to task completion.

U1. Disconfirmation: 
NOT present when: (a) the action's consequence is self-evident from the resulting interface state and no additional feedback is needed; (b) the absence of feedback is itself the meaningful signal — silence confirming no error; (c) feedback exists but is not reaching the user due to another Trap — in that case, Feedback Failure is the symptom and the root cause Trap (Effectively Invisible Element, Uncomprehended Element, etc.) is what requires fixing.

U2. Severity:
Part A — Consequence: Ranges from confusion and repeated attempts (minor) to inability to recover from an error (task failure) to compounding an irreversible action with no recovery guidance (significant harm) to potential injury due to absence of feedback regarding safety threats (severe harm).

Part B — Likelihood: High when: the action has an ambiguous or non-obvious outcome; the interface includes error states with no recovery guidance; physical controls have been replaced with touch surfaces that provide no tactile confirmation. Low when: the resulting state change is visually obvious and self-explanatory.

Part C — Combination rule: High severity when consequence is task failure or worse, regardless of likelihood.

U3. Confidence tier:
Tier 1 for error messages — auditable from design files: do they answer "what happened?" and "what should I do?" 
Tier 2 for noticeability and comprehensibility — whether feedback is seen and understood requires user knowledge. 
Tier 1 for actions that produce no noticeable system response — detectable by auditing action→response pairs.

U4. Root cause confirmation:
Identify which root cause is at work before flagging Feedback Failure: (1) no feedback element exists → Invisible Element; (2) feedback present but away from attentional focus → Effectively Invisible Element; (3) feedback present and noticed but unclear → Uncomprehended Element; (4) feedback present but physically hard to perceive → Physical Challenge; (5) feedback delayed → Slow or No Response; (6) feedback factually wrong → Incorrect Information. The root cause determines the fix — do not flag Feedback Failure without identifying which of these is causing it. 

U6. Report language:
Finding: When users take [action], the system fails to communicate [what happened / what to do next] in a way that is [noticeable / comprehensible / actionable].

Why it matters: Without clear feedback, users cannot confirm their action succeeded, recover from errors, or learn how the system responds to their input.

Confidence: [Tier 1: Confirmed for absent or non-actionable error messages / Tier 2: Flagged — confirm feedback visibility with user observation]

U7. Remediation direction:
Every action in the interface should produce a response that is immediate, clear, and sufficient. Error messages must answer two questions: what went wrong, and what should the user do now? Continuous real-time feedback (flagging errors as they occur) is superior to post-submission feedback. The fix depends entirely on the root cause — identify which Trap is causing the feedback failure before designing a solution.

About this Trap
PHYSICAL CHALLENGE Traps arise when a product is physically difficult or uncomfortable to use. It can manifest in many ways. Example may include touch targets that too small to hit reliably, text that is too faint to read without strain, controls that are placed where they can’t be comfortably reached, device forms that are too heavy or sharp-edged to hold for extended periods, audio prompts that are too quiet to hear clearly, surfaces that are too hot to touch, or VR headset video that is too jittery to watch without experiencing queasiness. In each case, the user understands what they need to do, but doing it means experiencing strain, discomfort, or worse, illness or injury.
Below, we dig into two design topics that have important consequences for physical comfort that are applicable to a broad range of products: targeting accuracy and text legibility.
Targeting accuracy: Bigger, closer buttons are easier to target
[“Missed it by one pixel” or “Tog’s Apple lawsuit” story here?]
Many interfaces require users to physically acquire a target: press a button, tap an icon, click a link. The science of target acquisition is captured by Fitts' Law. First proposed in the 1950s by Paul Morris Fitts, it is and one of the most replicated findings in human factors research. In simple terms, Fitts' Law states that the time required to acquire a target is a function of its size and its distance from the user's starting point. Larger, closer targets are acquired faster. Targets that allow overshooting without penalty, such as controls placed at screen corners on a bounded display, are effectively larger than their physical dimensions suggest.
 
Figure x – It’s the law: People target buttons faster and more accurately when they are closer and bigger.

The practical implications are not always obvious. Human finger pads are approximately 12mm across on average. Any touch target substantially smaller than this will produce targeting errors regardless of user skill or motivation. As mobile phone screens have grown larger, the physical reach required to access controls at the top of the screen has exceeded what many users can comfortably achieve with one hand, driving the introduction of reachability features that bring distant controls closer to the thumb. These are engineering workarounds for a Physical Challenge Trap that could have been avoided by keeping critical controls within comfortable reach in the first place.
Regarding reach on mobile devices, it’s important to note that the math that describes Fitts’ law assumes a user is unconstrained in their ability to reach a target. In reality, given the position of our hands or fingers when using a product, ergonomic constraints may make targets more or less difficult to reach. This is well illustrated in the mobile phone thumb-reach data collected by Steve Hoobler and visualized by Scott Hurff:

Text legibility: Easy to read text directly drives engagement
When Zillow changed the design of their property summary views they experienced a big jump in engagement. Based on the before and after images in figure X, can you tell why? 
 
Figure x – Zillow’s shift to high contrast text in its listing summaries led to a jump in engagement.
The change Zillow introduced was increasing the legibility of property detail text. This example shows that text legibility isn’t simply about increasing accessibility. It directly affects how people use products.
An interface is legible when users can read its text quickly and without strain. The factors most within a designer's control are font choice, font size relative to screen resolution, and the contrast between text and background. Contrast is particularly consequential: insufficient contrast between text and background is one of the most common legibility failures and one of the most easily avoided. 
If it’s easy to read it’s easy to do
In research conducted by Song and Schwarz’s, participants were asked to read a set of identical instructions for completing a physical exercise routine presented in one of two fonts. One of the fonts was a simpler design and easier to read. Participants were then asked how easy the font was to read and how easy it would be to complete the task described in the text. The participants presented with the simpler font judged it to be easier to read, which was no surprise. But they also estimates that the exercise routine would be more difficult to complete, and that they’d be less likely to stick with it. 
 
Figure x – People estimated an exercise to be much more difficult if the text describing it was difficult to read. If the text in your product is hard to read, users are likely to think your overall product will require more effort – a known predictor of disengagement. Song and Schwarz, 2008.
Participants clearly conflated the difficulty of reading text with the difficulty of executing the behavior the text describes. This has important implications for product design. Other research [Novemsky and colleagues (2007) presented the same information about two cordless phones in easy- or difficult-to-read fonts. They observed that 17 per cent of their participants postponed choice when the font was easy to read, whereas 41 per cent did so when the font was difficult to read. Apparently, participants misread the difficulty arising from the print font as reflecting the difficulty of making a choice.] [people are sensitive to their feelings of ease or difficulty, but insensitive to where these feelings come from] shows that the more effortful we estimate an activity to be, the less likely we are to engage in it. Together, these findings have important implications for product design. It suggests that poor text legibility in a product will be judged negatively, but that it will also lead users to judge the product as being more complex overall, and this judgement will reduce the likelihood that they use the product.

Other contributors to Physical Challenge
Targeting accuracy and text legibility are two of the most common contributors to Physical Challenge Traps, but they are far from the only ones. Appendix X includes guidelines and references covering general accessibility, control-to-display ratios, vestibular discomfort in VR and AR systems, touchable surface temperatures, and contributors to the perceived quality of handheld and wearable devices.  
Why does this Trap occur?
Physical Challenge Traps often arise when readily available ergonomic and accessibility standards are not referenced or adhered to, or when designing and testing takes places under conditions that do not reflect real-world use. For example, designers of mobile devices with good eyesight, working on high-resolution monitors, in evenly-lit environments, with both hands free, may fail to identify issues that a high-fidelity prototype would immediately reveal.   
How to avoid it 
Preventing it in the first place
Follow guidelines and standards. [Links]
Iteratively design and prototype on real hardware in realistic conditions, not on design mockups or simulators. 
For touch interfaces, ensure that all interactive targets meet minimum size guidelines and are reachable with one hand in the intended grip. 
For text, ensure contrast ratios against established accessibility standards and test legibility in the lighting conditions where the product will actually be used. 
For audio interfaces, anticipate acoustical characteristics of real world environments. 
Finding and fixing it in existing products
For mobile and wearable devices, Physical Challenge is one of the hardest Traps to detect in the early phases of development because it requires physical interaction with real hardware. Early iterative prototyping is critical.  When reviewing designs, systematically ask: what does this require of the user physically? Is every touch target large enough and close enough to reach reliably? Is every text element legible at the expected viewing distance and in the expected lighting? Is every audio element audible in the expected acoustic environment? Then verify these questions with real devices and real users rather than relying on the answers that look right on screen. 
Related Traps  
Often occurs alongside: 
Feedback Failure. An absence of effective feedback hinders users in developing physical master of a product. For example, the absence of home-row tactile indicators on a qwerty keyboard hinders the development of touch typing skills. 
Accidental Activation. Physical Challenge makes intended actions too difficult. Accidental Activation makes unintended actions too easy. They pull in opposite directions, and the design changes that reduce one can worsen the other.
Related Concepts  
Accessibility and Universal Design. Physical Challenge is the Trap most directly connected to accessibility. Many of the guidelines established by accessibility research, minimum touch target sizes, contrast ratio requirements, minimum font sizes, caption requirements for audio content, exist precisely because the population of users includes people whose physical capabilities differ significantly from the assumed norm. Designing to accessibility standards is not a separate exercise from designing for comfort: it is designing for the full range of human physical variation.
Weber-Fechner Law and Just Noticeable Differences. Formulated in the nineteenth century by Ernst Weber and Gustav Fechner, this law describes the relationship between the physical magnitude of a stimulus and its perceived intensity. Perception is logarithmic, not linear: each additional unit of physical change produces a smaller perceived difference as the baseline intensity increases. One practical implication relevant to legibility is that contrast improvements at the low end of the scale, where most legibility failures occur, produce the greatest perceptual return per unit of change. In plain terms: if your text contrast is already poor, even a modest improvement will be visible to users; if it is already good, the same improvement will go largely unnoticed.
Processing Fluency. Research by Song and Schwarz has shown that the ease with which information can be processed affects how people feel about it. Instructions printed in a difficult-to-read font are judged as more complex and time-consuming than the same instructions in a legible one. Products that are physically easy to interact with feel more capable and trustworthy than those that are not. The implication for interface design is significant: PHYSICAL CHALLENGE does not merely make tasks harder. It makes the product itself feel worse. Legibility and ease of physical interaction are not just usability considerations. They shape the user's fundamental perception of the product's quality.

Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Beyond some gross issues detectable in digital files, e.g., clearly undersized targets, reach issues, low contrast text, detection by an AI is difficult. Accurate data is required on the physical context in which the product will be used, including lighting conditions, device hardware, and the physical capabilities and limitations of the target user population. Many Physical Challenge Traps are invisible in static design files and only emerge through testing on real hardware in real environments.

Part C — Combination rule: High severity when consequence is task failure or worse, regardless of likelihood.

About this Trap
Accidental Activation is the mirror image of Physical Challenge. Where Physical Challenge makes intended actions too difficult, Accidental Activation makes unintended actions too easy. The Trap occurs when the physical properties of a control, its size, sensitivity, placement, or the gesture required to activate it, make it vulnerable to unintended triggering. Examples include buttons located where a user grips or otherwise touches the device when doing other things, buttons with overloaded actions (e.g., double taps, press & holds), etc.
An example: iOS Screen Cap
Accidental screenshots have been a persistent problem on iPhone since Apple replaced the home button with a side button, inadvertently creating a screenshot gesture that sits directly at the phone's natural grip points. Apple's attempts to mitigate the problem through software workarounds have left the underlying cause untouched. With the introduction of the Camera Control button on the iPhone 16, placed in the same grip zone, Apple added an analogous accidental camera launch problem alongside the original.

The other side of Physical Challenge
The relationship between this Trap and Physical Challenge is a source of tension. Making targets larger reduces Physical Challenge but increases the risk of Accidental Activation. Increasing touch sensitivity makes interactions more responsive but makes unintended activations more likely. Making gesture thresholds lower improves accessibility but makes incidental gestures more consequential. There is no universal resolution to this tension. The right balance depends on the context, the stakes of an accidental activation, and whether the action is reversible (see the Forgiving Tenet).
Why does this Trap occur?
Accidental Activation Traps often arise from optimizing the ease of access to a particular control without considering the full range of physical interactions a user will have with a device during normal use. It also arises from physical form factors that place controls near the natural grip points of a device, and from voice and gesture interfaces whose activation vocabulary overlaps with ordinary human behavior.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
The standard approach involves adding friction to the activation path: recessing or shielding controls, requiring a sequential action before a critical function executes, adding physical resistance, or requiring explicit confirmation for consequential and irreversible actions. These techniques are effective but carry a cost. They slow intentional activation and can introduce Unnecessary Steps. The tradeoff must be calibrated to context and stakes.
Finding and fixing it in existing products
Walk through every interaction in the interface and ask: what other physical actions, performed in the course of normal device use, could trigger this? Evaluate how the device is typically held, carried, and stored. Create test conditions that push the range of actions the user might engage in when using the product. Where accidental activation cannot be prevented through physical design alone, recessing controls, adjusting sensitivity thresholds, or redesigning gesture vocabulary, consider adding a confirmation step. But treat confirmation as a last resort. Every confirmation step adds friction for the user who acted intentionally, which is most users, most of the time. Reserve them for actions that are both consequential and irreversible, and only after the physical design options have been exhausted. 
Related Traps 
Not to be confused with: 
Inviting Dead End. An Inviting Dead End lures the user in. With Accidental Activation the risk goes unnoticed: the interface communicates nothing about it, and the user triggers an unintended action as a consequence. One Trap misleads; the other fails to prevent.
Physical Challenge. Accidental Activation and Physical Challenge are opposite failure modes. The changes that reduce one frequently worsen the other. Both must be considered together.
Often caused by: 
Bad Prediction. In many cases, Accidental Activations occur when a system misreads a user's intent. Voice and gesture interfaces are particularly prone to this: a wake word detected in background conversation, a gesture triggered by incidental movement. In these cases the system makes a prediction about what the user wants, acts on it, and gets it wrong. The Accidental Activation is the symptom. The bad prediction is the cause. This stands in contrast to the physical examples earlier in this section, where no interpretation of intent is involved. A button pressed accidentally is simply a button pressed. The system responds to the physical input without any attempt to judge whether the user meant it. 
Variable Outcome. Interfaces where the same physical action produces different outcomes depending on system state are particularly prone to Accidental Activation, because users cannot reliably predict what a given action will do. But lack of consistency is the root cause of the problem.
Related Concepts  
Fitts' Law. The same model that governs target acquisition time also governs accidental activation risk. Larger targets are acquired faster by users who want them, and more easily triggered by users who do not. Targets placed at screen edges are effectively larger due to the inability to overshoot them, which makes them efficient for intentional use but risky if positioned near areas the hand naturally rests. Understanding Fitts' Law as a two-sided principle, optimizing both for acquisition and against accidental triggering, is essential for any interface that involves physical controls.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Detection by an AI is difficult. Detections of some gross problems may be possible if the AI has sufficient information on grip points, target sizes, target spacing, etc., as well as accurate data on the physical context in which the product will be used, including lighting conditions, device hardware, and the physical capabilities and limitations of the target user population. Even with this, many Accidental Activation Traps are invisible in static design files and only emerge through testing on real hardware in real environments.

An interface is Responsive when it keeps pace with the user, allowing them to maintain a sustained sense of control.
Of all nine Tenets, Responsive may have the strongest claim to universality. Most Tenets involve trade-offs: what makes an interface more Understandable for one user may reduce it for another depending on their prior experience; what makes an interface more Efficient for an expert user may overwhelm a novice. Responsiveness does not work this way. In most contexts, no user prefers to wait. There are exceptions, for example in gaming entertainment experiences, where a well-timed pause or a countdown before a reveal can build anticipation. But the default assumption, that users want to accomplish their goals without unnecessary delay, holds across the overwhelming majority of interface contexts and is what this Tenet addresses.
Two Traps undermine responsiveness: SLOW OR NO RESPONSE and CAPTIVE WAIT. Both rob the user of the sense of control that defines a responsive experience. But they do so for different reasons and require different remedies.
As with our previous Tenet, Comfortable, there is a large body of work on this topic. What follows is a high-level framing of the key ideas related to responsiveness in system design with pointers to rich source material. 
 

 
“Time is not just what clocks measure.”
– Evan Thompson, on Henri Bergson

About this Trap
SLOW OR NO RESPONSE Traps arise from both objectively-measurable delays in system response and design decisions that impact the subjective experience of waiting. Examples include slow responses to various types of input, variable response times for the same action, long startup or shutdown times, etc.
We know what ‘fast enough’ is for many experiences
Different types of signals move through your brain at different rates. This explains, in part, what you experience when you look at the illusion on the facing page. High-contrast and low-contrast signals are processed by your visual system at slightly different rates. Your brain interprets the difference as evidence of motion.
The science of responsiveness as its applied to user interface design is built on a small number of critical timing thresholds, established through decades of psychophysical research. These thresholds reflect the underlying architecture of human perception and the time it takes signals to move through the nervous system.
For continuous actions, such as writing with electronic ink or tracking a physical environment through an augmented or extended reality headset, near-zero latency is needed (0-10 ms). If visual takes longer than this to arrive in response to a user’s actions, the user will perceive the delay. In the case of AR/XR, delays of this duration may induce motion sickness. 
 
Figure x – The different rates at which your brain processes different visual signals contributes to this illusion created by Akiyoshi Kitaoka.
For discrete user actions, such as key presses, button taps, scroll responses, a response within 100 milliseconds feels instantaneous. The user perceives no delay between action and outcome. Beyond that, delays become perceptible, and at more than one second, disruptive. Beyond ten seconds, attention wanders: the user may abandon the task, navigate away, or conclude that something has gone wrong. These thresholds describe perception, not preference. Users do not choose to find delays disruptive at one second. It is a function of how human time perception works. 
For conversational exchanges, such as those between a user and a voice assistant, like Alexa or ChatGPT, responses should arrive within a maximum of 1 second. If delays are longer than this, users will start to grow frustrated and attribute things to the system, such as: it’s not paying attention, it’s unintelligent, it’s rude, etc. For an ideal conversational experience, responses should be even faster than this. Gaps in human-to-human conversational exchanges average about 250 ms. If the conversations is especially good, utterances may even overlap as the two individuals get in sync and anticipate one another’s’ statements.

Thoughtful design can make time pass faster
While certain responses must fall within objective thresholds to be judged as fast enough, human time perception remains highly subjective. Beyond the aforementioned thresholds, objectively measured responses can feel much shorter or longer depending on how an interface is designed. Steve Seow's Designing and Engineering Time is the definitive treatment of this subject, and the title says it all: Time perception can be managed in ways that dramatically influence how responsive a product feels, independent of how fast it actually is.

Technics for increasing perceived responsiveness include: 
Occupied Time. Research on waiting behavior has consistently found that waits feel shorter when the user has something to do. Unoccupied waiting time is perceived as roughly 1.4 to 1.8 times longer than the same duration spent on a task, a finding replicated across service contexts and applied to interface design. Disney's queue design is the most cited real-world application: entertainment, visual variety, and the concealment of remaining queue length systematically reduce perceived wait time. The same principle applies to digital interfaces through skeleton screens, progressive loading, and background activity that allows users to continue working. This distinction matters because optimizing perceived speed and optimizing actual speed require different interventions.
Pre-fetching. Starting a process before the user explicitly initiates it shortens the perceived duration because users mark time from the moment they act, not from when the system began working.

Early completion. … 
The Peak-End Rule. Daniel Kahneman and colleagues established that people do not evaluate experiences by averaging their moment-to-moment quality. Instead, memory of an experience is dominated by two points: its most intense moment, the peak, and its final moment, the end. Duration itself has surprisingly little effect on remembered quality, a phenomenon Kahneman called duration neglect. For interface designers, the implication is direct: a slow process that ends quickly and cleanly will be remembered more favorably than one that starts quickly and bogs down at the end. Progress indicators that accelerate toward completion exploit this, as do systems that deliver a satisfying final confirmation even after a lengthy wait.

Why does this Trap occur?
Slow or No Response most often arises from engineering constraints: complex queries, heavy computations, network latency, or resource-constrained devices. It also arises from a failure to prioritize the response to user actions above other system processes, and from a failure to fully consider opporun. 
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
For perceived responsiveness, apply the principle of occupied time: give users something to engage with while they wait. Progress bars, skeleton screens, and animated loading states all reduce perceived wait time. Make progress visible and ensure it appears to be moving continuously rather than jumping in discrete increments. 
[editorial note: more to be added]
Finding and fixing it in existing products
Measure response times against established thresholds for every significant user interaction. For interactions that cannot meet the 100ms threshold, provide immediate confirmation that the action was received before the full response is ready. For processes that take longer than one second, provide continuous progress feedback that updates every two to five seconds. Never leave the user facing a static screen with no indication that the system is working.
Measure. Response times are objective and measurable, and this Trap is one of the most amenable to automated detection. Instrument every significant user interaction and compare measured response times against established thresholds. Also observe users: moments where users repeat an action, look away from the screen, or verbalize frustration are reliable signals of perceived slowness even when actual response times are within acceptable bounds.

FAQ 1: Can a system respond too quickly?
Yes. This occurs whenever a user needs more time than the system allows to process information or execute a response. Per Steve Seow, an example of where a user may need more processing time is  animated window transitions: removing the animation makes the system faster, but users lose the visual cue that tells them what happened to the window. A small deliberate delay preserves comprehension at negligible cost to performance. An example of where a user needs more time to execute a process is Windows double-click timing; if the threshold is too tight, users with less precise motor control may accidentally rename icons instead of opening applications. 
We do not have a dedicated ‘too fast’ Trap as these types of failures can be captured by the Physical Challenge Trap.
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside: 
Feedback Failure. When response times cannot be improved, it is important to provide the user with clear and accurate progress feedback.  
See also: 
Physical Challenge. Touch, gesture, and voice interfaces that respond too quickly can outpace the user's ability to respond, producing errors that a slightly slower, more deliberate system would have avoided.
Related Concepts 
Weber-Fechner Law Applied to Response Time. Weber's Law states that the minimum change required to perceive a difference in stimuli, aka the just noticeable difference (JND), such as weight, brightness, or sound, is a constant proportion of the original stimulus. The 20% rule is a practical application of this law, suggesting that to create a noticeable improvement in user experience (like website loading speed or performance), the change must be at least 20% of the current duration. A practical implication of this law: to make a response feel faster, it must be improved by at least 20%. At the same time, a series of individual imperceptible, sub-20% slowdowns can accumulate into a problem that is large enough to matter. 
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially. An AI can assess responsiveness if it has access to actual system response times measurable against established thresholds. Perceived slowness is harder to detect — a system may perform within acceptable bounds yet still feel slow due to poor progress feedback or unoccupied wait time. Given detailed design information, an AI could evaluate the presence and quality of progress feedback, but would still require data on users' response time expectations, which significantly shape their subjective experience of waiting.

About this Trap
A CAPTIVE WAIT Trap occurs when the system prevents the user from moving forward. It may be driven by technical constraints, but often it is a business or design-driven choice. Example include un-skippable ‘pre-roll’ ads ahead of videos, un-skippable cutscenes in videogames, install or update procedures that take over a device, etc.
Coined by Steve Seow, Captive Wait describes any process that holds the user hostage. He offers the example is a self-cleaning oven that locks itself during the cleaning cycle. If you suddenly remember you urgently need the oven after the cycle has started, every passing second without it completing is frustrating, particularly if there is no indication of when it will end. Digital interfaces present the same problem. When users start a task, they have a sequence of steps in mind. A mandatory update or an un-skippable ad commandeers the system and brings that flow to a halt. 
Why does this Trap occur?
Captive Wait arises from two sources. The first is organizational: business models that depend on forced exposure to content the user did not request. The second is design: mandatory flows imposed to ensure users receive information or complete setup steps that the team considers essential, without considering whether the user's time and autonomy are worth more than the certainty that the information was delivered.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
When designing a flow, question every point where the user will be unable to advance, back out, or skip. Ask: is this barrier necessary? If the information or content is important enough to mandate, can it be delivered in a way that respects the user's time? In most cases the best alternative is progressive disclosure: surface the critical information at the moment it is needed, rather than forcing the user through it at a time of the system's choosing. For processes that unavoidably require the system to be unavailable, give users advance notice so they can choose when to initiate them, allow them to attend to other tasks wherever possible, and notify them when the process is complete. Any process that takes more than ten seconds should include a way for users to either stop it or continue working while it runs in the background. Teams should be clear-eyed about the trade-off they are making when they impose captive waits.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Walk every task flow and identify every point where the user cannot choose to advance, back out, or skip. Pay particular attention to onboarding flows, setup sequences, advertising placements, and any screen that auto-advances or auto-dismisses without user control. For each instance found, ask whether the barrier can be removed entirely, made skippable, or at minimum shortened and accompanied by clear communication about its duration and purpose.
Related Traps 
See also:
Feedback Failure. A captive wait that provides no information about its duration or purpose compounds the experience of being held against one's will. Clear communication about why the wait is necessary and how long it will last does not eliminate the Trap but meaningfully reduces its severity.
Related Concepts 
Autonomy and Perceived Control. Research in both psychology and HCI consistently shows that perceived control over an experience significantly affects how that experience is rated, independent of its objective properties. Users who can skip an ad report better experiences than users who cannot, even when the ad duration is identical. Users who can exit a setup flow and return to it later report less frustration than those who must complete it in one sitting. Captive Wait violates perceived control directly and deliberately, which is why it generates disproportionate user frustration relative to its actual time cost.

An interface is EFFICIENT when it minimizes the number of steps a user must take and the amount of information they must parse. It achieves this by streamlining task flows, reducing what is presented to the user at any given moment, leveraging the user’s prior work, and anticipating their actions. 
Of all nine Tenets, EFFICIENT may be the one where the opportunity for improvement is most open-ended. Absolute efficiency is a practically unreachable goal, which makes it fertile territory for continuous innovation. Many of the most significant advances in interface design have involved efficiency improvements. Search and modern LLMs are clear examples. They increase Efficiency by allowing users to cut through hierarchy, surfacing relevant results, and remembering prior exchanges.

Three Traps undermine EFFICIENCY: UNNECESSARY STEP(S), INFORMATION OVERLOAD, and SYSTEM AMNESIA. Each captures a distinct way interfaces make users do more work than they need to.

About this Trap
Every step a user must take to accomplish a goal is a cost. Some of those costs are justified. Many are not. UNNECESSARY STEP(S) describe actions that could be eliminated, automated, or combined without any loss to the quality of the experience. Examples include multi-step workflows for simple tasks, nested architectures in lieu of ‘flat’ ones, and confirmation requests (in lieu of reversible actions).
Fewer steps aren’t always better
Knowing when to remove a step requires judgment. A wizard-style setup flow that guides a first-time user through ten sequential steps may provide a better experience than a single dense screen that presents all ten decisions at once: the cognitive cost of processing everything simultaneously may exceed the cost of the additional steps. Additional steps that make an experience more UNDERSTANDABLE are often a well-reasoned trade-off. The default, all else being equal, should be toward fewer steps. But the goal is the right number of steps, not the minimum number.
Why does this Trap occur? 
Unnecessary steps most often arise from three sources. The first is caution: designers add confirmation steps, required fields, and mandatory flows to prevent errors rather than allowing recovery from them. The second is organizational: steps are added by different teams at different times, each making locally reasonable decisions without auditing the cumulative effect on the overall flow. The third is technical: backend constraints or legacy systems impose steps on users that exist for engineering reasons rather than user reasons.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Two areas deserve particular attention in the design of digital interfaces: navigational hierarchy and confirmation dialogs.
Navigational hierarchy. Nested menu options are a common source of Unnecessary Steps. Every level of navigation a user must descend to reach their goal is a step that could potentially be eliminated by flattening the structure. An example this is the elimination of the  “hamburger menu” by some apps, including Spotify. They moved several of their most frequently accessed functions from under a hamburger menu to an always present nav bar, reporting significant gains in engagement for these functions as a result.  (We should note that this change actually addressed several Traps simultaneously: the nav bar options were made more visible and easier to reach, in addition to being reachable in one less step.)

About this Trap
INFORMATION OVERLOAD Traps arise when the amount of information the user is required to take in is greater than is necessary. Examples include verbose on-screen instructions, wordy responses from AI assistants, and visually cluttered displays.
The importance of brevity
Rule 17 in Strunk and White's The Elements of Style is "omit needless words." William Strunk considered it the most important rule for effective communication. Steve Krug later echoed this rule as his third law of usability: "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left." In our experience the guidance is right on – aggressive cutting is not only possible but invariably yields better, clearer communication. Of course, doing this cutting requires deep understanding of what users need to see. 
 
Figure X – First cited by Jeff Johnson in his book Designing with the Mind in Mind, the evolution of Jeep’s dealer search function illustrates the extent to which text can be reduced without a loss in clarity. 
The cost of more choices 
Hick's Law, formulated by William Edmund Hick in 1952 and refined by Ray Hyman, states that the time required to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number of choices available. Each doubling of the choice set adds a roughly constant increment of decision time. The practical implication: reducing the number of options presented at any decision point reduces the cognitive cost of choosing. Hick's Law is relevant to any design where users are selecting from alternatives, such as menu and navigation structures. It is the quantitative foundation for the intuition that less is more.
In each case, the solution is not to hide information but to make more deliberate choices about what earns a place on screen. Prioritization is the discipline that prevents this Trap. 
 
Figure X – Over time, Amazon substantially reduced the number of options presented to users under the Accounts & Lists tab. 
Why does this Trap occur?
INFORMATION OVERLOAD often arises from an unwillingness to make editorial choices. Adding detail feels safe, ensuring users will have all the information they’ll need. This is particularly acute in organizational contexts where different stakeholders advocate for the visibility of different features or content, and where the cumulative effect of many individually reasonable additions is an interface that serves no one well. It also arises from a failure to audit: interfaces accumulate content over time, and what was a reasonable amount at launch may become overwhelming as the product grows.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Start from the user's most likely goal in each context and build outward from there, adding only what directly serves that goal. Treat every additional element as something that must earn its place rather than something that can be included by default. Apply progressive disclosure to surface secondary information only when the user needs it, rather than presenting everything at once. Write all text, error messages, labels, and instructions to the minimum length that preserves clarity. If your team does not have a professional writer, find one. 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
For every element on screen, ask: does this serve the user's most likely goal in this context? If not, it is a candidate for removal, subordination, or progressive disclosure. Pay particular attention to homepages, dashboards, and navigation structures, where the temptation to show everything is greatest. Audit for verbosity in text as well: error messages, labels, and instructions should be as short as they can be while remaining clear.
Related Traps
Often occurs alongside: 
Gratuitous Redundancy. Duplicate options contribute to Information Overload, adding visual weight and decision points without adding value. In these cases, Gratuitous Redundancy is the root cause of reduced efficiency.
Distraction. Information Overload slows progress by presenting the user with an excess of information which can be a Distraction, but excess information is the root cause of the problem. In any case, the solution is the same: remove things that aren’t relevant to the user’s goal.

Potentially. An AI can assess element count, word count, and option density against established guidelines and flag interfaces that exceed typical thresholds. Determining whether specific information is necessary for the user's task requires knowledge of user goals and context that may not be present in the design file. Observation of real users remains the most reliable detection method.

About this Trap
SYSTEM AMNESIA Traps arise when a system forgets what the user has done in the past and, as a result, either makes the user do the same work twice (e.g., re-enter the same information into a form), or presents them with content/information/suggestions in ways that show it has not been paying attention (e.g., promoting content a user already owns, losing context between chat sessions, asking for an account number after the user has already authenticated). In each case, the system was previously exposed to the information it needed – either because it did not collect the information in the first place, or because it collected it, but did not use it.
[Example]
Retaining and reusing information provided by a user is one of the most powerful tools a system has for reducing user effort. The cumulative effect of a system that remembers well is an experience that feels progressively easier over time.
Why does this Trap occur?
SYSTEM AMNESIA most often arises from siloed data architecture: different parts of a product do not share information about the user, so each context starts from scratch. It also arises from deliberate choices to re-prompt users for information for verification or legal reasons, which may be justified in some contexts but is often applied more broadly than necessary. In conversational AI systems, context loss between sessions is frequently a technical constraint, but one whose user-facing cost is rarely fully accounted for in the design.
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
When designing any flow where the user provides information, ask from the outset: could the system reasonably be expected to retain this and use it later? Design for retention as a default rather than an afterthought. Ensure user data is shared across product contexts rather than siloed within them. For recommendation systems, build in logic that excludes content or features the user has already engaged with. For conversational AI systems, design explicitly for cross-session context from the start: decide what the system should remember, how it should store it, and how it should surface that memory in ways that feel helpful rather than intrusive.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Audit every point where the user provides information and ask: has this user provided this before? If yes and the system is not using it, that is a SYSTEM AMNESIA instance. Also audit cross-session behavior: does the system remember where the user left off? Does it recall preferences across sessions? Does it avoid promoting content or features the user has already engaged with? Each failure of this kind is a candidate for improvement.
Related Traps
Not to be confused with (and often occurs with): 
Memory Challenge. Memory Challenge describes cases where the system design requires users to remember something that is easy to forget. System Amnesia describes cases where the system fails to leverage information the user has previously provided, regardless of how easy it is to remember. 
Often a root cause of: 
Bad Prediction. A system that fails to learn from prior user behavior will make worse predictions over time. Poor collection, retention, or use of information known about the user is a common root cause of Bad Prediction.
See also: 
Unwanted Disclosure. Avoiding System Amnesia means retaining data about the user. Every effort must be made to keep this data secure. Failure to do this exposes the user to the risk of Unwanted Disclosure of their information. 
Related Concepts 
[add anything that applies or remove]
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially – but the bar is quite high. It requires that the AI have knowledge of the underlying data architecture, which may not be present in a design file alone. If the AI has access to the data architecture, it can identify the user behavior the system tracks, and the information it collects from the user, and then review whether this information is later utilized by the system for the user’s benefit.

An interface is ACCURATE when it is true in word and deed; i.e., when the information it presents is correct and the actions it takes on the user’s behalf are welcomed.
Misleading, distorted, out of date, or otherwise inaccurate content is not a new problem. However, the rise of AI-powered interfaces has made the Accuracy of information that interfaces present simultaneously more important and more challenging to uphold. Large language and other AI models are, at their core, probabilistic. They generate responses that are statistically plausible rather than verified as true. As a result, they can produce information that ranges from subtly distorted to flatly wrong. 
Two Traps undermine accuracy: INCORRECT INFORMATION, and BAD PREDICTION.

 





 


About this Trap
An INCORRECT INFORMATION Trap is present when a user is presented with information that is wrong. Examples include factual inaccuracies, hallucinations, algorithmically biased content, intentionally misleading information (aka lies), or something as innocuous as typographical errors. In these cases the user may or may not know the information is incorrect.
What makes this Trap particularly consequential is potential for going under the radar. Most other Traps produce friction that the user can feel: an icon or label that is not understood, a slow response, an exceedingly large number of steps. INCORRECT INFORMATION often produces no friction at all. The user is presented with information, act on it, and not immediately realize its wrong. 
Example: AIs have made this Trap a more common problem
[editorial note: to be added]
Why does this Trap occur?
INCORRECT INFORMATION arises from many sources: outdated data that has not been refreshed, AI models that generate plausible-sounding but unverified content, human error in content creation, algorithmic systems that optimize for engagement rather than accuracy, and deliberate choices to present information in misleading ways. The last category, intentional inaccuracy, sits at the intersection of ethics and interface quality and calls for a different response than the others.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
For any information the interface presents as fact, ask at the outset: what is the source of this information? How will it be kept current? What is the consequence of it being wrong? High-stakes information, covering health, finance, safety, and legal matters, warrants the highest standard of verification and the clearest disclosure of its limitations. AI-generated content should be explicitly labeled as such and, where possible, accompanied by source citations that allow users to verify it independently. Interfaces that display time-sensitive information should be designed with clear mechanisms for keeping it current from the start, not patched with staleness warnings after the fact.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Audit every category of information the interface presents and ask: when was this last verified? Could it be out of date? What happens to the user if it is wrong? Pay particular attention to AI-generated content, where inaccuracy may be intermittent and hard to detect without systematic testing. For algorithmic outputs, test for bias across different user populations: a system that produces accurate results on average may produce systematically skewed results for specific groups.
Related Traps  
Sometimes the root cause of:
Inviting Dead End. Incorrect information that suggests a wrong path forward is actually a correct one (e.g., a button that is simply mislabeled) functions as an Inviting Dead End, but the Incorrect Information is the root cause of the user taking the wrong path.
Often occurs alongside: 
Bad Prediction. Incorrect Information describes content that is factually wrong, incomplete, or error-filled. Bad Prediction describes when the system takes proactive steps that are not welcomed, regardless of whether these steps include the presentation of Incorrect Information.
Related Concepts
Epistemic Trust. Research in developmental and cognitive psychology has established that humans extend trust to sources of information based on cues including apparent expertise, consistency, and past accuracy. Interfaces that present information with confidence, regardless of its accuracy, exploit this tendency. Users have no reliable way to calibrate their trust in an interface's outputs without external verification, which is why the design obligation to be accurate is not merely a quality issue but an ethical one.
Confirmation Bias. The well-documented tendency of people to favor information that confirms their existing beliefs and to discount information that challenges them. Relevant to this Trap in two ways: first, users are less likely to notice or question INCORRECT INFORMATION that aligns with what they already believe, making certain inaccuracies systematically harder to detect. Second, algorithmic systems trained on user behavior may learn to surface information that confirms user beliefs rather than information that is accurate, because confirmatory information tends to generate more engagement.
Algorithmic Bias. Systems that learn from historical data will reproduce the biases present in that data. An interface that draws on biased training data will produce systematically skewed outputs, which may appear accurate in aggregate while being consistently wrong for specific user populations. Designing for accuracy requires testing across the full range of users the system will serve, not just the majority or the most represented group in the training data.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
With difficulty. An AI can flag content that is internally inconsistent (e.g., text that incorrectly describes a feature or capability of the system), lacks source citations, appears out of date, or is generated by a model known to hallucinate. Detecting whether specific information is factually wrong requires external verification against authoritative sources, which is context-dependent and cannot be automated reliably for all content types. Intentional inaccuracy is particularly hard to detect without access to the intent behind the design decision.

About this Trap
BAD PREDICTION Traps occur when a system misses in its attempt to proactively help the user. They may be experienced as auto-correct or auto-complete errors, proactive automation errors, irrelevant content recommendations, or ill-timed suggestions from a voice assistant, etc. The user understands what the system is suggesting or doing on their behalf but doesn’t find it valuable. Users asses these errors as poor judgment on the part of the system—its off target, i.e., inaccurate. The consequences of this inaccuracy can degrade other Tenets. For example, unwelcomed actions may distract the user, offend them, embarrass them, or them to work around or undo the error. 
[editorial note: consider adding examples of range of consequences – bad movie reco vs 911 false alarm]
This context-sensitivity is one of the things that makes BAD PREDICTION a nuanced Trap to evaluate. A system that autocompletes text and gets it wrong 10% of the time may still be a net positive if the 90% success rate saves enough effort to outweigh the friction of corrections. A system that automatically sends messages on the user's behalf with the same error rate is a very different proposition. The question is not whether prediction is perfect, but whether the benefit of acting on imperfect prediction outweighs the cost of getting it wrong in this particular context.
Why does this Trap occur?
BAD PREDICTION often arises from optimization pressures: systems trained to maximize engagement make efforts to predict what produces clicks rather than what serves user needs, which are not always the same thing. The problem is accurate prediction is really hard. The contextual factors that determine a user’s receptivity to suggestions are myriad and constantly shifting. Systems often lack the understanding they need to effectively ‘read the room’. A song suggestion that might be welcomed on one day might be judged as painful and ill-timed the next if something has changed in the user’s life. 
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
For every predictive feature, ask: what is the consequence of a wrong prediction? Can the user easily correct it? Is the system acting or suggesting? Acting requires a higher accuracy threshold than suggesting. Where the consequence of a wrong prediction is significant and reversal is difficult, the system should suggest rather than act, and should make it easy to dismiss the suggestion. Where prediction accuracy cannot be verified, default to inaction.
The safe rule to follow is ‘predict when certain’, or at minimum, only predict when the negative consequences of a bad prediction are well understood and trivial. 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Look for this Trap anywhere the system predicts what the user would or could do. For each instance, ask: how often does the prediction fail? What does the user have to do when it does? If correction requires more effort than the prediction saves, the feature is a net negative. Observe users directly: moments of hesitation, correction, or frustration following a system-initiated action are reliable signals of BAD PREDICTION.
Related Traps 
May be the root cause of: 
Accidental Activation. In many cases, Accidental Activations occur when a system misreads a user's intent. Voice and gesture interfaces are particularly prone to this: a wake word detected in background conversation, a gesture triggered by incidental movement. In these cases the system makes a prediction about what the user wants, acts on it, and gets it wrong. The Accidental Activation is the symptom. The Bad Prediction is the cause. 
Distraction: A poorly timed or irrelevant recommendation can be a Distraction to the user (e.g., and irrelevant notification that pops up and distracts the user from their current goal). 
Unwanted Disclosure: May cause the wrong content or information to be presented to others (e.g., an autocorrect error that is not caught before a user hits send). 
Unnecessary Step(s): May introduce consequence the user is forced to undo or step around (e.g., a row of bad suggestions in a music app puts other content that much further away, a thermostat that is proactively adjusted may need to be reset). 
Sometimes caused by: 
System Amnesia. A system that fails to retain prior user context will make worse predictions over time, making System Amnesia a common root cause of persistent Bad Prediction. 
Often occurs alongside: 
Unnecessary Step(s). When a prediction is wrong and cannot be easily dismissed or ignored, the user may need to take additional steps to correct it, introducing Unnecessary Steps. 
Incorrect Information. If the system acted or suggested without being asked and got it wrong, that is Bad Prediction. The information or action need not include factual inaccuracies, only be poorly timed or irrelevant. If it is inaccurate, both Traps apply.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially. An AI can identify predictive features in a design and flag those where the consequence of a wrong prediction is high. Determining whether a specific prediction is actually wrong requires access to data on prediction accuracy in real use, which is not present in the design file. Observation of users interacting with predictive features remains the most reliable detection method.

“With this assurance, he feels bold in his explorations of the world.”
- John Bowlby

An interface is PROTECTIVE when it allows users to explore freely, secure in the knowledge that they can reverse course if needed, that nothing will be shared without their knowledge or consent, and that their data will never be lost.
To operate with confidence, users must believe the system has their back. When they know they can undo actions, they are free to move through the system without hesitation: to make decisions, explore possibilities, and try things they are not certain about. This freedom is not just about recovering from mistakes, though it covers those too. It is about the assurance that no action is truly final unless the user chooses it to be.
The same assurance must also extend to users’ data. Just as they should be able to reverse any action, they should be able to trust that the system will never share their information or lose their work without their explicit consent. Both failures undermine the same thing: the user's ability to act freely. 
The data dimension of this Tenet has grown considerably more important as technology has become more embedded in daily life. Our devices follow us everywhere. They hear things, record things, and share things, sometimes in ways their users never intended. We also populate these systems ourselves, voluntarily and not, with our most personal information: financial records, health data, an exchange with a chatbot on a sensitive topic. This data does not stay on the device. It travels, accumulates, and persists. What constitutes an appropriate level of discretion has become one of the defining design challenges of our time, and it will only grow more pressing as interfaces continue to move into more intimate contexts.
Three Traps undermine protection: IRREVERSIBLE ACTION, UNWANTED DISCLOSURE and DATA LOSS. 

 



“When it's easy for people to back out of a process or undo an action, it fosters a sense of freedom and confidence.”
– Jakob Neisen

About this Trap
An IRREVERSIBLE ACTION Trap occurs when a user cannot backtrack or undo an action they have taken. The consequences can range from frustrating to catastrophic: a purchase that cannot be cancelled, a message that cannot be recalled, a file that cannot be restored.
Irreversibility is not always avoidable. Some actions have real-world consequences that cannot be undone: a payment that has been processed, a print job that has started, a message that has been delivered and read. This Trap applies when recovery is possible but not supported.
Example: Instagram
At one time, Instagram did not provide a way to undo the deletion of posts. Later, they introduced a thirty-day recovery window, giving users the ability to restore deleted content. The recovery path had always been technically feasible. It simply had not been designed.
Figure x - [editorial note: to be added]
Why does this Trap occur?
IRREVERSIBLE ACTION most often arises when teams design forward through a flow without designing backward. The intended path is clear. The recovery path for when a user arrives at a destination unintentionally is not considered. It also arises from technical constraints that are accepted as given when they could be engineered around, and from a not uncommon assumption that consequential actions require confirmation rather than reversibility.
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Design forwards and backwards. For every action in a flow, ask: what does the user do if they change their mind? Ensure your designs provide a persistent, consistent means of undoing actions. The recovery path deserves the same design attention as the forward path.
Forgive rather than confirm. Avoid confirmation dialogs as a substitute for reversibility. They add a step, interrupt the user's flow, and are frequently dismissed without being read. A system that allows users to undo is more forgiving, and more efficient, than one that asks them to confirm.
Proactively eliminate the need to undo. Look for opportunities to get ahead of potential errors, e.g., by asking the user if they meant to send an email without an attachment when the attachment is referenced within the body of the message. 
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
When evaluating a task flow, ask at every step: what happens if the user makes a mistake or changes their mind? Any action that cannot be undone is a candidate for this Trap. Where full reversibility is not possible, consider whether a time-limited recovery window is feasible: the ability to recall a sent email within thirty seconds, or to cancel a submitted order before it is processed, is better than no recovery option at all. Where confirmation dialogs currently exist, ask whether they could be replaced with reversibility. In most cases they can, and the experience is better for it.
FAQ: Should all actions be reversible?
No. For some tasks irreversibility is the point. For example, when a user’s intent is to permanently delete a sensitive file. In these cases, it becomes critical to confirm a user’s intent. Doing this well means doing more than just presenting users with a confirmation dialog. Jef Raskin observed that, over time, users can dismiss these dialogs automatically, rendering them ineffective. A confirmation that requires a non-habituating response, such as typing a specific word or phrase rather than clicking a button, is harder to dismiss without conscious attention. 
[editorial note: add Raskin citation on ‘complicating the confirmation’]
Related Traps 
Often the root cause of: 
Data Loss. When a user action causes data to be lost and cannot be undone, both Traps are in play simultaneously. Making the action reversible typically resolves both. 
Unnecessary Step(s). Confirmation dialogs are often added as a substitute for reversibility. Replacing them with reversibility removes the step and produces a better experience.
See also:
Bad Prediction. Proactive error prevention, such as flagging a missing attachment, is a form of prediction. It should only be implemented when certainty behind the prediction is high. 
Related Concepts  
User Control and Freedom. One of Neilsen’s 10 UI heuristics. It speaks to users’ potential for making mistakes and calls for providing clearly marked "emergency exits" to allow users to quickly recover from unwanted actions.
Error Prevention. Nielsen's fifth usability heuristic holds that it is better to design systems that prevent problems from occurring in the first place than to provide good error messages after the fact. Where that is not possible, the next best response is to design confirmation steps that are resistant to habituation, so users cannot dismiss them without conscious attention.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially. An AI can identify irreversible actions in a design and flag those where the consequence of a wrong prediction is high. Determining whether a specific prediction is reversible requires access to system code or detailed design flows that show all back paths.

About this Trap
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE Traps occur any time a system communicates something about a user’s data or behavior in a way they did not intend. Causes might include share-by-default privacy settings, un-silencable user or system-generated sounds, recording of video or audio without awareness or consent, etc.
The scope of this Trap is broader than it might first appear. The most obvious form is digital: data shared with third parties without clear consent, location information surfaced in unexpected ways, purchase history visible to household members who were not meant to see it. But the Trap also has a physical dimension. A device that reads out a notification in a crowded room, a voice assistant that responds audibly in a quiet office, a screen that displays sensitive information in a public place: these are all instances of UNWANTED DISCLOSURE, and they will become more common as computing moves further into social and physical contexts.
[editorial note: add example of remote/digital disclosure and real-time disclosure – notification beep]
The rise of advertising-based business models has made this Trap more prevalent and more consequential. Systems that monetize user data have structural incentives to collect more of it, share more of it, and surface more of it than users would choose if the choice were clearly presented to them. The interface decisions that enable this, default sharing settings, opaque data collection, opt-out rather than opt-in consent, are often deliberate. This is the territory where interface design and ethics most directly intersect.
Why does this Trap occur?
UNWANTED DISCLOSURE arises from two distinct sources. The first is oversight: designers do not fully consider how data collected in one context may surface in another, or how a feature that is useful in one setting becomes intrusive in another. The second is intention: systems are designed to share data in ways that serve business goals rather than user interests. Both are instances of this Trap, though they call for different responses.
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Any time user data is collected, ask: where could this data surface, and would the user expect and accept that? Any time an interface will be used in social or public contexts, ask: what could this system say, display, or reveal at an inopportune moment? Default settings should reflect what users would choose if they were paying full attention, not what maximizes data collection or sharing. Consent should be explicit, clear, and given at a moment when the user understands what they are consenting to.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Walk through every context in which the interface is likely to be used, including social and public ones. For each, ask: what data could surface here, and would the user expect it? Review default privacy settings and ask whether they reflect user interests or system interests. Trace every feature that collects, stores, or surfaces data and ask where that data could go. Any path that leads somewhere the user would not anticipate or accept is a candidate for this Trap.
Related Traps 
Often occurs alongside: 
Feedback Failure. An interface that provides feedback audibly in a context where the user cannot control the volume may produce Unwanted Disclosure as a side effect of otherwise appropriate feedback design.
Bad Prediction. Systems that surface user data proactively, such as displaying purchase history on a shared device, may do so based on a misjudgment of context. When that misjudgment exposes private information, Bad Prediction and Unwanted Disclosure occur together.
Related Concepts
Contextual Integrity. Philosopher Helen Nissenbaum's framework for privacy holds that what makes a disclosure appropriate or inappropriate is not simply whether information is secret, but whether it flows in ways that match the norms of the context in which it was originally shared. Medical information shared with a doctor flows appropriately to other treating physicians but not to employers. A purchase made on one platform does not flow appropriately to a social news feed. Contextual integrity provides a principled basis for evaluating whether a given data flow constitutes UNWANTED DISCLOSURE: the question to ask is not "is this data private?" but "does this flow match what the user would expect given the context in which they shared it?"
Privacy by Design. A framework developed by Ann Cavoukian, former Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario, which holds that privacy should be built into systems from the outset rather than added as an afterthought. Its core principle, that privacy should be the default rather than an opt-in, maps directly onto the most common source of this Trap: default settings that serve system interests rather than user interests.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Partially. An AI can identify features that collect or surface user data and flag default settings that appear to favor data sharing over user privacy. Determining whether a specific disclosure would be unexpected or harmful requires understanding of the social and physical contexts in which the interface will be used, which may not be present in the design file. The physical dimension of this Trap, disclosures that occur through sound or screen in social settings, is particularly difficult to detect without contextual knowledge of real-world use.

About this Trap
DATA LOSS Traps occur when a user unintentionally loses their work, whether through an action or inaction. Causes might include unexpected system or app shutdowns without auto-saving, forms that do not preserve partial entries, or data entry conflicts that occur when co-authoring.
The convention of requiring users to explicitly save their work is a historical artifact of an era when computer memory was scarce. It required users to take a deliberate action to preserve their work and penalized them when they forgot or took that action at the wrong moment. Cloud-based applications has gone a long way to minimize the occurrences of this Trap, but despite this, they still persist.
Example: 
Why does this Trap occur? 
Data Loss most often arises from technical constraints that were accepted as permanent when they were actually solvable: the assumption that saving must be explicit, that sessions cannot persist across closures, that co-authoring conflicts are unavoidable. It also arises from technical designs (native retention), technical failures (synchronization errors), and insufficient testing of failure modes: what happens when a session times out, when a network connection drops, when two users edit the same record simultaneously.   
How to avoid it  
Preventing it in the first place
Design systems to save continuously and automatically wherever technically possible. For collaborative tools, design conflict resolution mechanisms that protect all users' contributions rather than defaulting to last-write-wins. Design for failure from the outset: session timeouts, network interruptions, and unexpected shutdowns are not edge cases. They are certainties that will affect real users.
If data loss is the goal, complicate the confirmation. Sometimes a user intends to permanently delete data, for example, a sensitive financial record sitting on a shared computer. Obviously, systems should allow them to do this. But protections should be put in place; opening the door to deletion also opens the door to unintended data loss. One way this can happen is if a user unthinkingly clicks a confirmation dialog after having encountered many of them. To avoid this risk, the confirmation should be designed in a way that commands the user’s attention. For example, by requiring the user to type in a novel word before the deletion occurs. Jeff Raskin called this complicating the confirmation.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Identify every point in a product where the user creates or modifies data. For each, ask: what happens to this data if the session ends unexpectedly? What happens if the user navigates away without saving? What happens in a multi-user scenario? Any point where the answer is "it could be lost" is a candidate for this Trap. Test failure modes deliberately: simulate unexpected shutdowns, session timeouts, and network interruptions, and verify that user data is preserved across all of them.
Related Traps  
Not to be confused with: 
System Amnesia. DATA LOSS involve the system failing to retain information the user expected it to keep. System Amnesia involves the system failing to use data the user previously provided regardless of whether the system still has access to it.  
Often occurs alongside: 
Irreversible Action. When a user action causes data to be lost and that action cannot be undone, both Traps are in play simultaneously. The user took an action (Irreversible Action) and lost data as a result (Data Loss). 
See also: 
Unnecessary Step(s). Requiring users to explicitly save their work adds a step that continuous automatic saving can eliminate.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially. An AI can identify interfaces that lack auto-save mechanisms and flag forms or flows that do not preserve partial state. Testing for failure modes, session timeouts, network interruptions, and co-authoring conflicts requires active simulation rather than static analysis and is harder to automate reliably. Further, beyond some obvious example, understanding what data users would be more or less concerned about having exposed requires knowledge of user and their preferences.

“The more of the details of our daily life we can hand over to the effortless custody of automatism, the more our higher powers of mind will be set free.”
- William James

Every other Tenet in this framework describes a quality of the immediate experience: whether users can see what they need, reach what they want, wait a reasonable amount of time. HABITUATING is different. It describes what happens over time, whether repeated use of an interface eventually becomes automatic for the user.
An interface is HABITUATING when, with practice, users stop thinking about how to use it and focus entirely on their goals. The interactions that once required conscious attention become effortless.
Each of us has first-hand experience with this. Touch typing, riding a bike, driving a car: activities that once demanded our full attention. Once mastered, we could do them while our minds were elsewhere entirely. That shift from conscious deliberation to automaticity is one of the most remarkable things human beings do. It is also, as Raskin recognized, one of the most powerful things a designer can aim for.
Great interface designs tap into our capacity to form habits.
The human capacity to habituate is hardwired, but it only engages under the right conditions. When a product behaves in predictable ways, users cannot help but habituate to it. The Power Law of Practice shows that performance improves as a predictable function of repetition: each attempt takes less time than the last, following a curve that flattens as mastery approaches. When a product behaves unpredictably, or gives users different ways of doing the same thing, improvement is slow and interactions remain effortful. At worst, the consequences are catastrophic. Unpredictable controls are a recurring factor in automotive and aviation disasters.
The design decisions that support habituation are not obvious.
Two sub-tenets capture what must hold for good habits to form. Neither is as straightforward as it first appears, which is why the Traps associated with them are so pernicious. The first is Non-Redundant: the interface should not provide duplicate paths for completing the same action. Redundancy slows habituation because it introduces choices and deliberation. The tricky part is that some forms of redundancy are not only harmless but beneficial. The second is Consistent with Expectations: the same action should produce the same outcome. Without predictable responses, practice cannot accumulate into mastery. But sometimes generating a different response to the same action is perfectly acceptable. The Trap sections that follow work through these distinctions carefully.
Beyond their nuance, other factors lead to violations of these sub-tenets. Redundancy violations are typically well intended: teams add duplicate paths to improve discoverability, reasoning that more entry points mean users will find what they need. The result is a more cluttered interface that takes longer to navigate and longer to master. Consistency violations often emerge when teams try to get more functionality out of a limited set of controls by mapping different functions to the same controls across different system modes. Overloaded functionality like this is especially susceptible to the curse of knowledge: designers cannot unknow the state of their own system, and mode errors are difficult to catch when you already know how the system works.
The five Traps in this section are organized under three sub-tenets. The first two are described above. The third, Oriented, is a special case of both: a well-oriented interface provides one consistent action users can take from anywhere to return to a single home, making navigation itself something that can be done effortlessly.
Non-Redundant. Duplicate paths to the same destination slow the accumulation of mastery and clutter the interface. One Trap undermines non-redundancy: GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY.
Consistent with Expectations. The same user actions produce the same results, the same interface elements look the same wherever they appear, and the same elements can always be found in the same place. When any of these conditions fail, users are pulled back into conscious deliberation precisely when habit should be taking over. Three Traps undermine consistency: VARIABLE OUTCOME, WANDERING ELEMENT, and INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE.
Oriented. A single reliable home can be reached from anywhere with one consistent action. Without it, users must hold too much of the interface in conscious memory. One Trap undermines orientation: AMBIGUOUS HOME.
Know thy code.
Unlike the UNDERSTANDABLE Tenet, where knowing your users is the foundational requirement, these Traps are less dependent on user knowledge and more amenable to direct inspection. All humans are susceptible to them regardless of background or experience. When Larry Tesler, one of the pioneers of personal computing, was asked how he made product teams aware of HABITUATING Traps, his answer was immediate: "I tell them to look at their code." GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY shows up as multiple links pointing to the same destination. VARIABLE OUTCOME shows up as state-handlers routing the same user action to different outcomes. You do not always need a user in the room to find these problems. Knowing your users still matters, particularly for understanding their expectations. But with these Traps, the adage that serves you best is not "know thy user." It is "know thy code."  

" The ideal interface is one that can be operated entirely by habit."
— Jef Raskin

About this Trap
An interface is non-redundant when there is only one way to achieve a given goal, something Jef Raskin referred to, with tongue firmly in cheek, "monotony”—meaning “one way to do things”. The benefit of this: First, users are presented with fewer options, and considering options takes time (see Hick’s Law, which tells us decisions take time). Second, users put their focus and energy into practicing one method for doing something, rather than several. When humans repeatedly do the same thing the same way, it soon becomes automatic (think touch typing, riding a bike, etc.). While many teams resist the idea of monotony on instinct (more is better, right?), the research supports its spirit.
Gratuitous Redundancy describes when an interface provides multiple paths for completing the same goal in the same manner. Examples of include multiple instances of controls or links that serve the same function, etc. But the principle requires careful handling, because not all redundancy is the same. 
The distinction that matters is grammatical. Consider two ways of placing a phone call. In the first, a user selects a contact and then chooses to call. In the second, a user selects the phone function and then chooses a contact. Both paths reach the same destination, but they support different ways of thinking about the task: one starts with the object, the other with the action. This kind of redundancy adds flexibility without adding clutter. It accommodates different mental models without multiplying objects in the system. It is not gratuitous.
GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY is different. It occurs when an interface presents duplicate entry points to the same destination: two instances of the same icon, three links that go to the same page, a control that appears in both a toolbar and a menu doing exactly the same thing. This kind of redundancy does not serve different mental models. It simply multiplies choices. And multiplying choices has a measurable cost. The more choices a user faces, the longer it takes to make a decision. Designers who add redundant options to improve discoverability often achieve the opposite: a more cluttered interface that takes longer to navigate and longer to master.
GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY also directly undermines the Power Law of Practice. Mastery is a function of repeating the same action. When users can reach the same destination by multiple routes, their practice is divided across those routes rather than concentrated on one. The curve toward automaticity flattens. Mastery takes longer.
This Trap is also a gateway. Once introduced, gratuitous redundancy tends to generate other Traps. Duplicate options push other content off screen, creating INVISIBLE ELEMENTS. Scrolling to reach displaced content introduces UNNECESSARY STEPS. The proliferation of choices adds cognitive load that contributes to INFORMATION OVERLOAD. Fixing the redundancy often resolves several problems at once.
Why does this Trap occur?
GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY most often arises from a desire to make features discoverable. The reasoning is understandable: if users cannot find something, add another path to it. But…
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
For each goal a user might pursue, map every path the interface offers to achieve it before adding new ones. Establish a principle that each destination should be reachable by one primary path, and that any additional path must serve a genuinely different mental model rather than duplicating an existing one. Pay particular attention to navigation structures, homepages, and dashboards, where the temptation to add entry points is greatest.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
For each goal a user might pursue, map every path the interface currently offers to achieve it. Any destination reachable by more than one route from the same starting point is a candidate for this Trap. Audit the code directly: multiple links pointing to the same destination are a reliable signal. This Trap rarely surfaces in standard usability testing or design reviews, where teams typically walk the intended task flow and evaluate whether users can complete tasks. That exercise does not reveal duplicate paths. Detecting GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY requires systematically mapping all the ways a user might interpret a path forward, not just the intended one.
FAQ: Is redundancy ever good?
Yes. Accommodating users' different ways of conceptualizing a task, starting with the object or starting with the action, is desirable and does not constitute GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY. The distinction: beneficial redundancy provides different paths for users who think about a task differently. GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY provides duplicate paths for completing the same task in the same way, resulting in more clutter and more decision points without any corresponding benefit.
Related Traps

INVISIBLE ELEMENT. Duplicating options may push other content off screen, turning previously visible elements into INVISIBLE ELEMENTS.
UNNECESSARY STEPS. Content displaced by redundant options requires additional scrolling to access, introducing steps that would not otherwise exist.
INFORMATION OVERLOAD. Redundant options increase the volume of information the user must process, contributing to INFORMATION OVERLOAD.

Related Concepts
Hick's Law. Formulated by William Edmund Hick in 1952 and refined by Ray Hyman, Hick's Law states that the time required to make a decision increases logarithmically with the number of choices available. Each doubling of the choice set adds a roughly constant increment of decision time. GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY increases the number of choices the user faces without adding value, making Hick's Law the quantitative foundation for why this Trap slows navigation and delays mastery.
The Power Law of Practice. A well-established finding in cognitive psychology, the Power Law of Practice states that performance on a repeated task improves as a predictable function of the number of attempts, with each attempt taking less time than the last following a curve that flattens as mastery approaches. GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY undermines this law by dividing a user's practice across multiple paths to the same destination, preventing the concentration of repetition on a single route that mastery requires.
Raskin's Law of Monotony. Jef Raskin argued in The Humane Interface that interfaces should provide exactly one way to accomplish each task. He called this principle monotony, deliberately choosing a term most designers would resist, to make the point that the instinct toward variety and flexibility is often the enemy of habituation. The principle does not prohibit accommodating different mental models. It prohibits providing duplicate paths that serve the same model.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Better than most. GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY is detectable through structural analysis: multiple links pointing to the same destination, the same control appearing in multiple locations, duplicate navigation options. An AI reviewing a site map, code, or design file can identify these patterns systematically. Determining whether a redundant path serves a genuinely different mental model, and therefore whether it is gratuitous, requires understanding of user behavior that may not be present in the design file alone.

About this Trap
Examples include Mode-dependent controls (e.g., Caps Lock); Inconsistently supported functions (e.g., right-click support for some instances of the same interface element, but not others); etc.
VARIABLE OUTCOME is most often the result of a mode error. A mode is a system state that causes the same user action to produce a different result depending on context. The CapsLock key is the most familiar example. Pressing a letter key normally produces a lowercase character. With CapsLock active, the same press produces an uppercase one. The mode is easy to forget, and when you forget it, the outcome surprises you.
CapsLock errors are annoying but harmless. VARIABLE OUTCOME Traps are not always so benign. Mode errors are a recurring factor in catastrophic aviation accidents. A pilot who believes an aircraft is in one state when it is in another may take exactly the wrong action at exactly the wrong moment. The same class of error, a mismatch between expected and actual system state, has caused car accidents, medical device failures, and industrial incidents. The stakes vary enormously. The underlying mechanism is always the same.
What makes this Trap particularly difficult is that modes are not always the culprit, and the problem is not always as obvious as CapsLock. Consider an interface where right-clicking on some words reveals a definition while right-clicking on others does nothing. There is no mode in the strict sense. But the outcome varies in ways the user cannot predict, because no clear rule has been communicated. This is VARIABLE OUTCOME without a mode, and variations of it appear in products everywhere.
The key question is not whether the same action produces different results, but whether the user is attending to the signal that explains the difference. Modes are acceptable when they are clear enough that users cannot forget which mode they are in. A user pressing a button on a touchscreen is attending to what is on that button. If the button changes based on context but the user is looking at it, the variable outcome is expected and unproblematic. The same logic applies to quasi-modes: holding down the Shift key changes the behavior of every other key, but because the user is actively holding Shift, the mode is impossible to forget. The problem arises when the signal indicating the mode is not attended to, when it is located away from the user's focus, or when it requires the user to remember a system state they have no reason to be tracking.
The cure is not always to add a better signal. Sometimes the better solution is to eliminate the mode altogether. Video game research has repeatedly shown that controllers with overloaded buttons, where the same button does different things in different game states, produce slower learning than controllers where each button has one dedicated function. Even when the mode indicator is perfectly visible, eliminating the mode accelerates mastery.
Why does this Trap occur?
VARIABLE OUTCOME Traps almost always arise from good intentions. Assigning multiple functions to the same control is an efficient way to pack more capability into a limited interface. State-handlers in code are the mechanism: a single action routed to different outcomes depending on system state. This is standard engineering practice and is often the right technical choice. The problem arises when the user-facing implications are not considered alongside the technical ones.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
For every control in the interface, ask: does this action always produce the same result, or does the outcome depend on system state? For every state-dependent outcome, ask: will users reliably know which state they are in at the moment they act? If the answer is uncertain, consider eliminating the mode rather than improving its indicator. A control with one dedicated function is always preferable to a control whose behavior depends on a state the user must track. Where modes are unavoidable, ensure the mode indicator is located where the user will be attending when they act, not elsewhere on the screen.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
This is one of the most reliably missed Traps in the framework. The curse of knowledge is particularly acute here: designers and developers know the state-handling logic of their system. They know which mode they are in. They cannot easily imagine encountering the interface without that knowledge. As a result, they experience no dissonance when the same action produces different results. The most reliable detection technique is to scrub the code directly for state-handlers: any place where the same user action is routed to different outcomes is a candidate for this Trap. For each one, ask: is there any realistic scenario in which a user might take this action without attending to the system state that determines the outcome? If yes, you likely have a VARIABLE OUTCOME Trap. When evaluating designs in review, resist walking only the intended path. Actively look for controls that behave differently in different contexts and ask whether users will reliably know which context they are in.
Related Traps
Sometimes caused by:
INVISIBLE ELEMENT: The absence of any mode indicator may make it impossible for users to predict the outcome of mode-dependent actions. In these cases, fixing the INVISIBLE ELEMENT is a key step in eliminating the VARIABLE OUTCOME.
EFFECTIVELY INVISIBLE ELEMENT. When a mode indicator is located away from where the user is attending, it becomes effectively invisible, making it impossible for users to predict the outcome of mode-dependent actions. In these cases, fixing the EFFECTIVELY INVISIBLE ELEMENT will eliminate the VARIABLE OUTCOME.
Related Concepts
Modes and Mode Errors. A mode is a system state in which the same user action produces a different result than it would in another state. Jef Raskin, who wrote extensively on the subject in The Humane Interface, argued that modes are among the most dangerous features a designer can introduce, precisely because they make the system's behavior unpredictable to users who have lost track of which mode they are in. Raskin's preferred solution was modeless design: interfaces where the same action always produces the same result, regardless of system state. Where modes are unavoidable, his criterion for acceptability was strict: a mode is only safe when it is impossible for the user to forget which mode they are in.
Quasi-Modes. A quasi-mode is a system state that persists only as long as the user maintains a specific physical action, such as holding down a key or pressing a button. Because the user must actively maintain the mode, they cannot forget it. Raskin identified quasi-modes as the acceptable alternative to true modes: they allow state-dependent behavior without the mode-forgetting errors that make true modes dangerous. The Shift key is the canonical example: it changes the behavior of every other key on the keyboard, but because the user is holding it, the state is continuously present in their attention.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Better than most for the mode-based form. State-handlers in code, where the same user action is routed to different outcomes depending on system state, are detectable through code analysis. An AI reviewing the codebase can identify these patterns and flag them as candidates. Determining whether users will reliably attend to the mode indicator requires understanding of where users direct their attention during the interaction, which may require user observation to verify. The modeless form of this Trap, where outcomes vary without a formal mode, is harder to detect automatically and more dependent on design review and user observation.

About this Trap
Inconsistent placement of controls, status indicators, content, etc.
[Do not have to present the same visually]
The Power Law of Practice shows that mastery is a function of repeating the same action. When the same control appears in different locations across an interface, users cannot repeat exactly the same action to reach it. Each encounter requires a small act of search and reorientation rather than automatic reach. Practice accumulates across variations rather than concentrating on one reliable path.
The analogy is instructive: mastering an interface that places the same controls in different locations is like learning to drive a car whose brake pedal moves depending on which road you are on. The skill never fully consolidates because the conditions never stay constant.
This matters because humans have a remarkable capacity for spatial memory. Once a control is reliably located in a particular place, users stop consciously registering its position and simply reach for it. This is one of the most powerful forms of automaticity available to interface designers, and it requires no special design effort, only the discipline to keep things where they are. WANDERING ELEMENT squanders this capacity entirely.
Why does this Trap occur?
WANDERING ELEMENTS most often arise when different parts of an interface are designed by different teams or at different times, each making locally sensible decisions about placement without auditing what exists elsewhere. They also arise from iterative redesign, where elements are moved to optimize one context without considering the effect on others. The problem is compounded by the way interfaces are typically evaluated: task by task, flow by flow, rather than as a system whose consistency across contexts can be audited.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Establish placement conventions for high-frequency controls early in the design process and treat them as constraints rather than defaults. Search, navigation, editing, and confirmation controls are the ones users most need to reach automatically: their placement should be decided once and held consistently across every context in which they appear. When designing new screens or flows, check placement decisions against what exists elsewhere in the system before finalizing them.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Standard design reviews and usability tests follow task flows. They reveal whether users can find and use controls within a given task. They do not reveal whether the same controls are consistently placed across different task contexts. Discovering WANDERING ELEMENTS requires a deliberate cross-context audit: identify the controls and indicators that appear most frequently across the interface, map their placement systematically across every context in which they appear, and flag any inconsistencies. Pay particular attention to high-frequency controls: search, navigation, editing, and confirmation. These are the controls where inconsistent placement does the most damage to habituation.
Related Traps
Not to be confused with:
INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE. WANDERING ELEMENT describes inconsistent placement. INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE describes inconsistent visual presentation without reference to placement. A control can wander without changing appearance and can change appearance without wandering.
Related Concepts
Spatial Memory. Humans form spatial memories for frequently used objects automatically and without conscious effort. Research by Scarr, Cockburn, and Gutwin documents the extent to which interface design can leverage this capacity: when controls are reliably located, users stop consciously registering their position and simply reach for them, a powerful form of automaticity that requires nothing from the designer except consistency. WANDERING ELEMENT defeats this capacity by ensuring that no reliable spatial memory can form. Every encounter with a displaced control pulls the user back into conscious search, undoing the automaticity that consistent placement would have built.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Better than most. WANDERING ELEMENT is detectable through systematic comparison of element placement across screens and contexts. An AI reviewing a complete set of design files can map the position of recurring elements and flag inconsistencies. This is exactly the kind of cross-context audit that human reviewers tend to skip in favor of task-based walkthroughs, making AI-assisted detection particularly valuable here.

About this Trap
Differing visual or auditory representation of the same icons, labels, controls, sounds, etc.
WANDERING ELEMENT describes controls that move. INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE describes controls that change how they look or sound while staying in the same place. The effect on habituation is similar: users cannot form a reliable automatic response to something that does not reliably present itself the same way.
The problem is compounded by its relationship to comprehension. An element that looks different in different contexts may still be understood on its own terms in each context. But a user who has learned to recognize a control in one form may not recognize it in another. The inconsistency does not just slow habituation: it can temporarily break it, requiring the user to stop, identify, and consciously decide whether this is the same thing they have encountered before. At that moment, habit has failed and deliberation has resumed.
The Windows operating system offers a persistent real-world example. Users navigating settings encounter a mixture of modern Fluent Design menus and legacy Control Panel windows that use interface conventions from the 1990s. The controls are not incomprehensible in isolation. But the visual inconsistency between them means users cannot form a single automatic response to "navigate settings." They must adjust their mental model each time they cross the boundary between the two design languages.
Why does this Trap occur?
INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE most commonly arises in large products developed over long periods, where design languages evolve but legacy components are not updated. It also arises when different teams apply shared design systems inconsistently, or when components are adapted for different contexts without maintaining visual coherence across the system as a whole.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Establish and maintain a design system that specifies the visual and auditory presentation of every recurring interface element. Treat deviations from the design system as exceptions that require explicit justification rather than locally reasonable adaptations. When evolving a design language, audit legacy components and update them to match rather than allowing two design languages to coexist. Pay particular attention to iconography representing recurring concepts and controls supporting high-frequency actions: these are the elements users most need to recognize automatically.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Like WANDERING ELEMENT, this Trap is invisible to task-based evaluation. A user completing a specific task will encounter controls in specific forms. Whether those same controls appear differently elsewhere in the system is not revealed by watching that task. Detection requires a deliberate cross-system visual audit: identify the interface elements that appear most frequently across the product, compare their visual and auditory presentation systematically across every context in which they appear, and flag any discrepancies. Pay particular attention to controls that have been adapted or evolved over time, where inconsistency is most likely to have accumulated unnoticed.
Related Traps
Not to be confused with:
WANDERING ELEMENT. INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE describes controls that look different in different contexts. WANDERING ELEMENT describes controls that appear in different locations. A control can change appearance without wandering and wander without changing appearance.
Related Concepts
Design Systems. A design system is a shared set of standards, components, and guidelines that ensures visual and functional consistency across a product. Its primary value is precisely the prevention of INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE: by specifying how every recurring element should look and behave, a well-maintained design system makes consistency the path of least resistance for every team working on the product. The failure mode is a design system that exists on paper but is not enforced in practice, or that is updated without a corresponding audit of legacy components that no longer conform to it.
Gestalt Principle of Similarity. The Gestalt principle of similarity holds that elements that look alike are perceived as belonging to the same category. Applied to interface design, this principle works in both directions: consistent appearance signals to users that elements share a function, while inconsistent appearance signals that they do not. INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE violates this expectation, presenting the same element in forms that the visual system treats as different things, forcing conscious identification where automatic recognition should suffice.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Better than most. INCONSISTENT APPEARANCE is detectable through systematic visual comparison of recurring elements across screens. An AI reviewing a complete set of design files can identify elements that share a function but differ in visual presentation, flagging them as candidates for this Trap. As with WANDERING ELEMENT, this is exactly the kind of cross-context audit that human reviewers tend to skip in task-based evaluations, making AI-assisted detection particularly valuable.

“There’s no place like home.”
– Dorothy Gale

About this Trap
Hierarchically organized interfaces are well-oriented when there is one place users conceptualize as home: a single, reliable starting point they can reach from anywhere with one consistent action. It provides the spatial and navigational anchor from which all habituation in a hierarchically structured interface flows.
When a clear single home exists, returning to it becomes one of the most deeply automatic actions a user can perform. The destination never changes. The action never changes. The result is a form of navigation that requires no conscious thought, freeing the user's attention entirely for the task at hand. When home is ambiguous, the opposite is true. Users must hold the structure of the interface in conscious memory, deciding each time where to go to begin a new task. The burden that habituation is supposed to relieve remains fully in place.
AMBIGUOUS HOME is a special case of both preceding sub-tenets. A well-oriented interface is consistent with expectations because the home action and its outcome never vary. It is non-redundant because there is only one home and only one way to reach it. Violating either condition produces ambiguity. Multiple homes is a redundancy problem. An inconsistent action for returning home is a consistency problem. Both undermine the same capacity: the ability to navigate without thinking.
The consequences extend beyond navigation. When users lack a clear home, they also lack a reliable recovery point. Getting lost in an interface is a common experience. A single, always-accessible home makes recovery automatic. Without it, users must reason their way back, which is the opposite of what a HABITUATING interface should require.
Why does this Trap occur?
AMBIGUOUS HOME most often arises in complex products where different teams design different sections of an interface independently, each establishing their own starting points and navigation conventions. It also arises from incremental product growth: features are added over time, each with its own entry point, until the interface accumulates multiple competing homes that no single team intended to create. Products that span multiple platforms or interaction modes, such as a desktop and touch interface for the same application, are particularly prone to this Trap, as each mode may develop its own home convention.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Establish a single home early in the design process and treat it as a constraint rather than a convention. Every section of the interface, regardless of which team designs it, should have one consistent action for returning to that home. When adding new features or entry points, audit the effect on the overall navigational structure: does the addition create a competing home, or does it integrate cleanly into the existing one? For products that span multiple platforms or interaction modes, define the home convention explicitly for each and ensure they are consistent where possible.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
Ask users, without prompting, where they would go to start a new task or recover from being lost. Inconsistent answers indicate an AMBIGUOUS HOME. In a design review, identify every element or location in the interface that could plausibly be interpreted as a starting point. More than one is a problem. Also evaluate the action for returning home: is it the same from every context? Is it a single action, or does it require multiple steps in some parts of the interface?
Related Traps
Often occurs alongside:
POOR GROUPING. If multiple locations in the interface offer partially overlapping capabilities, users will be slow to develop a mental model for where to go to begin a task.
MEMORY CHALLENGE. Without a clear home, users must remember where they are and how to get back, placing demands on memory that a well-oriented interface should eliminate.
VARIABLE OUTCOME. If a home action does not reliably take the user home, they will not habituate to its use.
GRATUITOUS REDUNDANCY. Duplicate means of getting oriented and initiating tasks slow habituation to any single one of them.
Related Concepts
Spatial Memory. The spatial memory research documented in the WANDERING ELEMENT section applies here as well. A clearly defined home is the anchor point around which spatial memory of an interface organizes itself. Without it, users cannot build the kind of reliable mental map that allows navigation to become automatic. See Scarr, Cockburn, and Gutwin (2013) for a comprehensive treatment.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
Potentially. An AI can identify multiple elements in an interface that could plausibly function as a home, flagging them as candidates for review. Determining which, if any, users actually conceptualize as home requires user observation. The action for returning home, whether it is consistent across contexts and requires a single step, is more directly analyzable from design files and is a reliable starting point for automated review.

TRAP
Poor Aesthetic

 
“To define the beautiful is to misunderstand it.” 
— Charles Robert Anon (Fernando Pessoa) 

An interface is BEAUTIFUL when users find its design appropriate and attractive. Of all nine Tenets, this one is the most resistant to prescription. Beauty cannot be computed, and attempts to reduce it to a formula have failed in ways that range from instructive to genuinely funny. Two artists, Komar and Melamid, once commissioned extensive surveys to identify the components of paintings that people most and least liked, then used the results to paint the statistically ideal picture. The exercise was deliberately satirical: they knew that aggregating preferences would not produce great art, and that was exactly the point. The paintings, blue-green landscapes populated with George Washington, grazing deer, and wholesome families, satisfied every statistical preference and were clearly awful. Beauty defies recombination. You cannot assemble its ingredients and reliably produce it.
Aesthetic design is a discipline.
Design is not styling. It is a principled practice with centuries of accumulated knowledge behind it. The principles that inform well-executed design, alignment, hierarchy, contrast, proximity, proportion, white space, color theory, are not matters of personal taste. They are tools that skilled practitioners have developed and refined over time, some with scientific support: symmetry is processed more easily than asymmetry, appropriate white space reduces cognitive load, clear visual hierarchy tells users where to look. These principles are a foundation, not a formula. Knowing when to depart from them, and how, is where deep expertise earns its place.
To be BEAUTIFUL, a product must be functionally excellent.
A product cannot be BEAUTIFUL if it fails on other Tenets. An interface that confuses, strains, or frustrates users will not be experienced as beautiful, however accomplished its visual execution. Dieter Rams put it plainly: only well-executed objects can be beautiful.
But functional excellence is not enough. What transforms it into beauty is appropriate expression. The Chemex coffeemaker, invented by chemist Peter Schlumbohm, illustrates this. His stated goal was direct: "A coffeemaker must make coffee, and then I applied my knowledge of physics and chemistry." He designed in borosilicate glass because it was non-porous and thermally stable, borrowed the hourglass form from laboratory flasks, and invented a thicker paper filter to remove bitterness. The result has been in the permanent collection of the Museum of Modern Art since 1943. It did not achieve this by primarily trying to be beautiful. It achieved it by solving the problem with integrity. As designer Joseph McLaughlin has written: "Beauty is not the opposite of function. It is the highest form of it. It doesn't replace utility; it reveals it."
Dieter Rams's products at Braun followed the same logic. So did the original Leica camera. The commitment to solving the essential problem as well as it could be solved produced objects recognized as beautiful, often after initial resistance.
Beauty is not something to test with users.
Users evaluate novelty against familiarity. The more innovative and elegant a design, the more likely it is to look different, and the more likely it is to be met with suspicion. When potential users encountered the Motorola Razr before launch, a phone dramatically thinner than anything on the market, responses were broadly negative. It'll break. It doesn't look like a phone. After launch, several things shifted perception together: users discovered its functional superiority, effective marketing communicated what made it different, and seeing influential people adopt it gave others permission to reconsider their initial resistance. The Razr became the new standard of beauty. The Aeron chair followed the same arc. In both cases, acting on pre-launch aesthetic feedback would have killed the product.
You cannot reliably test for beauty before shipping. But you can test for the things that will prevent it from being realized. An interface that confuses, strains, or makes users wait is not beautiful. Evaluating the product against the other eight Tenets is a powerful filter against aesthetic failure. Getting those things right is a necessary foundation.
Beyond functional excellence, a product must be expressed in a way that is appropriate for its users, its context, and its moment. The Chemex achieved this not because Schlumbohm was a designer by training but because he brought exactly what good designers bring: deep knowledge of his domain, fluency in the aesthetic currents of his time, and the judgment to make formal decisions that proved lasting. That combination of cultural knowledge, awareness of social and aesthetic trends, and understanding of what distinguishes enduring design from fashionable design is what takes a product from functionally excellent to deeply relevant and meaningful. This is what designers are for. The other eight Tenets tell you what a product must do. This one tells you what it must be.

Affect and beauty One of the best known fluency effects is the mere exposure effect originally identified by Zajonc (1968): The more often we see an object, like a Chinese ideograph, the more we like it.
From a fluency perspective, repeated exposure is just one of many variables that facilitate fluent processing. If so, any other variable that makes processing easy should also increase liking. Empirically this is the case, as a growing number of studies shows. For example, we like a stimulus more when a preceding visual or semantic prime facilitates its processing – we even find a picture of a lock more beautiful when it was preceded by the word ‘key’ (see Reber et al., 2004). This positive response to fluently processed stimuli can also be captured with electromyography, a procedure that measures subtle muscle responses in the face (Winkielman & Cacioppo, 2001), indicating that fluent processing feels good. Our preference for fluently processed stimuli underlies many of the variables known to influence aesthetic experience, from symmetry and figure–ground contrast to the gestalt laws – all of these variables facilitate fluent processing (Reber et al., 2004). The same principle is also central to the observation that we prefer prototypical faces over more unusual ones – prototypical faces are easier to process and elicit a more positive affective response (Winkielman et al., 2006). Moreover, this research also sheds light on why scientists and poets alike believe that beauty and truth go hand in hand, despite all the beautiful and elegant theories that landed on the trash heap of science – intuitive judgements of beauty and truth are based on the same input, namely the experience of fluent processing (Reber et al., 2009; Schwarz, 2006).]

“No one can be compelled to acknowledge something as beautiful by … reasoning from principles or rules.”
- Immanuel Kant 
About this Trap
POOR AESTHETIC is the most difficult Trap in the framework to diagnose. Every other Trap can be identified through structural analysis, measurement, or observation of user behavior. POOR AESTHETIC cannot. There is no measurement that tells you a design is unattractive, no code to inspect. There is only the response of the people experiencing it, interpreted in the context of who they are, where they are, and when.
This makes it easy to dismiss. Teams under pressure routinely cut aesthetic investment, treating it as optional. It is not. A product that performs well on other Tenets but fails aesthetically will be perceived as less trustworthy, less desirable, and less worth recommending than a comparable product that feels right.
POOR AESTHETIC has two dimensions. The first is attractiveness: the design is visually unpleasant. The second is appropriateness: the design is mismatched to its context, audience, or purpose. The most common failures involve both. Appropriateness is particularly time-sensitive: what feels current today can feel dated in three years. It is a moving target, and tracking it requires knowledge of where social and aesthetic trends are going, not just where they have been.
Why does this Trap occur?
POOR AESTHETIC most often arises from two sources: insufficient investment, when aesthetic quality is cut under schedule or budget pressure, and insufficient expertise, when teams without strong design leadership make aesthetic decisions by committee or by relying on the judgment of prospective users.
How to avoid it
Preventing it in the first place
Ensure the product excels on the other eight Tenets. Functional failure cannot be rescued by visual execution, and functional excellence is the necessary foundation for aesthetic quality. Beyond that, give design expertise genuine authority from the earliest stages of development. The principles of good visual design, among them alignment, hierarchy, contrast, proximity, and appropriate use of color and typography, provide a practical starting point. Robin Williams's The Non-Designer's Design Book remains one of the most accessible guides. But principles alone will not produce beauty. Deep knowledge of the target audience, their cultural context, and the social and aesthetic trends shaping their expectations is what closes the gap between competent and resonant. That knowledge lives with your designers. Use it.
Finding and fixing it in existing designs
You cannot reliably test for beauty before shipping, but you can test for what will prevent it from being realized. Evaluate the product against the other eight Tenets and address every failure you find. If, in the process of doing this, users comment on aesthetics, interpret their feedback with care. Pre-launch feedback often reflects resistance to the unfamiliar rather than a reliable signal about the lasting response a product's aesthetics will generate.
Beauty is a relationship between a design and the people experiencing it, shaped by culture, social context, and the aesthetic trends of a particular moment. A design right for one audience may be wrong for another. A design that feels current today may feel dated in three years. Understanding these dynamics requires ongoing knowledge of where social and aesthetic trends are going. This is one of the designer's most distinctive contributions to a product team, and one of the hardest to replicate without genuine expertise.
Listen to users, but trust your designers.
Related Traps
POOR AESTHETIC is unique among the Traps in this framework in that it does not co-occur with other Traps in a predictable pattern. Any Trap that produces friction, confusion, or frustration will undermine the aesthetic experience. The relationship runs in both directions: the Aesthetic-Usability Effect establishes that aesthetic failures also produce perceptions of usability failure. Addressing failures across all other Tenets is both a functional and an aesthetic imperative.
Related Concepts
The Aesthetic-Usability Effect. Tractinsky, Katz, and Ikar (2000) established that users perceive attractive interfaces as easier to use, replicating and extending earlier findings by Kurosu and Kashimura (1995). Subsequent research found the relationship runs in both directions: more usable interfaces are also perceived as more beautiful. The causal direction remains contested. What is not contested is that the relationship is robust across multiple studies and interface types, and that functional and aesthetic quality are entangled: failures on one tend to produce perceptions of failure on the other.
Can an AI reliably find this Trap?
No. POOR AESTHETIC is the Trap least amenable to automated detection. An AI can flag violations of specific design principles, insufficient contrast, misaligned elements, inconsistent typography, but it cannot assess whether a design is beautiful or appropriate for its audience and moment. That judgment requires cultural knowledge, aesthetic sensibility, and understanding of the social context in which the product will be experienced. It is the designer's domain, and it remains so.

Using Tenets & Traps


How to conduct a Trap Analysis
We recommend a hybrid Heuristic Evaluation + Cognitive Walkthrough approach involving these steps:
1.	Familiarize yourself with the UI Tenets & Traps taxonomy. Take special note of those Traps that are flagged as 'easy-to-miss'.
2.	Establish a clear understanding of who your target users are. How familiar with the product domain? How familiar are they with similar UIs?
3.	Identify the tasks that are most important to your target users.
4.	Working independently, attempt ALL the ways the target user might try to complete each task on the product or prototype you are evaluating.
5.	Consider what will happen if the user does something unexpected, attempts to back-up, or starts over.
6.	Identify any issues that may limit task success or user satisfaction.
7.	Note any associated Traps. Some issues have several - note the most critical ones you see.
8.	For issues that have multiple Traps, ask yourself whether one Trap may be the root cause of the others. Understanding which Trap is at the root of an issue is often critical to finding the best solution.
9.	If you’re not sure which Traps apply, ask yourself which Tenets are being degraded - this can help to clarify the problem.
10.	Share your results with other reviewers. Use Tenets & Traps to facilitate a good discussion.
Worksheets and Trap Analysis report templates can be found at uitraps.com.
How to focus research and clarify results
When using UI Tenets & Traps in conjunction with any sort of testing with users, they tend to be used as follows:
•	As a way of quickly calling-out issues as the users are having them.  It becomes a shorthand for communication for team members versed in the UI Tenets & Traps system.  E.g., someone says, “Oh, look at that Effectively Invisible Element” …everyone in the room knows what that person means.  They may not always agree, …but they understand what the observer is saying.
•	As a way of labeling issues in the test report after the fact.  Often screenshots or video of the offending issues would have their various Trap cards next to them.  The presence of the Traps ends up lending more credence to the issues – because the issues are examples of durable problems that have been seen before.
•	As a mechanism for looking across a range of tasks for a product (or set of products) to see the nature of the sort of errors that are produced.  We have found that different organizations have different propensities to certain kinds of Traps.  That there is often something cultural going on that allows these Traps to manifest themselves in the interface design.  It can be very helpful to point out to organizations their propensity towards certain kinds of Traps and why that might be.

Finally, there is the gameplay itself. Gameplay has at least two characteristics that are unique: 
The goal of the game is rarely grounded in the “real world”.  It is given to the player by the game itself.
Challenge is desirable.  Games are challenging.  Without challenge, most games wouldn’t be fun. But the challenge in each game is different, so games have different Traps that will or will not apply.  
The result of these two unique elements means that games have additional Traps around challenge.  Additionally, it means that games will purposefully add certain kinds of Traps to create challenge.  For example, soccer is a game that imposes a Physical Challenge Trap on a player on purpose: players can’t use their hands. The game wouldn’t be as fun if players could use their hands.  Conversely, imposing a Physical Challenge Trap in a game like chess could destroy the gameplay.  Instead, chess purposefully makes it difficult to know or memorize all the possible moves for every situation –it imposes (amongst other things) a Memory Challenge Trap to provide challenge.  The point of all this is to point out that when making a game, it is important to understand what areas have been made purposefully harder when doing a Trap analysis.
Q2: Do UI Tenets & Traps apply to all user populations?
We’ve found that UI Tenets & Traps apply broadly to different user cohorts, though we have found that different populations may affect the bar or threshold for what is considered a Trap.  For example, some researchers we’ve worked with studied software aimed at younger children.  They initially felt that there were some gaps in the UI Tenets & Traps system related to children.  So, they poured through their reports, reviews and publicly reported issues with their software and competitors software aimed at younger children.  They didn’t find any new Traps.  Instead they found that the threshold for triggering some Traps was different.  For example, in general children are shorter and their hands are smaller than adults.  This meant that children could not hold hardware designed for adults.  An existing Trap applied (Physical Challenge), but the threshold was different.  Similar stories can be told with other user populations that have different capabilities and limitations than other user groups.  Same Traps, different thresholds.

UI Tenets & Traps was informed by a rigorous audit of the user interface literature, as well as many existing heuristic taxonomies and guidelines. We defined our Tenets with the explicit intent of creating a global, but still manageable, set of categories within which we could organize the information we were gathering. 
The charts below illustrate how Tenets provide this framing. The color of a bar indicates which of our Tenets each of a set of noted authors addressed in concept in their writings. The height of a bar indicates the degree to which the author expanded upon and defined concepts related to a Tenet. (More specifically, the height of the bar reflects a count of the principles, axioms, guidelines, or heuristics that each of the authors cited in their work in relation to a given Tenet.)
What these charts show is that no author developed guidelines or principles for every Tenet. Rather they tended to go deep in a subset. (And we’re glad they did, because there is amazing work here.) The Tenets provide a human-centered framing that allows any guideline or research finding to be considered in relation to it. As we state in our introduction, we began the process of building UI Tenets & Traps out of recognition that there is a ton of information out there, and that it’s hard to get your head around it. Our Tenet framing is an effort to make this easier.

 Appendix B: Traps and Dark Patterns
What are Dark Patterns?
Dark (or ‘deceptive’) patterns are design tricks used in websites and apps that exploit human psychology to make people do things they wouldn’t otherwise do, like buy something or share personal information. An example of a dark pattern is ‘Sneaking’, which is the adding of unwanted items into people’s shopping carts during the checkout process. Recently, AI has presented new opportunities to manipulate users, for example, by using deep fakes to create false impressions. It has also given us a new class of emergent dark patterns, i.e., manipulative patterns that were not designed-in intentionally, but which exist as an artifact of how AIs are trained. An example is ‘sycophancy’, which causes harm by affirming users’ incorrect beliefs.  
How are Dark Patterns and Traps different?
Both Dark Patterns and Traps describe detectable problems in user interfaces that can negatively impact the user experience. Dark patterns address efforts to manipulate users. Traps address any type of problem that can negatively impact the user experience, irrespective of whether there is a motive behind it. As such, Traps address a broader class of problems. If you solve for Traps, you are likely to eliminate most dark patterns. This is not to say that dark patterns aren’t useful. On the contrary, they provide highly resolved lens for finding a particular type of problem, and as such work well in combination with Traps. 
Is there a direct relationship between any specific Dark Patterns and Traps? 
In some cases there is a one-to-one relationship between a dark pattern and a Trap. For example, the dark pattern ‘Disguised Ad’ is an example of particular instance of an Inviting Dead End Trap. But more commonly, a given dark pattern may have a variety of different Traps that may be associated with it. For example, the dark pattern ‘Hard to Cancel’ might be the result of Unnecessary Step(s) in the cancelation flow that create added friction for the user, or Effectively Invisible Elements that makes important information within the flow less noticeable.

 Appendix C: Comfortable Tenet
Targeting Accuracy  
Many interfaces are visual in nature. Users interact with visual interfaces by the means of controls. In ergonomic parlance, engaging a control is known as ‘target acquisition’. Good user interfaces allow targets to be acquired quickly and without error. The model for predicting ease of target acquisition is Fitts’ Law4, which has been around since it was first proposed in the 1950s. The formula is: 
T = average time taken to complete the movement.   
a = start/stop time of the device.  
b = the inherent speed of the device.  
D = distance from the starting point to the center of the target.  
W = width of the target measured along the axis of motion.  
In simple terms, Fitts’ Law states that the time required to move to a target area is a function of the distance to and the size of the target. All things being equal, bigger targets will be acquired more quickly, and closer targets will be acquired more quickly. This seems straightforward, but when applied, it can produce some very interesting and counterintuitive conclusions. For example, the most efficient target placements differ greatly between windows on a PC and a 360 degree 1st-person shooter environment on a PC. In a PC running Windows or iOS, the fastest targets to acquire are the corners, due to the fact that the user can overshoot the corner without penalty, increasing the effective target size of any control that is placed there. Conversely, if you run Halo on a PC, the fastest target to acquire is in the middle of the screen since the targeting reticule (or ‘cross-hairs’) remains in the middle at all times, requiring zero travel distance for the user.

Old/Extra

 
This an alternative opening to the book I explored – these two sections capture what we often open our presentations with: the idea that the mistakes keep getting made – supported by examples, it also addresses enshitification which is kind of an elephant in the room – the source of so much bad design; not sure it all works though
Shouldn't stuff just work by now?
Product teams have access to decades of human interface research telling them what makes products work for people. And yet:
A video call participant jumps into the conversation with an important point, only to hear a chorus of "You're on mute!" A homeowner struggles to get their smart home app to work as reliably as the light switch it replaced. More consequentially, a passenger in a car accident finds themselves trapped, unable to simply open the door.
With all the knowledge available, why do so many products continue to frustrate and even endanger the people they're meant to serve? What prevents companies from getting the design right?
 
Figure x – Why are headlines like these still so common?
Three things get in the way.
Placing business goals over user needs. A subscription service quietly makes cancellation harder than sign-up. A free credit monitoring app pre-selects a checkbox that leads users to unknowingly share personal data. This the is the realm of dark patterns and enshitification: design choices that serve business goals at the direct expense of users. 
Prioritizing features over outcomes. In many companies, features are a currency — the thing teams are rewarded for delivering. This can lead to bloated products and unnecessary complexity for users.
Adopting a ‘not-invented-here’ mindset. Some companies prioritize differentiated experiences over ones that simply work, treating innovation as an end in itself.
Failing to apply existing design knowledge. Even when teams prioritize the needs of their users above all else, their interfaces often miss the mark. Deep, well-established knowledge about human perception, cognition, and ergonomics fails to make it from page to product. The knowledge exists. It just does not get applied.
UI Tenets & Traps directly addresses the final cause above, and by making good design easier to achieve, it raises the cost of the first three.
